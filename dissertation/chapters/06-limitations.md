# Chapter 6: Limitations and Objections

The argument of Chapters 3 to 5 is that a synthetic Socratic examination can gather evidence fit to inform a grade under conditions the literature makes explicit, and that Gadfly meets many of those conditions and fails some for procedural reasons. This chapter sets out the limits of that argument: what it rests on, what it cannot show, and the objections a reader may fairly raise. Sections 6.1 to 6.7 state the limitations. Section 6.8 states the objections and the replies the dissertation can give, and Section 6.9 summarizes.

## 6.1 No empirical evaluation

The dissertation reports no study. It states a model, describes a system against it, and designs a test (Chapter 7). Every claim about what Gadfly's scores mean is therefore a claim about what they would mean if the conditions held, not a finding that they do.

The point has sharpened since Chapter 4 was written. The changes described in Section 5.2 added a set of instruments to the build: a calibration gate for the grading panel, a probe of the panel with padded and less fluent paraphrases, word error rates by group, gaze episode rates by group, and a count of read-out flags. Each is the right instrument for a question Chapter 2 raised, and none has yet been run on real examinations. An instrument that exists but has not been used is easy to mistake for evidence, especially in a system's own documentation and interface: a grading page that can show a panel's calibration status invites the reader to assume there is one. Until the project's double-marked pilot has been run (Gadfly repository, `docs/PILOT.md`), there is no agreement figure for the panel, no word error rate for any group, no rate of false read-out flags and no fade rate by group, and no expected value for any of them should be quoted.

## 6.2 Evidence from neighbouring tasks

No study found in the research pass tests language models grading spoken, transcribed Socratic answers, multi-turn follow-ups, or per-objective scoring in an oral examination (Section 2.12). The evidence on machine judgment comes from neighbouring tasks: pairwise preferences between chat responses (Zheng et al., 2023), question answering and chatbot ranking (Verga et al., 2024), short written answers (Henkel et al., 2024), reading comprehension (Henkel et al., 2023), essays (Mizumoto & Eguchi, 2023; Pack et al., 2024) and handwritten physics solutions (Kortemeyer, 2023). Three differences limit how far that evidence carries.

- **The evidence is a transcript.** The judges read the output of a speech recognizer, which differs by speaker group (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024). No study above had a recognizer between the student and the judge.
- **The evidence is a conversation.** An answer to a follow-up depends on the question, which depends on the previous answer. A judge that scores each answer alone, as Gadfly's does during the examination, and a grader that reads the answers to one objective together, as Gadfly's does afterwards, face a task none of these studies posed.
- **The evidence is spoken explanation.** Spoken answers contain false starts, fillers and repairs that written answers do not, and the instruction to ignore them has not been tested (Section 5.3.2).

The same limit applies to the human baseline. The figures for human agreement come from essay marking (Shermis, 2014; Meadows & Billington, 2005), and agreement between human examiners of spoken Socratic answers may be higher or lower. The acceptance thresholds Gadfly now applies were set for automated essay scoring in large-scale testing (Williamson et al., 2012, as cited in Casabianca et al., 2023), and their suitability for this task is itself an assumption.

## 6.3 Sources read at the abstract level

Forty-three of the 83 works in the reference list were read only at the abstract or publisher-page level, and one only at the record level (Appendix A). The rule that a claim attributed to such a work is confined to what its abstract establishes keeps the dissertation from misreporting them, but it does not make abstracts into full evidence. Four works carry more weight than an abstract can bear comfortably (`references/UNRETRIEVED.md`, Section 5):

- **Wass et al. (2003)** supplies the variance components behind the argument that reliability comes from sampling (Sections 2.4.2 and 3.7) and the illustration in Section 3.7. If the full paper qualified the components, for instance by the design of the orals or the estimation method, the shape of the argument would survive, since van der Vleuten et al. (2010), read in the relevant sections, makes the same point, but the illustration would need to change.
- **Koenecke et al. (2020)** supplies the size of the racial gap in speech recognition. The direction of the finding is not in doubt, but its size for the recognizers Gadfly uses is unknown in any case, and the full paper might show conditions under which the gap narrows or widens.
- **Huxham et al. (2012)** supplies the finding that students scored higher in oral than in written formats with no group visibly disadvantaged. The dissertation already treats the second half as too weak to rely on (Appendix A, correction 9).
- **Fox et al. (2011)** supplies the finding that explaining to a listener is reactive and that all verbal reporting slows people down, on which Sections 2.3.2, 4.3.5 and 5.7.3 rely. The finding is stated in the abstract in the terms the dissertation uses, but the moderators and the conditions of the studies were not read.

Other works read at the abstract level bear on single points rather than on the argument: the meta-analyses of anxiety (Hembree, 1988; von der Embse et al., 2018), the studies of voice-stress analysis (Harnsberger et al., 2009; Giddens et al., 2013), and the studies of language models scoring student work (Section 2.9.2). In each case reading the full text could qualify a figure but is unlikely to reverse a direction. The one case in which it could reverse a direction is Caffrey et al. (2008), whose finding that dynamic assessment predicted best when help was not contingent on the student's response supports the fixed hint ladder; if the full review qualified that finding, the argument for the ladder would rest on comparability alone, which is enough for it (Section 2.8.2).

Six primary works are cited only through secondary sources, and the measurement-theory literature on which Chapter 3's algebra draws was not located at all (Appendix A, Sections A.3 and A.7). The second is the larger limitation. Chapter 3 uses the forms of expected information gain, a threshold response model, optional stopping and a generalizability coefficient without the literature in which they are standard. The model's requirements are stated so that they follow from its own definitions, but its relation to item response theory, computerized adaptive testing and generalizability theory, which would let it be fitted to data, is unestablished.

## 6.4 Populations the evidence does not cover

The evidence reviewed covers some students well and others not at all.

- **Neurodivergent students.** No source on neurodivergent students in oral or voice examinations was verified (Section 2.12). Several of Gadfly's mechanisms act on behaviour that differs systematically for some neurodivergent students: eye contact, which the gaze fade enforces; the timing of speech, which the delivery cues and the read-out pattern read; and the confrontational move of the elenchus. The eye-contact accommodation and the extra-time suppression of the read-out pattern give instructors a remedy for students known to need it, but a remedy that depends on a declared accommodation does not reach students who have not declared one, and the dissertation can say nothing about the size of the effect.
- **Students who cannot use the voice channel.** Under decision (a) there is no route for a student for whom speaking, or being recorded, is the barrier (Section 5.7.1). The evidence on speech recognition covers dialect and stuttering; it says nothing about students with other speech or hearing impairments, or about students taking an examination in a language they speak with limited fluency.
- **Adults in dynamic assessment.** The dynamic-assessment evidence comes from young children, students with disabilities, and decoding and reasoning tasks (Section 2.8.3). Its application to adults answering conceptual questions aloud, which is what Gadfly's Learning Mode does, is untested.
- **Languages other than English.** Every source on speech recognition, judging and anxiety that the dissertation cites concerns English, except the work on second-language anxiety, which concerns learners of a foreign language (Horwitz et al., 1986). The judges' instructions, the hedging phrases the delivery cue looks for, and the recitation check's word-overlap thresholds are all stated for English.
- **Students without stable equipment or a private room.** A voice-and-camera examination assumes a working microphone, a camera, a connection and a room in which a second voice, a second face or a glance at a noise will not be recorded. The clock now stops during pauses the student did not cause (GAD-T263), but nothing in the evidence reviewed measures who is disadvantaged by the requirement itself, and the camera-based evidence of disparities (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022) concerns skin tone, not circumstance.

## 6.5 The examiner changes the examined

Explaining to a listener is the reactive kind of verbal report: it changes performance, usually for the better, and slows it down (Fox et al., 2011; Ericsson & Simon, 1998). A Socratic examination is that kind of report. Three consequences limit the reading of a session as a measurement.

First, the student's state may change during the session. An objective examined late may be examined after the student has, in explaining earlier objectives, come to understand more. The evidence rules treat answers as evidence about a fixed state (Section 3.9), so a level reached late is reported as if the student brought it in. That is a misdescription, though not obviously an unfair one: understanding reached by explaining under questioning is still understanding, and a grade that credits it measures something real. It does mean that the order of objectives, which the instructor sets, can change what is reported.

Second, the examiner's manner may change the student's capacity to answer. The model treats the probability that a student who understands will answer adequately as a property of the student (Section 3.3); the *Apology* records that the elenchus made those examined hostile (Plato, 1966, 21d), and the anxiety evidence suggests that a confrontational examiner may lower that probability for anxious students (Section 5.3.4). Gadfly's examiner is told never to correct, confirm or remark on delivery, and its contradiction question is neutral in form, but how it is experienced was not studied.

Third, the examination is also a learning event (Section 2.6). Released results now give per-objective feedback (GAD-T273), and the retrieval-practice evidence supports the claim that a further attempt with feedback will improve performance on what was examined (Rowland, 2014; Pan & Rickard, 2018). The same fact limits what a second attempt's score means: an improvement may reflect learning from the first attempt's questions and feedback rather than from study, and it will be concentrated on what the first attempt probed. Reporting the score change between attempts, as the build now does, is useful to the student. Treating it as a measure of learning would need its own validation (Section 2.7.2), and the dissertation does not claim one.

## 6.6 Dependence on model providers

Gadfly's examiner and grader are panels of models from four commercial providers. Three limits follow.

**Drift.** Model behaviour changes over time. In the one study of the reviewed set that examined it, interrater reliability "decreased slightly over time" and "all models are subject to fluctuations in their performance" (Pack et al., 2024, abstract). A calibration set that accepted a panel for an examination is evidence about the models as they were when it was run. In the build, a calibration is recorded against a panel name supplied when it is evaluated, and nothing found in the build reopens it when a provider changes the model behind that name. A calibration should be treated as expiring, and re-run when any member changes.

**Reproducibility.** The planner and the evidence rules are deterministic and can be replayed (Sections 4.3.1 and 4.5). The panels are not: the grader samples at a low temperature, and the same answer judged twice by the same provider may receive different levels. The build records the prompt-set version, the members' votes and their dissent, which is what an audit needs, but it cannot reproduce a judgment exactly. Disagreements between members, which the panel design exists to average out, also make any single replay differ from the original.

**Correlated change.** The four providers are separate companies, but their models are trained on overlapping data by similar methods, and the formal case for a panel (Section 3.8) assumes that the biases of different families are partly independent. If the providers' models converge, the independence on which the panel's advantage rests shrinks, and nothing in the build would show it, short of the bias probes and calibration being re-run.

## 6.7 Authorship and the case

This dissertation was written in collaboration with a language model, and its research notes were prepared by language-model agents. A dissertation about synthetic examiners written with a synthetic collaborator invites the objection that its author has an interest in the conclusion and its collaborator a tendency to agree with whatever is asked. The verification ledger is the answer the dissertation gives: every source is recorded at the level at which it was read, every quotation was seen, and every correction is kept (Section 1.7). The ledger does not remove the limitation. It makes it checkable.

A second limitation concerns the case. Gadfly is developed by the organization for which the dissertation is written, and the dissertation's descriptions of it were checked against code written in part by the same kind of agents that helped write the dissertation. Two things temper this. The descriptions were checked against the code, not against the project's documentation, and the check has repeatedly found the documentation wrong: corrections 30 to 37 and 40 are cases in which the project's own descriptions of its system, or Chapter 4's, said more or less than the code does. And the system changed while it was described. Fourteen of the changes Chapter 4 proposed were made on the day Chapter 4 was written (Section 5.2), in part in response to the same evidence review. A dissertation that describes a system its own evidence is changing cannot be a neutral observer of it. What it can be is explicit about which commit it describes, which this one is.

## 6.8 Objections and replies

The objections below are those a reader is most likely to raise. Each is stated in its strongest form, and the reply says how far the dissertation can answer it.

**"An oral examination is inherently unfair to anxious, second-language and disfluent students."** The evidence supports the premise: anxiety is associated with lower scores (Hembree, 1988; von der Embse et al., 2018), and speech recognition errs more for some groups (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024). The reply is that the evidence supports a weaker conclusion: an oral examination is unfair to these students to the extent that its design adds pressure and treats recognizer error as content, and both can be reduced. Gadfly reduces some (verified transcripts, adaptive silence, a clock that stops during pauses, unsure words marked for graders) and, by decision, adds others (Section 5.7). Whether what remains is acceptable is not something the evidence reviewed can decide. The dissertation does not claim that a Socratic oral examination is fairer than other formats, and the evidence would not support that claim.

**"A machine examiner can be gamed."** Graders can be manipulated by instructions hidden in student work (Milani et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2024), and a spoken answer can carry them as easily as a written one. The reply is that Gadfly treats the transcript as untrusted data, gives no credit for text addressed to the grader and sends it to a human, and never lets the panel's grade stand alone. These are the controls the evidence supports. They have not been tested against a determined student, and Chapter 7 should include an adversarial condition.

**"A student can receive answers through an earpiece and speak them as their own."** This is the strongest form of the integrity objection, and the dissertation cannot answer it with evidence. The camera will not see an earpiece, the audio signal for a second voice will not hear one, and the recitation check will not fire on an answer spoken naturally. The only control is the follow-up question, and the claim that follow-up defeats relayed answers is a hypothesis with no direct support in the sources reviewed (Section 5.5). The relay condition of Chapter 7 is the test.

**"The human examiner will rubber-stamp the machine."** Human evaluators shown a model's judgment changed their own choice in about a third of cases (Zheng et al., 2023), and the Act names automation bias as something overseers must guard against (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 14(4)(b)). The reply is that blind grading by default is a direct guard: the human enters a score before seeing the panel's, so the disagreement between them is measurable and large ones block finalization. The guard is configurable, and it does not extend to reconciliation, which may be done by the same person who entered the human score (Section 5.9). The objection is answered for grading and open for reconciliation.

**"The standard of truth is a rubric a machine wrote."** The rubric for each concept is written by the panel from the instructor's material, and the instructor may edit it but need not approve it before an examination runs on it (Appendix A, correction 35). A grade can therefore rest on a standard no person has read. The reply is that every rubric point is tied to passages of the instructor's material, is versioned, and is recorded with every judgment made under it, so an error in it is recoverable. But recoverable is not the same as reviewed, and the simplest change that would answer the objection, requiring approval before a summative examination, has not been made.

**"Why not examine students in person, on paper?"** Nothing in this dissertation shows that a synthetic Socratic examination is a better measure than a supervised written examination, and the evidence review states that the evidence does not let the project claim it. The dissertation's question is narrower: whether a Socratic oral examination, which has properties a written one lacks, can be conducted at a scale at which oral examination has never been practical, and what the evidence it gathers would then be fit for. A supervised written examination is an alternative, not an answer to that question.

**"The formal model is decoration."** Chapter 3's algebra is used without the measurement literature in which it is standard (Section 6.3), and the model has not been fitted to data. The reply is that the model's work in this dissertation is to derive requirements, not to estimate parameters, and that each requirement follows from its definitions and was checked against code. Whether the model predicts anything is a question for Chapter 7.

## 6.9 Summary

The argument of this dissertation rests on evidence from neighbouring tasks, on a set of sources of which about half were read at the abstract level, and on a description of a single system that changed while it was described. It reports no study. It cannot say how accurate Gadfly's panel is, how much its recognizer disadvantages any group, how often its integrity signals misfire, or whether its follow-up questions defeat relayed answers, and it cannot speak for neurodivergent students, students who cannot speak the examination, or examinations in other languages. What it can say is what each of those questions would take to answer. The next chapter designs the study.
