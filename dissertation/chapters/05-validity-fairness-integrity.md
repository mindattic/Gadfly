# Chapter 5: Validity, Fairness and Integrity

## 5.1 What the score means

Chapter 4 asked whether Gadfly does what the model of Chapter 3 requires. This chapter asks a different question: if it does, what may the resulting score be taken to mean, for whom might it mean something different, and what may the system's integrity signals be taken to show? The question is one of validity in the ordinary sense of the word: whether the inferences drawn from a score are warranted by the evidence behind it. The chapter states that argument in its own terms, as a chain of inferences, and cites for each link only the sources recorded in Appendix A, at their recorded levels. It does not draw on the measurement literature in which validity arguments are formally developed, because none of that literature was among the sources verified (Appendix A, Section A.7).

A score from a synthetic Socratic examination supports a decision only through five inferences, each of which can fail on its own.

1. **From utterance to transcript.** What the judges read is a transcript of what the student said. The inference is that the transcript says what the student meant. It fails when the speech recognizer mis-hears, and the evidence is that it mis-hears some groups of speakers more than others (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024).
2. **From transcript to judged answer.** The inference is that the panel's judgment of an answer reflects the answer's content against the rubric, not its length, its fluency, its position, or instructions hidden in it. It fails through the known biases of language-model judges (Section 2.9) and through the quality of the rubric itself, which in Gadfly a panel writes and an instructor may, but need not, review (Section 4.2).
3. **From judged answers to an objective's level.** The inference is that the level reported for an objective reflects the student's understanding of that objective, and not the path the examiner happened to take. It fails when the path can change the level (Section 3.6), and when the examination itself changes what the student understands (Section 2.3.2).
4. **From objective levels to a grade.** The inference is that the objectives examined are a large enough sample of the subject for the grade to generalize. It fails on small samples, because content contributes more score variance than examiners do (Wass et al., 2003; van der Vleuten et al., 2010).
5. **From the grade to a decision.** The inference is that the people and procedures that turn the panel's and the human's judgments into a published grade do so accountably: blind where blindness matters, with disagreements resolved on the evidence, and with a person, not the system, deciding. It fails through automation bias (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 14(4)(b); Zheng et al., 2023) and through procedures that let one person both judge and ratify the same judgment (Section 4.9).

The rest of the chapter takes these links in turn. Section 5.2 records what has changed in Gadfly since Chapter 4 was written, because much of what this chapter can say depends on it. Sections 5.3 and 5.4 treat the first two links as matters of fairness: construct-irrelevant variance, and the fairness of machine judgment. Section 5.5 sets out the argument that the examination is its own main integrity control, and what the evidence does and does not support in it. Section 5.6 sets out the limits of surveillance. Section 5.7 sets out the four decisions the project's owner has made against parts of the evidence, with the evidence on both sides. Section 5.8 sets out the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act duties that bear on the system, and Section 5.9 the accountability of the whole. Section 5.10 summarizes.

## 5.2 The method since Chapter 4

Chapter 4 described Gadfly as committed at `e691015`. It named sixteen changes proposed by the project's evidence-to-design review (Gadfly repository, `docs/EVIDENCE_MAP.md`), GAD-T258 to GAD-T273, none of which had then been approved, and a batch of uncommitted bug fixes. Within the same day, the owner approved fourteen of the sixteen, and they and the bug-fix batch were committed to the main line. This chapter describes the system as committed at `5643444` (21 November 2026). Chapter 4 is left as a description of the earlier commit, and where its assessments are now outdated the update is recorded here rather than by rewriting it.

### 5.2.1 What landed

- **The bug-fix batch** (`d78d879`, GAD-T275). Two fixes bear on the evidence rules. A contradiction put to the student whose answer has not been judged now stays pending, so it blocks the objective from settling instead of counting as resolved (`Gadfly/Engine/ObjectiveAssessor.cs`), and the planner now counts a contradiction as confronted only when one question has put both of its claims to the student (`QuestionPlanner.FollowUp`). A good answer to a Synthesize question, which is asked only once an objective is settled, can now raise the settled level when corroborated but can no longer reopen the objective. Two fixes bear on the delivery cues of Section 4.3.5: hesitations had been counted twice, because the disfluency measure already includes filled pauses, and the wait before the first word had been computed in part from the difference between the browser's clock and the server's, so that clock skew could read as a long pause (`Gadfly/Engine/AnswerDelivery.cs`). Both errors made the delivery cues fire more often than designed. Section 5.3.3 returns to what that shows.
- **Contradiction and presupposition** (GAD-T266). A confrontation is now settled by the judge's consistency criterion, which on a Contradiction move scores whether the student sees, settles and reasons out the conflict, "either revision being legitimate", rather than by the overall score, whose accuracy component depends on which claim the student kept (`ObjectiveAssessor.ResolutionScore`). Correctness is left to the rubric and the source. The question prompt, the leak guard and the response analysis now also test whether a question's form presupposes an answer point (exam prompt set version 6).
- **Transfer only from Apply** (GAD-T268). Only a positive answer to Apply, a situation new to the student, now counts as evidence of transfer, and the top level (Transfer and Critical Reasoning) requires one. A ScenarioVariation what-if is recorded as extrapolation, at the Application dimension, and Synthesize no longer counts as transfer. Each Apply question carries a deterministic label naming the respect in which its situation differs from the source, from the context dimensions of Barnett and Ceci (2002), nearest first, with a note that the examination measures sequestered transfer (Bransford & Schwartz, 1999) (`Gadfly/Engine/TransferLabels.cs`).
- **Probe counts and formative labelling** (GAD-T267). Each objective's report shows how many follow-up questions it received. A policy setting, `MinSummativeObjectives` (4 by default), sets the fewest objectives an examination must examine for its scores to be labelled summative; Learning Mode sessions and shorter examinations are labelled formative in both reports (`Gadfly/Engine/Objectives.cs`).
- **Grading-panel bias controls** (GAD-T269). The grading prompt now says that "length, repetition and restatement earn nothing," that grammar, accent, fluency and non-native phrasing are never penalized and polish never rewarded, and that each criterion's evidence and quotation come before its level. The order of the rubric criteria, and of each criterion's level descriptors, is shuffled on every call. A probe, `PanelBiasCheck`, submits padded and less fluent paraphrases of the same answers to a panel so that their scores can be compared (`Gadfly.Sources/Grading/AiObjectiveGrader.cs`, `PanelBiasCheck.cs`).
- **Panel calibration and reconciliation triggers** (GAD-T270). A grading panel is accepted for an examination only after a blind, double-marked calibration set of at least 30 answers shows a quadratic-weighted kappa of at least .70 between panel and human, no more than .10 below the agreement between the two human markers, and an absolute standardized mean difference under .15, with the panel compared against each marker and the weaker comparison counting (`Gadfly/Assessment/PanelCalibration.cs`). Each evaluation is an audit event; the latest is frozen into each session's grading policy and shown on the grading page. Until a panel is accepted, its grades for that examination are labelled provisional. Single objectives now also go to reconciliation when the panel members are split by more than one level, or when the score sits on a grade boundary (`Gadfly/Assessment/ReconciliationTriggers.cs`).
- **Speech recognition fairness** (GAD-T265). Words the recognizer was unsure of, in transcripts the student did not check, are named to the grading panel, which is told never to treat them as wrong content, and marked for the human grader. A student's transcript correction is shown to the grader with the number of words it changed. An analytics view reports word error rate against student-checked transcripts, the share of answers corrected and the share of unsure words, by a disfluency measure computed from the transcript or by a grouping the institution pastes in and that is never stored, withholding small groups and warning of disparities (`Gadfly.Server/Analytics/SpeechRecognitionAudit.cs`).
- **Time** (GAD-T263, GAD-T264). The examination clock now stops while the examination is paused because the microphone, camera or examiner connection was lost, with the paused time added back up to a cap (10 minutes, or half the budget). The silence that ends an answer now adapts to the student's own pauses within the answer (one and a half times their 80th-percentile pause, no shorter than the configured minimum of 4 seconds and no longer than a new maximum of 12), and both bounds lengthen under the extra-time accommodation.
- **Gaze** (GAD-T261). A look away that begins in the first 10 seconds of an answer now starts the fade only after 6 seconds, not 2, and the spoken request moves later by as much. An instructor can declare an eye-contact accommodation on an assignment, audited, under which the screen never fades and the examiner never asks for eye contact or remarks on reading-like eye movement; face presence and a second face are still checked, and episodes are recorded as accommodated, at the lowest severity. An analytics view reports look-aways, reading-like episodes, fades, time faded and spoken requests per 10 answering minutes, by accommodation or by a pasted, unstored grouping, withholding groups under 5 students and flagging rates that differ by more than a quarter (`Gadfly.Server/Analytics/GazeFlagRates.cs`).
- **The read-out pattern** (GAD-T272). The timing-based read-out pattern of the recitation check no longer fires for a student with an extra-time accommodation and can be switched off per examination. A command-line measure counts how often it fires over a set of transcripts, including where it was suppressed, so that its rate of false flags on honest students can be measured. The source-overlap test is unchanged.
- **Voice in the European Union** (GAD-T259). An organization marked as being in the EU or EEA, and any student marked as living there, now get no voice measurements unless an administrator turns them on for everyone, an audited act that the project's privacy documentation says should follow legal advice. When they are off, the browser's voice numbers are discarded before the answer is stored, nothing is compared, and neither the voice comparison nor its pause-and-speech-rate stand-in can raise the probing pressure. The other timing cues, the wait before the first word and hesitations per hundred words, which come from the speech recognizer, still can (`AnswerDelivery.Read`); the project's documentation says that "nothing about the voice" then steers probing, which is broader than the code (Appendix A, correction 40). Everywhere, the reviewer's line now shows raw acoustic differences and names no state: it no longer says "strain," "stress" or "hesitation." The privacy documentation's statement that "Nothing infers emotion," which Appendix A recorded as exposed (correction 28), was withdrawn. Outside the EU, the strain measure still drives probing, as the owner decided.
- **Fixed hint ladder** (GAD-T271). In Learning Mode, each hint and guided explanation is now the next rung of the objective's ladder, written by the instructor (or a fixed default ladder), and no model writes or rewords it; the planner decides only when to give it. Reports show the hints given beside the unaided level, and no gain or learning-potential score is computed.
- **Clear scores** (GAD-T262). Clearing an attempt's scores, which the owner decided should hold the attempt out of grading (decision (d), Section 5.7.4), now first copies the grades, reports, disagreements, reconciliations, approvals and appeals it removes into one audit record per attempt, and the student is told that the scores were cleared for review and that the attempt still counts.
- **Per-objective feedback** (GAD-T273). Released results now say, for each objective, which rubric criteria were scored below their maximum, with the panel's reason and the descriptor of the next level, which misconceptions to correct, and how the objective's score changed since the student's earlier attempt.

Two of the sixteen proposals were not approved and remain held: GAD-T258, which would drive probing from the content of answers instead of from voice strain, and GAD-T260, which would let an instructor grant a typed-answer accommodation. Both conflict with an owner's decision (Section 5.7).

Two further updates concern parts of Chapter 4 that the table does not cover. The practice mode now meets the requirement of Section 4.10 in full: help is a fixed, instructor-written ladder, given only when the planner judges an answer inadequate, and no gain score is computed, which is what Caffrey et al. (2008) and De Beer (2006) recommend for any use beyond practice. And Chapter 4's account of the integrity signals was incomplete. Section 4.8 described three kinds of signal; the build also records browser events (the examination tab hidden, the window losing focus, leaving full screen, an extra screen, pasting, copying the question), audio events from the recognizer (a second voice, whispering, a reading-aloud rhythm), a camera episode in which the face in view stops resembling the face at the start, and a language-model estimate of how far an answer "reads like written text" compared with the student's other answers (`Gadfly.Sources/TextStyleAssessor.cs`). All are shown to reviewers as context and none enters the score, but the last two are of kinds the literature of Chapter 2 treats with particular caution (Appendix A, correction 37; Section 5.6).

The pattern of Section 4.11 holds, with the shortfalls narrowed. The parts of the method that decide what the evidence is now meet the model's requirements closely. What remains are, first, the reporting of how much of each objective a score covers; second, the independence of the human side of grading; and third, the signals the owner has decided should act on the examination. Every remaining shortfall is again procedural, and every change of the day was procedural too: none of them required a more capable model.

## 5.3 Construct-irrelevant variance

A score is construct-irrelevant to the extent that it varies with something other than the understanding it is meant to measure. Section 2.5 identified the main sources in a spoken examination: anxiety, second-language speaking, accent and dialect, disfluency, and, above all, the speech recognizer. They can reach a Socratic examination's score by three routes: through the transcript, which is the evidence the judges read; through the judges, if they reward or penalize how something was said; and through the path, if a signal that varies with them changes which questions a student receives.

### 5.3.1 Through the transcript

The recognizer is the largest documented source. Its error is not noise around the evidence but a change in it: a word the recognizer mis-hears is, to the judges, a word the student said. In a test of five commercial recognizers the average word error rate was 0.35 for black speakers and 0.19 for white speakers, and the gap was as large on identical phrases (Koenecke et al., 2020, abstract); six leading recognizers were biased against the speech of people who stutter (Mujtaba et al., 2024, abstract). Both studies were read at the abstract level, and neither tested the recognizer Gadfly uses, so they establish the kind of risk, not its size for this system.

Gadfly's design answers this route in three ways, two of them new. Uncertain recognition becomes a question to the student before grading, and an unresolved transcript blocks the panel's grading (Section 4.7). Words the recognizer was unsure of, if the student did not check them, are now named to the panel with an instruction not to treat them as wrong content, and marked for the human grader. And word error rate can now be compared by group. This is the measurement Section 2.5.2 called for, and its form deserves scrutiny, because the comparison can mislead in two ways. First, the error rate is measured against transcripts the students themselves checked and corrected. Students who did not check their transcripts contribute no reference, and if the students the recognizer serves worst are also the least likely to find and correct its errors, perhaps because correcting is slow or tiring, the measured gap will understate the true one. Second, the built-in grouping is by a disfluency measure computed from the transcript, which is the recognizer's own output, so a recognizer that drops disfluencies will place some disfluent speakers in the fluent group. The pasted grouping avoids the second problem but depends on the institution holding, and being permitted to use, the relevant information. Neither problem makes the measure useless; both mean that a small measured gap is weaker evidence of fairness than a large one is of unfairness.

### 5.3.2 Through the judges

The judges are told to ignore fluency and form, and, since GAD-T269, that length and restatement earn nothing and that polish is never rewarded. Section 4.6.1 observed that these are instructions, not measurements. The build now includes the instrument that would turn them into measurements: a probe that submits padded and less fluent paraphrases of the same answers to the panel and compares the scores. This is the test the outline of this chapter proposed, of "paraphrased answers of equal content and unequal fluency." It has not been run on a calibration set, and until it has, the claim that the panel scores content rather than delivery rests on the prompt. The evidence that judges can be swayed by length (Zheng et al., 2023) and that detectors of machine text misclassify the writing of non-native speakers (Liang et al., 2023) gives reason to expect some effect; Liang et al. concerns detectors, not graders (Appendix A, correction 22), so the size of any effect on Gadfly's graders is an open question, not a known one.

The human examiner is not exempt. Joughin (2010) warns that "examiners can mistake a student's articulateness for knowledge" (p. 7), and nothing in the build tests the human for it. The calibration set of GAD-T270 compares panel and human with each other, so a bias both share would not appear in it. A paraphrase probe run on human examiners as well as on the panel would detect a shared bias; Chapter 7 includes one.

### 5.3.3 Through the path

The third route is the one specific to adaptive examination. Section 3.6 showed that a signal which steers the examiner can change a score only if the scoring rule lets the path matter, and Section 4.5 showed that Gadfly's evidence rules let it matter little: a level is the corroborated level, a harder question failed above it tests the ceiling rather than lowering the level, and the number of questions per objective is capped. The residual effect is that more questions above a student's level are more chances for a mixed or negative answer to block corroboration of a level shown once, and more chances for a chance positive.

Two things have changed. The number of follow-ups per objective is now reported, so a reviewer can see when one objective ran long. And the delivery cues themselves were found, in the bug-fix batch, to have been miscomputed: hesitations double-counted and waits partly made of clock skew. The fix matters less than what it shows. A delivery signal is a measurement with its own error, and its error is invisible unless its behaviour is measured. The gaze rates of GAD-T261 and the read-out count of GAD-T272 measure two such signals; nothing yet reports, by group, how often the probing pressure was raised, or on what cue. Until it does, the claim that delivery cues do not reach the score through the path rests on the scoring rule alone, which is strong but not complete.

### 5.3.4 Time and anxiety

Anxiety is associated with poorer performance across a large literature (Hembree, 1988; von der Embse et al., 2018), and scores rise when it is treated (Hembree, 1988). A spoken examination adds speaking anxiety, and an examination in a second language adds language anxiety (Horwitz et al., 1986). Design choices that add time pressure fall hardest on these students (Section 2.5.1). Two such choices that Chapter 4 recorded have been changed: the clock stops during a pause the student did not cause, and the silence that ends an answer now adapts to the student's own pauses within a floor and a ceiling. Both changes reduce construct-irrelevant variance by the mechanism the literature names. Neither has been measured: whether an adaptive end-of-answer silence cuts off fewer answers from anxious or second-language speakers is an empirical question, and the parameters chosen (the 80th percentile, a factor of one and a half, a ceiling of 12 seconds) are design choices, not values from a source.

Three pressures remain by decision. The camera and microphone are required throughout (decision (a)); looking away fades the screen and prompts a spoken request (decision (c)); and, outside the EU, a voice measure that varies with stress steers harder questions (decision (b)). Each is discussed in Section 5.7. Here it is enough to note that each adds pressure where the evidence says anxiety does harm, and that the examination's confrontational move, putting two of a student's own claims to them, is itself the kind of exposure the *Apology* records as making those examined hostile (Plato, 1966, 21d). The prompt asks the examiner to quote the two claims and ask "how both can be true," which is neutral in form; whether students experience it as neutral is not known.

## 5.4 Fairness of machine judgment

### 5.4.1 What the agreement thresholds can and cannot show

Since GAD-T270, Gadfly will not accept its grading panel for an examination until a blind, double-marked calibration set meets the aggregate thresholds used for automated scoring: a quadratic-weighted kappa of .70 or more, a degradation of less than .10 from human–human agreement, and a standardized mean difference of less than .15 (Williamson et al., 2012, as cited in Casabianca et al., 2023). This meets the requirement of Section 3.8 that the thresholds be applied to sets of answers, not to single ones (Appendix A, correction 21), and the build applies them conservatively, counting the panel's weaker comparison with the two human markers.

Three cautions apply. First, the thresholds are rules of practice, not derived optima, and they come from essay scoring, where human agreement ranged from 0.61 to 0.85 (Shermis, 2014). Second, agreement is not validity: a model "may do a good job of matching human scoring behavior, but for reasons unrelated (or unsatisfactorily related) to the construct of interest" (Shermis, 2014). A panel that matched human graders because both rewarded articulate delivery would pass. Third, an aggregate threshold can hide a group difference: a panel can meet all three thresholds overall and differ from human graders for one group of students. The calibration evaluation does not break its statistics down by group. The standardized mean difference by group is the obvious addition, and it requires the same institutional data on groups that the speech-recognition and gaze measures need.

A fourth point concerns what follows from failing. A panel that has not been accepted for an examination keeps its weight in the combined score; its grades are labelled provisional, and the human still grades blind, disagreements are still reconciled and a person still approves. That is defensible, since the panel is never the sole grader, but it means the calibration gate informs the people who grade rather than constraining the arithmetic. Section 3.8 used the thresholds "to decide whether the panel may be used at all." The build uses them to decide whether the panel may be trusted, and leaves its use to the people.

The reconciliation triggers that came with the calibration gate are of a different kind. Sending an objective to reconciliation when the panel's members are split by more than one level follows directly from Section 3.8: a split panel is a sign that the evidence is hard to judge, and the mean of a split panel is not a sound judgment. Sending it when the score sits near a grade boundary is a reasonable precaution, since an error there changes a decision. The project's code and research notes support the boundary trigger by citing Wang et al. (2024) for judgments being least stable near a cut, but that finding concerns pairwise comparisons, whose verdicts were least stable when the two answers were close in quality; it is an analogy for a rubric boundary, not evidence about one (Appendix A, correction 41). Both triggers are design proposals whose limits, as the code itself says, should be tuned on calibration data.

### 5.4.2 Biases of the panel

The biases Section 2.9 documented are position, length, self-preference and susceptibility to injected instructions. The build now addresses each in part. Position bias was documented for pairwise comparison (Wang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023), which Gadfly does not use; the shuffling of criteria and levels removes a fixed order where it was easiest to remove, but the student's answers are still presented in examination order, because the citations depend on their numbering. A shuffled presentation that kept stable labels would remove the remaining fixed order at no cost to citation. Length is addressed by instruction and by the paraphrase probe. Injection is addressed by fencing the student's words as data and by flagging any answer that addresses the grader (Section 4.6.1; Milani et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2024). Self-preference is not addressed. The same four providers write the questions and grade the answers, and self-preference correlated with a model's ability to recognize its own outputs (Panickssery et al., 2024, preprint abstract). Whether a model grades answers to its own questions more kindly is untested; it is a cheap thing to test, by comparing grades from members whose family did and did not write the question.

### 5.4.3 Monitoring score gaps

The outline of this chapter called for monitoring of score gaps by speaker group. The build now monitors two inputs to the score by group, the recognizer's error and the gaze episodes, but not the scores themselves. A gap in scores between groups is not, by itself, evidence of unfairness, since groups can differ in what they have learned. A gap that appears only in the panel's scores, or only in the human's, or that persists between students of equal standing on some independent measure, is. The comparison of the two graders' scores by group can be made from data the build already keeps, and it is the one fairness check that bears directly on the grade. Chapter 7 includes it.

## 5.5 Integrity by design

The argument that a Socratic examination is its own main integrity control can be stated in the terms of Section 3.3. A question is informative about understanding to the extent that an adequate answer is hard to produce without it. A follow-up written from the student's own previous answer cannot be prepared in advance, so it raises the cost of answering without understanding: a student relaying answers from another source must relay the follow-ups too, and in real time. On this argument, the examination does not need to catch a student relaying answers; it needs only to ask questions whose answers a relay is less likely to supply.

What the evidence supports in this argument is mostly negative. The alternatives are weak. Proctored online examinations produce lower scores than unproctored ones (Alessio et al., 2017; Dendir & Maxwell, 2020), but both findings are inferences from score changes, and neither shows that any signal identifies a person who cheated (Appendix A, correction 27). Detectors of machine-written text are neither accurate nor reliable (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023) and misclassify non-native writers (Liang et al., 2023). Gaze is too coarse to resolve reading (Section 2.10.2), and voice-stress analysis performs at about chance (Section 2.10.3). And the only cues to deception with some support concern the content of what is said, in that liars "tell less compelling tales" (DePaulo et al., 2003, abstract). If anything distinguishes an answer the student cannot stand behind, it is more likely to be found in how they answer a follow-up than in how they look or sound.

What the evidence does not yet support is the positive claim. No source in the verified ledger shows that conversational follow-up defeats relayed answers, or that a person relaying answers will contradict themselves under questioning (Appendix A, correction 6). A case study of online oral examination found it workable against impersonation (Akimov & Malin, 2020), and students surveyed after interactive orals saw them as deterring misconduct (Sotiriadou et al., 2020), but both were read at the abstract level and neither measured relayed answers. The claim that Socratic follow-up is the strongest integrity control available is therefore a hypothesis, and a testable one: Chapter 7 designs a condition in which participants relay answers from another source.

Two features of the build bear on the hypothesis directly. The first is the recitation check, which is the integrity control the evidence supports best because it acts on content and responds with a question rather than a verdict (Section 4.8.1). Since GAD-T272, its weaker half, the read-out pattern, is suppressed for students with extra time and its false-flag rate can be measured. The second is a limit. The design was written for a student who reads or types an answer from another source, and a voice examination with a camera makes both harder. It is not designed for a student who receives a relayed answer by ear, through a hidden earpiece, and speaks it in their own voice. Against that case the follow-up is the only control, and whether it works is exactly the untested hypothesis. The audio signal for a second voice would not hear an earpiece.

## 5.6 The limits of surveillance

### 5.6.1 What each signal can show

The evidence assigns each kind of signal a ceiling, and the build's treatment of each can be read against it.

- **Gaze.** Webcam gaze error is about four degrees of visual angle (Papoutsaki et al., 2016; Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2018), while reading moves in jumps of seven to nine letters (Rayner, 2009). A webcam can tell that a student looked away; it cannot reliably tell reading notes from thinking. The build records gaze at the lowest severities, never scores it, and, since GAD-T261, gives a thinking grace at the start of each answer, an accommodation that removes the fade, and rates by group. The ceiling is respected in the record. It is not respected in the fade, which acts on the student during the answer on the strength of a signal that cannot tell thinking from reading (Section 5.7.3).
- **Face.** Face detection in one proctoring product flagged students with darker skin for review more often (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022), and commercial gender classification erred most on darker-skinned women (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018), which is evidence about facial analysis in general rather than about proctoring. The build's camera episodes for an absent face, a second face and a face that no longer resembles the one at the start are all exposed to this kind of disparity. The gaze-rate view of GAD-T261 reports look-aways, reading-like episodes and fades by group, but not absent-face or changed-face episodes, which are the ones the face-detection evidence concerns most directly.
- **Voice.** Voice-stress analysis has performed at about chance in laboratory and field tests (Harnsberger et al., 2009; Damphousse et al., 2007), the National Research Council found "little or no scientific basis" for it (National Research Council, 2003), and the acoustic correlates of stress are inconsistent (Giddens et al., 2013). The build's relabelling of the reviewer's line as raw acoustic differences, naming no state, matches what the measures can support. The probing use outside the EU does not (Section 5.7.2).
- **Text style.** Gadfly asks its panel to estimate how likely it is that a spoken answer "was read aloud from AI-generated or written text rather than composed while speaking," compared with the same student's other answers. The estimate is stored with the exam-time judgment of each answer, which blind grading hides; when the panel's mean estimate is 0.7 or more, it is also recorded as an integrity event, "Answer reads like written text," at low severity, which the human examiner sees while grading (`Gadfly.Server/Sessions/SessionOrchestrator.cs`). Both carry the caution that it is weak evidence that never changes the score. This is a detector of machine-written text in all but name, applied to transcribed speech. Comparing a student with their own other answers is the least unfair form such a signal could take, and it is shown only as context. But the evidence on detectors is the strongest negative evidence in Chapter 2: seven widely used detectors labelled more than half of a set of essays by non-native English writers as machine-generated (Liang et al., 2023), and detectors as a class were found neither accurate nor reliable (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023). Section 2.9.4 concluded that they "should never be used as integrity signals." The text-style estimate is not one of the tools those studies tested, and its within-student comparison may behave differently, but nothing measures how it behaves. It was the integrity signal in the build with the least support and the least scrutiny: it did not appear in the project's evidence-to-design review, and Chapter 4 did not describe it (Appendix A, correction 37). The owner has since chosen to keep it only as reviewer-only context rather than drop it (GAD-T284). It is now labelled everywhere as an unvalidated estimate whose false-flag rate has not been measured, it is hidden from the blind human examiner, with the integrity event it becomes, until the examiner's score is submitted (GAD-T285), and it remains outside the score and is never a verdict. The build also now includes the instrument that would measure it: a command that counts how often the estimate reaches the reviewer threshold over recorded sessions (`gadfly textstyle-rate`), which, run on sessions from students known to be answering honestly and separately for each group, gives its false-flag rate by group. No such data exists yet, and the evidence-to-design review now records the signal with the evidence against it (`docs/EVIDENCE_MAP.md`, mechanic 9).

### 5.6.2 Signals that act, and signals that inform

The distinction that matters most is not between strong and weak signals but between signals that inform a reviewer and signals that act on the student. A signal that only informs a reviewer, shown with an honest description of its ceiling, after the content has been graded, does the least harm a weak signal can do. A signal that acts on the student during the examination changes the conditions under which the evidence is gathered, and therefore potentially the evidence, even if it never enters the score.

By that distinction the build has four signals that act: the recitation check, which excludes an answer and asks for it again; the delivery cues, which raise the probing pressure; the gaze fade and the spoken request to look at the examiner; and, outside the EU, the voice measure as one of the delivery cues. The first acts on content and is the best supported. The other three act on signals the evidence says are weak, and two of them do so by the owner's decision.

A further point concerns timing. Section 4.8.2 recorded that the human examiner sees the gaze episodes and recitation flags while grading, because blind grading hides the machine's judgment but not the integrity context. The same was true of the text-style estimate once it passed 0.7: its score was hidden with the exam-time judgments, but the integrity event it became was not; since GAD-T284 and GAD-T285 the event, too, is hidden until the blind score is submitted. The research notes recommended showing integrity context only after the content-based score (Gadfly repository, `docs/research/06-integrity-proctoring-gaze-voice.md`), by the anchoring mechanism of Section 3.8. Until GAD-T285 the build hid the machine's judgment of the content from the blind human examiner and showed the machine's integrity context, which is the weaker of the two kinds of evidence and the one more likely to prime a judgment of the student rather than of the answer; it now hides both until the examiner's score is submitted.

## 5.7 The owner's decisions and their evidence

The project's owner has made four decisions that the evidence review questions. They are recorded in `docs/EVIDENCE_MAP.md` with the evidence on both sides, and that document does not override them. Neither does this dissertation. Each decision is a choice between values that the evidence informs but does not settle, such as how much integrity protection is worth how much burden on which students, and it belongs to the people accountable for the system. What a dissertation can do is state each decision precisely, set out the evidence for and against it at its verified level, say what the build has done since to soften it, and say what evidence would test it.

### 5.7.1 Decision (a): voice and camera only, with no typed answers

**The decision.** Examinations are spoken, with the camera on, and there is no typed-answer mode, "to stop cheating."

**The evidence for it.** Unproctored remote examinations are inflated (Alessio et al., 2017; Dendir & Maxwell, 2020), so some protection is justified. A spoken answer, followed up from the student's own words, is harder to paste from another source than a typed one: a typed answer can be produced by copying, a spoken one must at least be read aloud, and reading aloud leaves traces the recitation check can catch. The camera supplies context, such as an absent or second face, that a typed mode would not.

**The evidence against it.** Speech recognition is the largest documented fairness risk in a voice examination (Section 5.3.1), and it falls on identifiable groups: speakers of some dialects and people who stutter (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024). Joughin (2010) recommends alternative arrangements for students for whom anxiety is disabling and adjustments for speech or hearing impairment. A student who cannot use the voice examination, for any of these reasons, has under the decision no route to the assessment at all. Section 2.5.2 concluded that a typed-answer accommodation is "a requirement rather than a convenience" for such students. The integrity argument is weaker than it looks: the strongest integrity control the evidence supports, the follow-up written from the student's own answer, works on typed answers too, and with the camera kept on most of the remaining context would be kept.

**What the build has done since.** Several changes reduce the cost of the decision without changing it: transcript verification, unsure words marked for graders, word error rate by group, an adaptive end-of-answer silence, and a clock that stops during pauses. None helps a student for whom speaking is itself the barrier. The proposed accommodation (GAD-T260), which would leave voice as the default and let an instructor grant typing per student, is held.

**What would test it.** Two measurements bear on the decision. The word error rate by group, once a pilot supplies it, shows how much the voice channel costs the students it serves worst. A comparison of follow-up performance under spoken and typed answers, with and without a relayed source, would show how much integrity the voice channel adds over the follow-up alone. Chapter 7 includes both.

### 5.7.2 Decision (b): voice strain drives harder probing

**The decision.** Voice strain and stress detection drives harder probing, as the owner asked. Since GAD-T259, which the owner approved, the decision applies outside the EU only.

**The evidence for it.** Pressing a student whose answer came out shaky can bring out whether the concept is really understood, and comparing a student with their own baseline, not with other students, is the least unfair way to read any delivery signal (Section 4.3.5). The build confines the measure to steering questions and informing reviewers, never grading, and hides it from blind graders, which is the least harmful use available. And the evidence rules limit how far any signal can reach the score through the path (Section 5.3.3).

**The evidence against it.** The measure has no demonstrated validity for anything an examination cares about. Voice-stress systems performed at about chance in detecting stress, not only deception (Harnsberger et al., 2009, abstract), the field test found about nine false flags for each true one (Damphousse et al., 2007), and the acoustic correlates of stress are inconsistent, with jitter tending to fall rather than rise where any effect is found (Giddens et al., 2013; Appendix A, correction 25). The baseline is the student's first answers, when warm-up nerves and easier opening questions both differ from later ones. Anxiety, accent, second-language speaking and stuttering change the features measured (Section 2.5), so the students most likely to be pressed harder are the ones the anxiety evidence says need less pressure, not more. The same aim, pressing where an answer is weak, can be met from the content of the answer: hedging, vagueness and missing causal links are text, and the only deception cues with some support concern content (DePaulo et al., 2003). In the EU, the use is exposed to the prohibition on emotion recognition in education (Section 5.8.2), which is why the owner approved switching it off there.

**What the build has done since.** In the EU it is off by default, and when it is off neither the voice comparison nor its stand-in steers probing, though the wait before the first word and hesitations still do. Everywhere, the reviewer sees raw differences rather than a named state. The privacy documentation no longer says that nothing infers emotion. Outside the EU, the measure still raises the probing pressure at 0.5 above the student's baseline.

**What would test it.** The decision makes an empirical prediction: that answers given under raised voice-strain pressure are more often weak on content, so that pressing on them finds gaps that pressing elsewhere would not. Gadfly records the cues that set the pressure and the judged score of the next answer, so the prediction can be tested on pilot data by comparing what harder probing finds when the cue is strain and when it is content. The prediction would also have to hold across groups: if the cue fires more often for anxious, second-language or disfluent speakers at the same level of understanding, the decision trades fairness for whatever the prediction gains.

The decision also creates two regimes. A student marked as living in the EU and one who is not may take the same examination under different examiners: one whose questions can be steered by the sound of their voice, one whose questions cannot. The scoring rule makes it unlikely that this changes their grades much, but the two students do not take the same examination, and their paths are not comparable by the standard of Section 3.6.

### 5.7.3 Decision (c): the gaze fade and "look at me"

**The decision.** When the student looks away from the examiner while answering, the screen fades and the examiner asks, "Please look at me while you answer."

**The evidence for it.** The use is lawful on the Commission's own example: gaze-point tracking in an online examination "to detect if unauthorized material is used ... is not prohibited, because the system does not identify or infer emotions" (European Commission, 2025, para. 255). Unproctored examinations are inflated, so some deterrent is justified. The build keeps the gaze record at low confidence, never scores it and never penalizes a student for it, which matches what the signal can support.

**The evidence against it.** The signal cannot tell reading notes from thinking (Section 5.6.1). Explaining aloud is slow (Fox et al., 2011), so answers contain long stretches of thinking, and to the extent that students look away while they think, a fade that starts after a few seconds will fire on honest thinking. How often students look away while they think was not among the sources verified, and the project's own description of looking away as "a well-supported sign of thinking" is not supported by them (Appendix A, correction 34); the evidence review's statement that people look away while thinking is cited to Fox et al. (2011), whose meta-analysis concerns the time and accuracy of verbal reports, not gaze (Appendix A, correction 42). The objection does not need the claim: it is enough that the signal cannot tell thinking from reading. The fade adds pressure during the answer, and anxious students already do worse under proctoring (Woldeab & Brothen, 2019). Face and eye detection may fail more often for some groups (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022). Because the fade acts during the answer, it changes the conditions of the examination, and so potentially the evidence, even though the gaze record never enters the score.

**What the build has done since.** GAD-T261, which the owner approved, keeps the decision and makes it fairer: a thinking grace at the start of each answer, an instructor-declared accommodation that switches the fade and the remarks off, and rates by group. The accommodation answers the strongest objection for students known to need it. The grace answers part of the objection about thinking, though the 10-second and 6-second values are design choices, not values from a source.

**What would test it.** The rates by group, once there are enough students to compare, show whether the fade falls more often on some groups. Whether the fade deters anything can be tested only in a study with a relay condition, run with and without the fade. And whether the fade costs honest students anything can be tested by comparing, within students, the judged quality of answers during which the screen faded with those during which it did not.

### 5.7.4 Decision (d): clearing scores holds the attempt out of grading

**The decision.** When an administrator clears an attempt's scores, the grades, reports, disagreements, reconciliations, approvals and appeals are removed, results are withdrawn, and the attempt is held at "Examination In Progress," a status from which nothing moves it on its own, so that the automatic grading does not grade it again seconds later. The attempt and its transcript are kept, and it still counts as used.

**The evidence for it.** An attempt an administrator has decided to clear should not be re-graded by the machine, unseen, as soon as it is cleared. A person should decide whether and when it is graded, which is what effective human oversight asks (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 14(1)). The action is audited.

**The evidence against it.** Grading a learning outcome is a high-risk use under the Act (Annex III, point 3(b)), and removing the records of how a grade was reached, reconciled, approved and appealed removes the trail that oversight depends on. Clearing also withdraws the released feedback that makes a further attempt worth taking, since repeated testing helps only with feedback, and helps least when students mostly fail (Rowland, 2014), while the cleared attempt still counts against the student's limit.

**What the build has done since.** GAD-T262, which the owner approved, keeps the decision and answers two of the objections: the removed records are copied into an audit record before removal, and the student is told that the scores were cleared for review and that the attempt still counts. The third objection, that the attempt counts against the student's limit while its feedback is withdrawn, stands as part of the decision.

**What would test it.** This decision is less an empirical claim than a choice of procedure, and the relevant evidence is about its use: how often scores are cleared, by whom, for what reasons, and with what outcome for the student. The audit records now hold that information.

### 5.7.5 What the decisions have in common

Three of the four decisions, (a), (b) and (c), resolve a conflict between integrity and fairness in favour of integrity, and in each case the integrity gain rests on signals or arrangements whose effect on integrity is untested while their cost to some students is documented. The fourth, (d), resolves a conflict between two forms of oversight. In each case the build has since added measurement, an accommodation or an audit trail that reduces the cost without reversing the decision, and in each case the empirical test of Chapter 7 can supply the evidence that is now missing. That is the most this dissertation claims about them: that each is a hypothesis about a trade-off, and that each can be tested.

## 5.8 The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act

The analysis here is descriptive and is not legal advice (Section 1.6). It rests on the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and the Commission's guidelines on prohibited practices that were read in the research pass (Appendix A, Section A.1), and it says so where a duty depends on provisions that were not read.

### 5.8.1 Classification

A system that evaluates learning outcomes in educational institutions, and one that monitors and detects prohibited behaviour of students during tests, are listed high-risk uses (Annex III, points 3(b) and 3(d)). Gadfly is both: its panel grades objectives, and its integrity signals monitor the examination. Article 6(3) allows a listed system to escape the high-risk category where it does not materially influence the outcome of decision-making, for instance by performing a narrow procedural or purely preparatory task. The panel's grade carries a fixed weight in the combined score (0.4 by default), and its judgments during the examination choose the questions. That is material influence on the outcome, and the exemption is doubtful. The integrity signals are a closer case. They never enter the score, but four of them act on the examination (Section 5.6.2), and the recitation check excludes evidence. A system that shapes the conditions of an assessment is hard to describe as purely preparatory.

### 5.8.2 The prohibition on emotion recognition

The Act prohibits the use of AI systems "to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons" (Art. 5(1)(f)). An emotion recognition system identifies or infers "emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data" (Art. 3(39)), and biometric data includes data relating to "behavioural characteristics" (Art. 3(34)). Recital 18 lists emotions such as embarrassment and shame and excludes physical states such as pain or fatigue; Recital 44 records "serious concerns about the scientific basis" of such systems. The Commission's non-binding guidelines read "emotions" "in a wide sense" (para. 247), list voice and eye tracking among behavioural biometrics (para. 251), and give the decisive example: if an examination system "is also used to detect emotions, such as emotional arousal and anxiousness, this would fall within the scope of the prohibition" (para. 255). Integrity is not a safety reason, since safety means "protection of life and health and not to protect other interests, for example property against theft or fraud" (para. 258).

Each of Gadfly's signals can be read against this text.

- **Gaze, face and reading-like eye movement** are the guidelines' own example of a use that is not prohibited, because they look for unauthorized material, not emotions.
- **The voice measure** is the exposed one. Its inputs, pitch, jitter and loudness, are standard features of vocal stress. Its use outside the EU, to decide that a student's answer was delivered under strain and should be pressed harder, comes close to detecting "emotional arousal and anxiousness." The relabelling as raw differences does not by itself change the analysis, because the guidelines turn on what the system is used to infer, not on what its output is called. The EU default of GAD-T259 removes the exposure where the markers that trigger it are set. Two questions remain. First, the default turns on an organization's marking of itself as in the EU or EEA and on an instructor's marking of a student as living there; whether these markers track the Act's territorial scope depends on provisions not read here. Second, an administrator can turn the measurements on for everyone, including EU students, an act the project's documentation says should follow legal advice. The project's corrected privacy text now goes further than the guidelines, saying that Gadfly processes features "that the EU AI Act guidelines treat as emotion recognition when used in education"; the guidelines classify by the inference, not by the features, so the more accurate statement is that the features are of the kind from which such an inference would be made (Appendix A, correction 39).
- **The text-based delivery cue** (hedging phrases) is derived from the transcript, not from biometric data, and so falls outside the definition. The timing cues (the wait before the first word, hesitations per hundred words) are derived from the student's speech and are closer to the line. GAD-T259 switched off the voice comparison and its pause-and-speech-rate stand-in for EU students, but not these cues, which still raise the probing pressure in the EU (Appendix A, correction 40). They are used as signs of shaky delivery, not labelled as states, and a long wait or many hesitations is not an emotion in the sense of Recital 18; but they vary with anxiety (Section 2.5), and if they were used to infer it, the guidelines' example would apply to them as well.
- **The text-style estimate** infers whether an answer was composed while speaking, which is a claim about behaviour, not emotion. It does not appear to be within the prohibition, though it is within point 3(d) as monitoring.

### 5.8.3 Human oversight

High-risk systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons" (Art. 14(1)), and those overseeing them must remain aware of "the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output produced by a high-risk AI system (automation bias)" (Art. 14(4)(b)). Gadfly's grading design maps onto these provisions closely. The human weight cannot be set to zero; the human examiner grades blind by default, which is a direct guard against automation bias, given that human evaluators shown a model's judgment changed their own choice in about a third of cases (Zheng et al., 2023); only a person can approve and publish; and, since GAD-T270, the panel's calibration status is shown to graders, so that they can see when its grades are provisional.

Three features work against effective oversight. Blind grading is a policy default that an examination's policy can turn off. A reconciliation can be recorded, and the grade then approved, by the same person who entered the human score (Appendix A, correction 30), which makes the human side of oversight a single person's judgment ratified by that person. And the human examiner grades with the integrity context in view, which invites the anchoring that blind grading is meant to prevent. The calibration label adds a new consideration: a grader told that the panel is "calibrated for this exam" may defer to it more, which is the automation bias Article 14(4)(b) names. Blindness protects against this during grading; it does not protect the reconciliation that follows.

### 5.8.4 Duties not reviewed here

Classification as high-risk brings further obligations for providers and deployers of such systems. The project's own privacy documentation names risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight, accuracy documentation and registration before deployment. The provisions that establish these obligations were not read in the research pass, so this dissertation does not state them or assess the build against them. The application dates of the high-risk rules remain an open item: the Regulation as published states that it applies from 2 August 2026 (Art. 113), with later dates for some provisions, and whether amendments since have changed those dates was not checked (Appendix A, Section A.6).

## 5.9 Accountability

Accountability asks whether the people affected by a grade can see how it was reached, contest it, and know who decided. Gadfly's design is strongest on the record and weakest on independence.

**The record.** Every grading action, including every refused attempt, is written to an append-only audit log; the grading policy in force is copied into the grade record; the panel's calibration status is frozen into each session's policy; and since GAD-T262 even cleared scores leave an audit copy. The planner is deterministic and records why each question was asked, so a session can be replayed. The prompt set in force is versioned. Few published uses of language-model grading describe records this complete.

**The student's view.** A student sees a grade only once it is published, and, since GAD-T273, sees for each objective which criteria fell short, why, what the next level looks like, and how the objective's score changed since the earlier attempt. That is the feedback the retrieval-practice literature says a further attempt needs to be worth taking (Rowland, 2014; Dunlosky et al., 2013), and it is specific to what was examined, which is the only material on which a retake will improve (Pan & Rickard, 2018). Where voice measurements are on, the student also sees a short description of their own voice in their live transcript. The student does not see the integrity context recorded about them, which reviewers do see, and so cannot answer it except through an appeal.

**The separation of recommending and deciding.** The panel recommends; a person decides. That separation is real in the arithmetic, since the human weight cannot be zero and only a person can approve. It is weaker in the people. The permission to score is separated from the permission to approve by role, not by person, and the instructor role holds both (Section 4.9). An examination can therefore be graded, reconciled and approved by one person. This is the most important remaining gap in the accountability of the design, because it is the one the record cannot repair: an audit log that shows the same name in every row records the absence of independent judgment, but does not supply it.

## 5.10 Summary

This chapter has asked what a score from a synthetic Socratic examination may be taken to mean. Its answers bear on three of the research questions of Section 1.4.

On comparability (RQ2), the method as committed now meets almost every requirement the model sets for keeping the path out of the score, and reports the number of questions each objective received. What it does not yet report is how much of each objective the score covers, and what it does not yet measure is whether the signals that steer the path fire more often for some groups.

On combining machine and human judgment (RQ3), the method now gates trust in its panel by aggregate calibration, applies the thresholds to sets of answers as the evidence requires, and grades the human blind by default. The remaining weaknesses are that calibration informs rather than constrains the panel's weight, that self-preference is untested, and that one person may judge, reconcile and approve.

On integrity signals (RQ4), the evidence and the law agree on the shape of the answer. Signals that inform a reviewer, at an honest confidence and after the content has been graded, do little harm. Signals that act on the student during the examination change the evidence, and need evidence of their own. The examination's follow-up questions are the best-supported integrity control, but the support is indirect, and the claim that they defeat relayed answers remains a hypothesis. Of the signals that act, the recitation check is supported; the delivery cues, the gaze fade and, outside the EU, the voice measure are not, and the last is the one most exposed under the Act. Three of the four owner's decisions trade a documented cost to some students for an untested integrity gain. The dissertation records them as decisions, not errors, and Chapter 7 designs the study that would test each.

None of the remaining shortfalls is a matter of model capability. Every change made to Gadfly on the day this chapter describes was procedural: an evidence rule, a label, a threshold, an accommodation, an audit record. That is the thesis of Chapter 1 seen once more: the soundness of the evidence depends on conditions the method and the institution must choose to meet. Chapter 6 sets out the limits of the argument that has led here.
