# Elenchus at Scale
### Socratic Oral Assessment by Synthetic Examiners

_by MindAttic_

## Abstract

*Draft, 21 November 2026. To be revised when Chapters 3 to 8 are drafted.*

Capable language models have broken the assumption on which the written examination rested: that the words submitted were produced by the person whose name is on them. Watching students more closely is a weak remedy, because proctoring, gaze tracking, voice analysis and detectors of machine-written text either cannot identify an individual who cheated or misfire on identifiable groups of students. The older remedy is to change the assessment: to question the student aloud and follow up on what they say. Oral examination resists outsourced answers but does not scale. This dissertation asks whether a synthetic examiner, a system of language models that questions a student aloud in the Socratic manner, writes each follow-up from the student's own previous answer and judges the answers against the instructor's material, can conduct such examinations at scale, and under what conditions the evidence it gathers is fit to inform a grade. Using Gadfly, a working system, as its case, it argues that a Socratic examination is a procedure for gathering evidence about understanding, and that the soundness of that evidence depends less on the capability of the examiner's models than on conditions that the literature makes explicit: wide sampling of objectives; scoring anchored to fixed rubrics rather than to the path the conversation took; controls for anxiety, accent, disfluency and transcription error; a panel of judges from different model families combined with blind human grading and reconciliation; and integrity signals kept as context, never as verdicts. The dissertation reviews six literatures against a recorded verification ledger, states a formal model of examination as sequential evidence-gathering by questioning, states the method as requirements an implementation can be checked against, and designs the empirical test that would show whether a system meets them. It reports no empirical evaluation, and it records that no study yet tests language models grading spoken Socratic answers.

**Keywords:** Socratic method; elenchus; oral assessment; viva; large language models; LLM-as-a-judge; automated scoring; dynamic assessment; retrieval practice; academic integrity; remote proctoring; speech recognition fairness; AI Act

## Chapter 1 — Introduction

### 1.1 The problem

For most of its history, the written examination rested on an assumption so basic that it was rarely stated: the words on the page were produced by the person whose name was at the top. Remote examination weakened that assumption, and capable language models have broken it. A student who can type a question into a chat window can now return, within seconds, a fluent and usually correct answer to almost any question a course examination is likely to ask. The difficulty is not only that such answers can be copied. It is that a finished answer, however good, no longer carries much information about the understanding of the person who submitted it.

The responses on offer fall into two families. The first tries to restore the old assumption by watching the student: remote proctoring, webcam and screen monitoring, and software that claims to detect machine-written text. The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 gives reasons to doubt each of these. Proctored online examinations do produce lower scores than unproctored ones (Alessio et al., 2017; Dendir & Maxwell, 2020), which suggests that unproctored scores are inflated, but no surveillance signal has been shown to identify an individual who cheated. Detectors of machine-written text are neither accurate nor reliable (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023), and seven widely used detectors labelled more than half of a set of essays by non-native English writers as machine-generated (Liang et al., 2023). Face detection in one widely used proctoring product flagged students with darker skin more often (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022). The second family changes the assessment instead of the surveillance: it asks for work whose production is hard to delegate. The oldest such form is the oral examination, in which the examiner hears the answer being made and can ask about it.

Oral examination has a long record in doctoral defences, professional certification and medical licensing, and Chapter 2 shows that it can be made reliable (Wass et al., 2003; van der Vleuten et al., 2010). It has one decisive weakness: it does not scale. Each examination costs the full attention of at least one qualified examiner for its whole length, and reliable scores in assessments of competence require wide sampling across content and examiners, with estimates of at least three to four hours of testing time for minimally reliable scores (van der Vleuten et al., 2010). In postgraduate medical certification, some countries have moved away from oral examinations altogether because of continuing concerns about whether they support certification decisions reliably and fairly (Memon et al., 2010).

This dissertation asks whether that weakness can now be removed without giving up what makes oral examination worth having. Language models can do more than write answers. They can ask questions, listen to answers transcribed from speech, follow up on what was said, and judge answers against a rubric. A synthetic examiner could, in principle, conduct a Socratic oral examination for every student in a course, at any hour, in parallel. Whether it should, and under what conditions the evidence it gathers can bear the weight of a grade, are the questions this work sets out to answer.

### 1.2 The case: Gadfly

The dissertation is built around a working system. Gadfly is a platform, developed by MindAttic, in which a student demonstrates understanding of a subject by being questioned out loud, in the manner of Socrates, by a synthetic examiner that follows up on what the student has just said. Its design, as recorded in the project's own plan, rests on five commitments.

1. **Grounding in the instructor's material.** The instructor supplies the source material. It is parsed and divided into passages, a set of language models extracts a concept map from it, and for each concept a rubric is built listing the points that a real understanding covers, the acceptable variants and the common misconceptions, each point tied to the passages it came from. The instructor can review and edit both, and edited items are kept when the material is processed again; the current build does not require that approval before an examination runs (Section 4.2).
2. **Questions written from the student's own words.** The examiner chooses, for each concept, one of a small set of moves: Elicit ("what is it, in your own words?"), Clarify (pin down a vague term), Probe ("why does that follow?"), Counterexample (offer a case that breaks the student's definition), Contradiction (confront the student with two of their own claims that conflict), Apply or Transfer (a new situation that needs the concept rather than its definition), Synthesize (connect two concepts) and Close (end the concept because understanding has been shown, the student has reached an acknowledged impasse, or time has run out). In the current build Close is not a question the examiner asks: a concept ends when fixed rules of evidence say it is settled, or when a limit closes it (Section 4.5). Each follow-up is generated from the student's previous answer, so it cannot be prepared in advance.
3. **A ledger of claims.** The examiner extracts the claims the student makes and keeps them, so that later claims can be checked against earlier ones. This is the device that gives the elenchus, the Socratic examination of a person's beliefs, its name in the system.
4. **Judgment by a panel, with a human in charge.** Each answer is judged against the source material by a panel of language models from different families, each citing the passages it relied on and free to dissent. A human examiner grades blind, without seeing the panel's scores until their own are submitted. Combined scores are computed under a policy fixed before grading begins, large disagreements block finalization until they are reconciled with a documented cause and resolution (by a second reviewer when one is requested; the current build does not require one, Section 4.9), and only a human can approve and publish a grade.
5. **Integrity signals as evidence, never as verdicts.** Webcam and audio signals (another face, a second voice, sustained gaze at a region off the screen) are recorded for a human reviewer. They never fail a student automatically and never enter the score arithmetic. In the current build some of them do act on the examination itself (delivery cues, including voice strain, steer how hard the next question presses, and looking away dims the screen), and an answer that reads as recited is left out of the evidence; Chapter 4 examines each.

Gadfly is used here as a case in the methodological sense: a concrete, inspectable instance of the general design the dissertation examines. The argument does not depend on Gadfly's particular code, and Chapter 4 states the method in terms that any implementation could meet or fail. But a working system disciplines theory. Every claim made about synthetic Socratic examination must be one that a system like Gadfly could be built to satisfy, and every requirement the literature imposes must be one that can be checked against a specification.

### 1.3 The thesis

The thesis of this dissertation is that **a Socratic oral examination is a procedure for gathering evidence about understanding, and that a synthetic examiner can conduct one at scale whose evidence is fit to inform a grade, but only under conditions that the literature makes explicit and that are largely independent of how capable the examiner's language models are.**

The thesis has three parts.

The first is a reframing. An examination is not a container of questions but a sequence of observations, each chosen in the light of the ones before, from which an inference about a student's understanding is drawn. Socratic questioning is distinctive because each question is chosen to be maximally informative given what the student has just said, and because the answers it elicits are hard to produce without the understanding they are meant to reveal. Chapter 3 states this as a formal model of evidence-gathering by questioning.

The second is a claim of possibility. The elements of a Socratic examination that once required a skilled human examiner (following an answer, choosing a probing next question, noticing that two claims conflict, judging an explanation against a body of material) are elements that current language models can perform, imperfectly but measurably. Scale removes the main practical objection to oral examination.

The third is a claim about conditions, and it carries most of the weight. The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 shows that the validity of an oral examination depends on things that have little to do with the examiner's intelligence: how widely it samples the content, how far the scoring is anchored to fixed criteria rather than to the path the conversation took, whether anxiety, accent and disfluency are kept from contaminating the score, how human judgment is combined with machine judgment, and whether integrity signals are kept in their place. A more capable examiner makes the conversation better. It does not, by itself, make the evidence sound. The conditions under which it is sound are institutional and procedural, and the dissertation's main contribution is to state them, ground each in evidence, and design the test that would show whether a system meets them.

### 1.4 Research questions

The dissertation addresses five questions.

- **RQ1. What does a Socratic examination measure, and what can it not measure?** In particular, what does a contradiction between two of a student's claims show, and what does a correct answer to a probing follow-up show? (Chapters 2 and 3.)
- **RQ2. Under what conditions can adaptive questioning by a synthetic examiner produce evidence that is comparable across students?** Adaptive follow-up is the method's strength and the main threat to its reliability, since no two students receive the same questions. (Chapters 3, 4 and 5.)
- **RQ3. How should the judgments of a panel of language models and of a human examiner be combined into a grade?** What does the evidence on machine judgment, human marking reliability and automation bias require of that combination? (Chapters 2, 4 and 5.)
- **RQ4. What is the proper role of integrity signals in a spoken examination?** What do webcam gaze and acoustic features of speech actually measure, and what does the law of the jurisdictions in which such a system would operate permit? (Chapters 2 and 5.)
- **RQ5. What empirical study would test whether a synthetic Socratic examination meets these conditions?** (Chapter 7.)

### 1.5 Contributions

The dissertation makes four contributions.

1. **A verified synthesis** of six literatures that bear on synthetic oral examination and have not previously been brought together: the philosophical literature on the Socratic elenchus, the clinical interview and the psychology of verbal reports; the validity, reliability and fairness of oral assessment; retrieval practice, self-explanation and transfer; dynamic assessment; the reliability and biases of language models as judges; and the evidence on proctoring, gaze tracking and voice-stress analysis. Every source was checked at a recorded level, and Appendix A records that level for each.
2. **A formal model** of the examination as sequential evidence-gathering, which separates what a question is for (reducing uncertainty about a student's understanding of an objective) from what the score is (an inference about that understanding), and which makes precise why adaptive questioning and comparable scoring are compatible only if the score is anchored to the objective and not to the conversational path.
3. **A statement of method,** the Gadfly method, as a set of requirements an implementation can be checked against, each traced to the evidence that motivates it.
4. **A design for an empirical test,** stating the measures, comparisons and thresholds that would show whether a system that implements the method produces evidence fit to inform a grade, and the results that would show it does not.

### 1.6 Scope and limits

The work is a theoretical and design dissertation. It does not report an empirical evaluation of Gadfly, because none has yet been run, and it does not claim that synthetic Socratic examination works. It states the conditions under which it would, and the study that would find out. Several limits follow and are stated here so that no later chapter appears to claim more.

- **No source tests the central practice.** The verification pass behind Chapter 2 found no study of language models grading spoken, transcribed Socratic answers, multi-turn follow-ups or per-objective scoring in an oral examination. Agreement figures from essays and short written answers are evidence about neighbouring tasks, not about this one, and the dissertation never quotes them as Gadfly's expected accuracy.
- **The integrity claim is a hypothesis.** It is often said that a person relaying another source's answers will contradict themselves under questioning. No verified source supports this, and Chapter 2 records it as a hypothesis for the empirical test.
- **Some populations are not covered by the evidence.** No source on neurodivergent students in oral or voice examinations was verified, and the dissertation says nothing about them beyond the need to find out.
- **The method serves examination, not tutoring.** A Socratic tutor that hints is a different instrument with a different purpose. Chapter 2 reviews dynamic assessment, in which help given during the test is part of the measurement, and Chapter 4 argues that any such mode must be kept apart from the scored examination.
- **The legal analysis is descriptive.** The dissertation reports what the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act and the European Commission's guidelines on it say about systems that evaluate learning outcomes or infer emotions in education. It is not legal advice, and the application dates of the high-risk rules are recorded in Appendix A as an item to confirm.

## Chapter 2 — Literature Review

### 2.1 Scope and method of the review

A synthetic Socratic examination draws on several literatures that rarely cite one another. Philosophers have argued for half a century about what the Socratic elenchus can establish. Developmental psychologists built the clinical interview to follow a child's reasoning without leading it. Researchers in medical education measured the reliability of oral examinations. Cognitive psychologists showed that being tested changes what is learned. The dynamic assessment tradition made help given during a test part of the measurement. Computer scientists have begun to measure how language models behave as judges, and a separate literature has measured what remote proctoring, gaze tracking and voice analysis can detect. This chapter brings these literatures together around the questions set out in Chapter 1.

### 2.2 The elenchus: what Socratic questioning examines

#### 2.2.1 The examination of a claim to knowledge

The image from which Gadfly takes its name is Socrates' own. In Plato's *Apology*, Socrates describes himself as attached to the city "as a gadfly to a horse, which, though large and well bred, is sluggish on account of his size and needs to be aroused by stinging" (Plato, 1966, 30e). The same text describes what his questioning was for. Examining a man reputed to be wise, Socrates concluded that "this man thinks he knows something when he does not, whereas I, as I do not know anything, do not think I do either" (Plato, 1966, 21d). The target of the examination is a person's claim to know, not only a single proposition: Socrates "tried to show him that he thought he was wise, but was not" (Plato, 1966, 21c–d).

The *Apology* also records a cost. In Plato's account the examination made those examined hostile to the examiner (Plato, 1966, 21d). An examination designed to expose the gap between thinking one knows and knowing is, for the person examined, an exposure. That cost reappears in the modern literature on oral assessment as anxiety (Section 2.5), and it bears on how a synthetic examiner should word its most confrontational move.

#### 2.2.2 The slave-boy and the problem of leading questions

The *Meno* contains the classic picture of questioning that draws a correct answer out of a learner. Socrates leads an untaught boy to the solution of a geometrical problem and insists that he is not teaching: "Do you observe, Meno, that I am not teaching the boy anything, but merely asking him each time?" (Plato, 1967, 82e). The passage separates three states that a Socratic examination must distinguish: confident but mistaken belief, the felt perplexity that follows its refutation, and true opinion that is not yet knowledge. Before the questioning the boy "thought he knew then, and confidently answered as though he knew"; afterwards he "besides not knowing does not think he knows" (Plato, 1967, 84a–b). Socrates offers a route from true opinion to understanding: "if he were repeatedly asked these same questions in a variety of forms, you know he will have in the end as exact an understanding of them as anyone" (Plato, 1967, 85c).

The passage is often cited as a model of questioning that does not lead. It is a poor one. Many of Socrates' questions between 84d and 85b are leading questions that invite a yes or no: "Has not each of the inside lines cut off half of each of these four spaces? Boy Yes." (Plato, 1967, 85a). The text that names the ideal of not teaching also shows how easily questioning slides into supplying the answer. For an examination this matters twice over. A question that contains its answer measures nothing, and a synthetic examiner generating questions from a student's previous answer is under constant pressure to make the next question easier to answer than it should be. Section 2.3 shows that a better model of the non-leading question comes from developmental psychology, not from the *Meno*.

#### 2.2.3 Consistency, not truth

The modern scholarly debate over the elenchus was set by Gregory Vlastos's 1983 article. Vlastos defined the elenchus as "a search for moral truth by adversary argument in which a thesis is debated only if asserted as the answerer's own belief and is regarded as refuted only if the negation of his thesis is deduced from his own beliefs" (Vlastos, 1983, p. 30, as cited in Wolfsdorf, 2013). From this definition he drew what became known as the problem of the elenchus: "how is it that Socrates claims to have proved a thesis false when, in point of logic, all he has proved in any given argument is that the thesis is inconsistent with the conjunction of agreed-upon premises for which no reason has been given in that argument?" (Vlastos, 1983, p. 49, as cited in Wolfsdorf, 2013). Wolfsdorf (2013) describes this article as the work that "galvanized" Anglophone scholarship on Socrates' method for the following quarter century, and reports that Vlastos himself later conceded that his solution to the problem fits only part of the dialogues.

Benson's response dissolves the problem rather than solving it. Read through Wolfsdorf's (2013) summary, Benson (1995) concludes, after examining the elenchi of several early dialogues, that Socrates shows only that the interlocutor's beliefs are inconsistent, so that the problem Vlastos posed does not arise in those texts. Benson (2000), in a book-length treatment of the epistemology of the early dialogues, places the question-and-answer elenchus among a set of epistemological concerns, with a model of knowledge that the publisher's description calls closer to modern scientific understanding than to ordinary knowledge. Seeskin (1987) reads the method as moral as well as epistemic: its goal, in the publisher's description, is "improving the soul of the respondent."

For an examination the consequence of this debate is direct. On the best-supported reading, an elenctic exchange establishes that a person's stated claims do not fit together. It does not establish which of the conflicting claims is wrong, and it does not, by itself, establish that the person lacks understanding of the subject. A student confronted with two of their own claims that conflict has shown an incoherence, and may resolve it well or badly; a resolution in either direction can be correct. The source material and the rubric, not the contradiction, must decide which claim was mistaken. The literature also yields a rule that a synthetic examiner can follow: only the answerer's own beliefs count. Vlastos's definition admits as premises only what the answerer asserts as their own, so an examiner that confronts a student with a claim the examiner supplied and the student merely accepted is not conducting an elenchus. Finally, the scholarship gives no warrant for speaking of a single "Socratic method": the elenctic pattern Vlastos described fits only part of the dialogues (Wolfsdorf, 2013).

### 2.3 The clinical interview and the psychology of verbal reports

#### 2.3.1 Following the answer without suggesting it

The method that comes closest to what a Socratic examiner must do when it follows a student's answer was developed by Piaget to study children's reasoning. Piaget rejected both the fixed questionnaire, which imposes the examiner's framing, and pure observation, which cannot direct attention to what matters. His alternative was a clinical examination that follows the child's own answers: "The only way to avoid such difficulties is to vary the questions, to make counter-suggestions, in short, to give up all idea of a fixed questionnaire" (Piaget, 1929, p. 4). The clinical method makes two demands that matter here. The examiner must follow the answer the child actually gave, and the examiner must not suggest the answer the examiner expects. A conviction that survives a counter-suggestion is better evidence than one stated once, which echoes the *Meno*'s "variety of forms" (Plato, 1967, 85c) without its leading questions.

Ginsburg (1997) restates the clinical interview as a method for research, clinical practice and education, in the tradition of Piaget and Vygotsky. In the publisher's description, the book argues that standardized tests often miss complex aspects of thinking, and its chapters include guidance for conducting the interview and an evaluation of how good clinical interviews are. The clinical interview thus offers a precedent for a flexible, answer-following examination that is also disciplined. Its discipline also yields a sharper test of whether a question leads than the *Meno* does: a question can suggest an answer by presupposing it, not only by stating it. "What makes X happen?" presupposes that something outside X makes it happen.

#### 2.3.2 What spoken reasoning can and cannot show

An oral examination asks people to put their reasoning into words. Two questions follow: does putting it into words change the reasoning, and can people's accounts of their reasoning be trusted?

On the first question, Ericsson and Simon (1980) argued, in the words of their abstract, that "verbalizing information is shown to affect cognitive processes only if the instructions require verbalization of information that would not otherwise be attended to." Their later work drew a distinction that is decisive for an examination. Thinking aloud, in which a person voices the thoughts they are already having, need not change the course of thought, but "verbal descriptions of one's thinking directed to another individual are likely to alter the course of thinking" (Ericsson & Simon, 1998, p. 181). A meta-analysis of 94 studies with nearly 3,500 participants found that plain thinking aloud had an effect on performance "indistinguishable from zero (r = -.03)," whereas "procedures that entail describing or explaining thoughts and actions are significantly reactive, leading to higher performance than silent control conditions," and that "all verbal reporting procedures tend to increase times to complete tasks" (Fox et al., 2011, abstract).

A Socratic examination is not thinking aloud. It is explaining to a listener, which is the reactive kind of verbal report. Two consequences follow. First, the examination is likely to change the student, usually for the better: the student who explains may understand more at the end of the examination than at its start, so that improvement late in a session is not proof of knowledge brought to it. Second, spoken answers will be slower than silent reasoning. Long pauses and slow speech are normal properties of the format, which matters for any integrity signal that treats latency or hesitation as suspicious (Section 2.10).

On the second question, Nisbett and Wilson (1977) reviewed evidence that "there may be little or no direct introspective access to higher order cognitive processes," and that people's reports of why they responded as they did "are based on a priori, implicit causal theories" (abstract). Their review is often cited as showing that verbal reports are worthless. It does not show that: the same abstract states that "accurate reports will occur when influential stimuli are salient and are plausible causes of the responses they produce." Ericsson and Simon (1980) located the source of inaccurate reports in requests for "information that was never directly heeded, thus forcing subjects to infer rather than remember their mental processes" (abstract). For an examination the lesson is to score justifications rather than introspection. A rubric criterion such as "depth of reasoning" should judge whether the student's explanation is correct and connected to the principles of the subject, not whether it is a faithful report of the mental process that produced the answer. Questions of the form "how did you arrive at that?" ask for exactly the kind of information that may never have been attended to.

### 2.4 Oral assessment: validity, reliability and fairness

#### 2.4.1 Describing an oral assessment

Joughin (2010) describes oral assessments as varying along six dimensions: what is assessed, interaction, authenticity, structure, who assesses, and whether the assessment is purely oral or combined with other modes. The framework, which Joughin restates in the 2010 guide from his earlier work, is reused in later medical-education work (Memon et al., 2010). It locates a synthetic Socratic examination precisely. Interaction is high, since every follow-up is written from the previous answer. Structure is low to medium: the objectives are fixed, but the path through them is not. The assessors are a panel of language models and a human examiner. The mode is purely oral.

#### 2.4.2 Reliability comes from sampling

The most widely repeated claim about oral examinations is that structure makes them reliable. The evidence supports a different and more useful claim: reliability comes mostly from sampling. Van der Vleuten et al. (2010) state the principle bluntly: "one measure is no measure, and single-point assessments are not to be trusted." They argue that competence is specific rather than generic, that objectivity does not equal reliability, and that "even the notoriously subjective, old-fashioned, oral examination can be made reliable by wide sampling across content and examiners," while estimating that "at least 3–4 h of testing time are required to obtain minimally reliable scores."

The clearest quantitative evidence comes from the oral component of the membership examination of the Royal College of General Practitioners. In 896 candidates who each sat two 20-minute orals of five topics, the reported reliability coefficients were "intercase 0.65; (B) pass/fail 0.85" (Wass et al., 2003, abstract). The variance in scores was 41% attributable to candidates, 32% to the content of the orals and 27% to examiners and general error, and the authors projected that four orals with two examiners each, or five with one, would raise reliability to 0.78 for the intercase coefficient and 0.94 for pass or fail decisions. The content sampled contributed more to score variance than the examiners did. The authors conclude that "structured oral examinations can achieve reliabilities appropriate to high stakes examinations" (Wass et al., 2003, abstract), but their own figures attribute the gain to more topics and more examiners.

The implication for a synthetic examiner is easy to miss. The temptation is to improve reliability by improving the judge: a stronger model, a larger panel. The evidence suggests that reliability will usually improve more by examining more objectives than by adding judges, because the content sampled is the larger source of error. An examination on two or three concepts, however carefully judged, is a small sample.

#### 2.4.3 The cost of adaptive questioning

Joughin (2010) names variation in follow-up questions between candidates as a threat to reliability, and warns that "examiners can mistake a student's articulateness for knowledge" (p. 7). Both warnings apply with full force to a synthetic examiner. Adaptive follow-up, the source of the method's resistance to rehearsed or relayed answers, is also the source of its incomparability: no two students receive the same questions. The tension cannot be removed, but it can be managed. If every objective opens with the same question, and every answer is scored against the same rubric for that objective rather than against the depth the conversation happened to reach, then the path can vary while the standard does not. Chapter 3 states this condition formally.

#### 2.4.4 Oral and written formats compared

The direct comparisons are small and read here only at the abstract level, but they are consistent. In a study in which 99 biology students were randomly assigned to oral or written versions of the same questions, and a second cohort of 29 took both, both cohorts scored significantly higher in the oral format; students reported more nervousness about orals but often judged them more useful and more authentic, and "there was no evidence of particular groups of students being disadvantaged in the oral tests" (Huxham et al., 2012, abstract). The last finding is easily overstated. It does not show that anxiety has no effect on oral scores, and the sample was too small to detect small effects in subgroups. A single-course case study of online oral examination found the format workable and useful against impersonation and dishonesty, with validity, reliability and fairness depending on careful planning (Akimov & Malin, 2020). A survey of students who took scaffolded authentic assessments ending in an interactive oral reported that students saw such assessments as deterring misconduct (Sotiriadou et al., 2020). None of these studies involved automated scoring.

### 2.5 Construct-irrelevant variance: anxiety, accent and speech recognition

#### 2.5.1 Anxiety

Joughin (2010) puts the concern in one sentence: "anxiety that interferes with a student's performance will not give a true indication of his or her ability" (p. 7). The evidence that test anxiety is associated with poorer performance is extensive. A meta-analysis of 562 studies concluded, in the words of its abstract, that "test anxiety (TA) causes poor performance," and reported that "improved test performance and grade point average (GPA) consistently accompany TA reduction" (Hembree, 1988). The first statement is a causal reading of largely correlational data; the second, that scores rise when anxiety is treated, is the stronger evidence. A later meta-analysis of 238 studies found test anxiety "significantly and negatively related to a wide range of educational performance outcomes," with relationships in the small-to-moderate range, and higher anxiety where tests are perceived as difficult or carry high stakes (von der Embse et al., 2018, abstract). Anxiety about speaking a second language is, moreover, a construct distinct from general test anxiety (Horwitz et al., 1986).

A spoken examination adds speaking anxiety to test anxiety, and an examination conducted in a second language adds language anxiety to both. The effect is modest on average but real, and it bears most heavily on the students least able to absorb it. Joughin (2010) recommends rehearsal ("Practising presentations in class and providing rehearsals for vivas may help," p. 7) and alternative arrangements for students for whom anxiety is disabling. For a synthetic examiner, design choices that add time pressure, such as a fixed silence that ends an answer or a clock that keeps running during a pause, fall hardest on anxious, second-language and disfluent speakers.

#### 2.5.2 Speech recognition

The largest documented fairness risk in a voice examination is not the prejudice of a human listener but the speech recognizer. In a test of five commercial automatic speech recognition systems on speech from black and white American speakers, "all five ASR systems exhibited substantial racial disparities," with an average word error rate of 0.35 for black speakers against 0.19 for white speakers, and "the race gap was equally large on a subset of identical phrases spoken by black and white individuals" (Koenecke et al., 2020, abstract). Six leading recognizers tested on the speech of people who stutter showed "consistent and statistically significant accuracy bias across all ASRs against disfluent speech," in word error rate, character error rate and semantic accuracy (Mujtaba et al., 2024, abstract).

In a synthetic oral examination the transcript is what the judges score. A transcription error is therefore not noise around the measurement but a change in the evidence, and it is a change that falls systematically on identifiable groups. The literature implies three obligations: measure the recognizer's error rate by accent, dialect and fluency group; make transcript correction easy and visible to reviewers; and flag segments the recognizer was unsure of, so that they are not scored as wrong content. It also implies that a typed-answer accommodation, which Joughin's (2010) recommendation of adjustments for speech or hearing impairment supports, is a requirement rather than a convenience.

### 2.6 The examination as a learning event

#### 2.6.1 Retrieval practice

A large literature shows that being tested on material improves later retention of it more than studying it again. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) put the conclusion in their title and abstract: "testing is a powerful means of improving learning, not just assessing it." Their result needs its context. One week after study, students who had been tested "recalled 56% of the material, whereas the restudy group recalled only 42%"; but five minutes after study, restudying had been ahead (81% against 75%), and the final test was free recall of the same passage. The study shows durable retention, not understanding. Karpicke and Blunt (2011) went further, showing that retrieval practice "enhanced long-term learning above and beyond elaborative study with concept mapping by one and a half standard deviations (d = 1.50)" (Fig. 1 caption), on a final test that included inference questions requiring several concepts to be connected, and that "the advantage of retrieval practice occurred even when the criterial test involved creating concept maps" (abstract).

The meta-analyses agree on the direction and qualify the size. Rowland (2014) analysed 159 effect sizes from 61 studies and found an overall random-effects effect of g = 0.50 (95% CI [0.42, 0.58]), with high heterogeneity and with "initial recall tests yielding larger testing benefits than recognition tests" (abstract). Rowland's moderator analysis also found essentially no benefit from testing when there was no feedback and success on the practice test was 50% or lower. Adesope et al. (2017) found that "practice tests are more beneficial for learning than restudying and all other comparison conditions," with effects "moderated by the features of practice tests" (abstract). Dunlosky et al. (2013) "rate practice testing as having high utility," noting that "the advantage of practice testing with feedback over restudy is extremely robust" (Section 8.5).

Two boundary conditions qualify the evidence. First, benefits transfer, but selectively. Pan and Rickard (2018) found that "testing can yield transferrable learning as measured relative to a nontesting reexposure control condition" (d = 0.40, 95% CI [0.31, 0.50]), with transfer "greatest across test formats, to application and inference questions," but "weakest to rearranged stimulus-response items, to untested materials seen during initial study, and to problems involving worked examples" (abstract). Second, the effect may shrink with the complexity of the material. Van Gog and Sweller (2015) argue that research "indicated that the testing effect decreases as the complexity of learning materials increases" and may "even disappear when the complexity of learning material is very high" (abstract). Karpicke and Aue (2015) reply that "van Gog and Sweller's idea of 'element interactivity' is not defined in a quantitative, measurable way" (abstract). The debate is unresolved.

For a synthetic Socratic examination, this literature supports a claim of formative value with three conditions attached. Open, recall-type questions of the kind the method asks are the format that helps most (Rowland, 2014). Repeated attempts help only if the student learns, between attempts, what was missing; an examination that gives no feedback during the session can deliver its learning benefit only through the feedback in its released results. And the benefit will be specific to what was asked. A student who retakes an examination improves on what the examination probed, not on what it did not reach (Pan & Rickard, 2018).

#### 2.6.2 Self-explanation and the "why" question

The follow-up that asks "why does that follow?" draws on a second literature. Chi et al. (1989) found, in protocols of physics students studying worked examples, that "'Good' students learn with understanding: They generate many explanations which refine and expand the conditions for the action parts of the example solutions, and relate these actions to principles in the text" (abstract, p. 145). The finding is correlational. Chi et al. (1994) supplied the experiment: fourteen eighth-grade students prompted to explain each line of a text on the circulatory system gained more from pretest to posttest than ten who read the text twice, and on the harder questions, which required inference, the prompted group improved by 22.6% and the unprompted group by 12.5% (abstract, p. 439; pp. 452–454). A meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes found an overall effect of g = .55 for inducing self-explanation, and prompts phrased as questions (g = .56) did at least as well as prompts phrased as instructions (g = .40) (Bisra et al., 2018). Dunlosky et al. (2013) gave elaborative interrogation and self-explanation "moderate utility assessments," with concerns about longer and more complex material and about durability over educationally relevant delays.

The bearing on an examination is real but indirect. The self-explanation literature studies learners explaining material to themselves while studying, and Chi, as Bisra et al. (2018) report, distinguishes self-explanation from "explaining to another." Answering an examiner's "why" question is explaining to another. The evidence therefore supports the "why" question in two ways by analogy: as a probe, because an answer that ties a step to the principle behind it marks a learner who understands (Chi et al., 1989), and as a moderate aid to learning (Bisra et al., 2018; Chi et al., 1994). It also supports keeping hints out of the scored examination, since self-explanation outperformed explanations supplied by an instructor in the meta-analysis (Bisra et al., 2018).

### 2.7 Measuring understanding rather than recall

#### 2.7.1 Taxonomies of objectives and of answers

The original taxonomy of educational objectives ordered six classes, Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis and Evaluation, which its authors said "appears to us to represent something of the hierarchical order of the different classes of objectives" (Bloom et al., 1956, Introduction). The revision renamed the classes as verbs, moved Synthesis above Evaluation and renamed it Create, added a separate knowledge dimension (factual, conceptual, procedural and metacognitive), and relaxed "the requirement of a strict hierarchy ... to allow the categories to overlap one another" (Krathwohl, 2002). The revision's own overview notes that some processes under Understand, such as explaining, are more complex than at least one under Apply (Krathwohl, 2002). The rank of a category is therefore not a measure of difficulty, and an examination should not award points by category.

The original taxonomy draws a line that matters for the Socratic move that asks "what would change if...?" Predicting consequences from conditions already given is extrapolation, which Bloom et al. (1956) place under Comprehension: it "differs from application, however, in that the thinking is based on what is given rather than on some abstraction brought from the other experiences to the situation." Application requires something else: "the emphasis in writing application items is on getting situations new to the student" (Bloom et al., 1956). A what-if inside the scenario the student has described tests extrapolation; a question that requires the principle in a situation new to the student tests application. An examination that claims to probe transfer must ask the second.

Taxonomies of objectives describe what an examiner intends to test. A different tradition classifies the structure of the answer a student actually gives. The SOLO taxonomy of Biggs and Collis (1982) is commonly described as distinguishing answers that make one relevant point, several unconnected points, points integrated into a coherent whole, and a whole generalized to a new domain. The book was located but not read in this research pass, so the dissertation uses the distinction only as a commonly described one and does not attribute specific wording to it. It is nonetheless the kind of distinction a claim ledger makes visible: a ledger records how many relevant claims a student made and whether they were connected.

#### 2.7.2 Transfer

What an examination of understanding most wants to know is whether the student can use what they know in a situation they have not seen. The research on transfer gives that question less support than it is usually given. Barnett and Ceci (2002) observe that the debate over far transfer has "made little progress toward resolution," attribute this to "a failure to specify various dimensions along which transfer can occur," and propose nine such dimensions, concluding that "estimation of a single effect size for far transfer is misguided" and that, while "evidence for transfer under some conditions is substantial," "critical conditions for many key questions are untested" (abstract). An examination that labels its transfer questions "near" or "far" says too little; it should say in what respects each question's situation differs from the source material.

Bransford and Schwartz (1999) add a sharper point. Most transfer studies use what they call "sequestered problem solving," in which learners have "no opportunities ... to demonstrate their abilities to learn to solve new problems by seeking help from other resources such as texts or colleagues or by trying things out" (pp. 8–9), receive feedback and revise. Their thesis is that this methodology "is responsible for much of the pessimism about evidence for transfer" (p. 9), and they propose measuring "preparation for future learning" instead. A Socratic examination with no resources and no hints is a sequestered measure. It measures unaided direct application and will understate transfer. Improvement across attempts after feedback is a candidate measure of preparation for future learning, but one that would need its own validation.

### 2.8 Dynamic assessment

#### 2.8.1 The zone of proximal development

Every examination makes a choice about help. The Socratic examination described in Chapter 1 gives none: its clarifying questions must not hint. Dynamic assessment makes the opposite choice, and its literature is the strongest argument that the choice has a cost.

The tradition begins with Vygotsky's zone of proximal development, "the distance between the actual developmental level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential development as determined through problem solving under adult guidance or in collaboration with more capable peers" (Vygotsky, 1978, p. 86). Vygotsky's argument is that unaided performance is incomplete information: "The actual developmental level characterizes mental development retrospectively, while the zone of proximal development characterizes mental development prospectively" (pp. 86–87). Two corrections to the usual retelling should be recorded. The passage says "more capable peers"; the phrase "more knowledgeable other" is not in it. And the 1978 text is an edited translation, so its wording should be quoted as that edition's.

#### 2.8.2 Interventionist and interactionist assessment

Dynamic assessment, in the description of Haywood and Lidz (2007), "embeds interaction within the framework of a test-intervene-retest approach." Its traditions divide on how the help is given. Lantolf and Poehner (2004) ground dynamic assessment in the zone of proximal development and define it by the examiner's mediation of performance through prompts, hints and leading questions, both to reveal abilities and to promote development during the assessment. Later work distinguishes an interventionist model, in which, as Poehner puts it, mediators "must instead follow a highly scripted approach to mediation in which all prompts, hints, and leading questions have been arranged hierarchically," from an interactionist model in which mediation emerges from the dialogue (Poehner, 2008, pp. 44–45, as cited in Sarabi Asl et al., 2024). Sarabi Asl et al. (2024) observe that the scripted model, "while bringing about the comfort of better generalizability and large-scale administration, deprives learners and instructors of co-creating a ZPD" (p. 5).

A current interventionist protocol shows what a fixed ladder of prompts looks like. Children who could not solve an item after a general instruction "received help according to a standardised graduated prompting protocol that consisted of four prompts," running from metacognitive to step-by-step, which "were only provided after an incorrect answer," and scoring used pre- and post-test accuracy together with "the number of hints children were provided with, and the type of hints they utilised" (Stad et al., 2019). The interactionist tradition rejects such ladders. The authors of the Learning Potential Assessment Device write that "interactions that are limited in their mediational flexibility, such as graduated prompting ... will not make possible the detection of the microchanges which can be produced in the individual on a variety of levels" (Feuerstein et al., n.d.). The field does not agree that the number of prompts a student needs is a good measure.

#### 2.8.3 Validity and reliability of dynamic assessment

The claim that dynamic assessment predicts later achievement better than conventional tests is overstated. A review of 24 studies found "superior predictive validity for DA when feedback is not contingent on student response, when applied to students with disabilities rather than at-risk or typically achieving students, and when independent DA and criterion-referenced tests were used as outcomes" (Caffrey et al., 2008, abstract). The same research group later summarized that review as finding "that traditional tests and DA similarly predicted future academic performance" (Fuchs et al., 2011), while showing that their own scripted dynamic assessment of early reading, with "five levels of more and more explicit scaffolded instruction," contributed unique variance to later reading outcomes beyond established predictors, at a cost of 20 to 30 minutes per administration. The finding that prediction was best when feedback was not contingent on the student's response favours a fixed ladder over improvised help.

The measurement problems are acknowledged within the field. De Beer (2006) writes that "there is a paucity of published empirical data on the reliability and validity of dynamic testing," reports that, "according to Grigorenko and Sternberg (1998), multiple attempts to quantify learning potential and transform such testing into robust psychological diagnostic tools have not produced consistent results," and warns that "the difference score or ZPD has often (incorrectly) been referred to as that which indicates 'potential'." A gain score, or a count of prompts needed, is confounded with the level at which the student started.

For a Socratic examination the conclusion is a separation, not a rejection. Nothing in this literature undermines an unhinted examination: unaided performance is incomplete information, not wrong information. But the literature supports, as a separate activity, a practice mode in which help is given and the student's response to it is observed. If such help is ever counted, comparability requires the interventionist form: the same instructor-written ladder of prompts for every student, given only after an inadequate answer. Help improvised by a language model is interactionist and may be valuable for learning, but its prompt counts are not comparable between students. None of this evidence, which comes from young children, students with disabilities, and decoding and reasoning tasks, has been tested on adults answering conceptual questions aloud.

### 2.9 Machine judgment

#### 2.9.1 Language models as judges

The use of language models to judge other models' outputs has produced the first systematic evidence on their reliability as raters, and it shows both promise and specific, measurable biases. In the study that established the practice, the strongest model's agreement with human preferences "reaches 85%, which is even higher than the agreement among humans (81%)," but this figure is for pairwise preferences between chat responses with ties excluded, not for rubric scoring (Zheng et al., 2023, p. 7). The same study found a strong position bias: when two answers were presented in swapped order, "only GPT-4 outputs consistent results in more than 60% of cases" (p. 5). It is often cited as showing that models favour their own outputs; it does not, and its authors state that their study "cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias" (p. 5). It also found that human evaluators shown the model's judgment "deemed GPT-4's judgments reasonable in 75% of cases and are even willing to change their choices in 34% of cases" (p. 7), a finding about human deference that bears directly on how human and machine judgment should be combined.

Position bias can be severe. Wang et al. (2024) report that one model "could beat ChatGPT on 66 over 80 tested queries with ChatGPT as an evaluator" (abstract), with verdicts changing on swapped order in up to 82.5% of comparisons for one judge, and with the rate of such conflicts rising as the quality gap between the two answers narrows: judgments are least stable exactly where they are hardest. Self-preference, separately measured, exists: two models distinguished their own outputs from others' above chance, and after fine-tuning the strength of self-preference correlated linearly with that ability to recognize their own outputs (Panickssery et al., 2024, abstract and Fig. 1).

A panel of different models is one response. Verga et al. (2024) built a panel from "three models being drawn from three disparate model families" and found that it agreed with human judgments better than a single large model did on question-answering tasks (Cohen's kappa 0.763, 0.906 and 0.867 for the panel against 0.627, 0.841 and 0.830 for the single judge), and that the single large judge ranked a variant of itself higher than its actual position. The evidence is a preprint on tasks that are not educational. Chiang and Lee (2023) found that a model given the same instructions and samples used in a human evaluation produced ratings consistent with expert human ratings on story-generation tasks, while opening their abstract with the caution that "human evaluation is indispensable and inevitable for assessing the quality of texts."

#### 2.9.2 Language models scoring student work

The studies of language models scoring student work are small, recent and read here mostly at the abstract level. On short-answer questions in K–12 education, one model "with basic few-shot prompting performed well (Kappa, 0.70)," "very close to human-level performance (0.75)" (Henkel et al., 2024, abstract). On a new dataset of reading-comprehension answers, agreement with expert raters reached a quadratic-weighted kappa of 0.92 (Henkel et al., 2023, preprint abstract), the highest figure in this literature and on the most constrained items. Essay scoring with an earlier model showed "a certain level of accuracy and reliability" that "could provide valuable support for human evaluations" (Mizumoto & Eguchi, 2023, abstract). A study of handwritten physics solutions concluded that for summative, high-stakes use the tool "should only be used for an initial round of grading that sorts and flags solution approaches" (Kortemeyer, 2023, preprint abstract). A study of essays by English-language learners found that the best model showed "excellent intrarater reliability and good validity," but that interrater reliability "decreased slightly over time" and that "all models are subject to fluctuations in their performance" (Pack et al., 2024, abstract). Every one of these studies frames machine scoring as support for human judgment, triage, or formative feedback, not as a replacement for it in high-stakes decisions.

#### 2.9.3 The human baseline

Machine agreement must be read against human agreement, which is lower than commonly assumed. In the large public demonstration of automated essay scoring, "human quadratic-weighted kappas ranged from 0.61 to 0.85 and were closely followed by the machine ranges which went from 0.60 to 0.84" (Shermis, 2014). The same study warns that "a predictive model may do a good job of matching human scoring behavior, but for reasons unrelated (or unsatisfactorily related) to the construct of interest," and that scoring measures may be "subject to manipulation by test-takers and coaches." The widely used acceptance thresholds for automated scoring are aggregate ones: "a QWK or correlation of .70 or higher is commonly used as an acceptable level of concordance between the human and automated scores," together with a degradation from human–human agreement of less than .10 and a standardized mean difference of less than .15 (Williamson et al., 2012, as cited in Casabianca et al., 2023). They apply to a set of answers, not to any single answer.

Human marking of open answers has a long record of unreliability. A review of the literature on marking reliability found that "irrespective of the subject area, however, essay questions presented the greatest reliability problem," reporting a historical study in which agreement between pairs of markers of history scripts "ranged from -0.41 to 0.85 with an average of just 0.44," and recommended "removing the names from the essays," training markers and "the double marking of essays" (Meadows & Billington, 2005, pp. 18, 41). Neither human nor machine judgment is a gold standard, which argues for comparing them blind and reconciling disagreements on the evidence, rather than letting either override the other.

#### 2.9.4 Security and fairness of machine judgment

Language-model graders can be manipulated. In a study of injection strategies hidden in student work, "all the experiments show that the considered LLMs are vulnerable to every injection strategy that could be tested," and grading assignments in batches made all models more lenient regardless of injection (Milani et al., 2025, p. 11). In a deployment with more than a thousand students, "students can easily manipulate the LLM-based evaluator to output specific strings" and could obtain high scores without meeting the rubric (Chiang et al., 2024, abstract). A transcript of a student's speech is untrusted input to a grader, and a spoken answer can carry instructions addressed to it as easily as a written one.

One widely cited fairness result is often misattributed to graders. Liang et al. (2023) showed that seven widely used detectors of machine-written text "incorrectly labeled more than half of the TOEFL essays as 'AI-generated' (average false-positive rate: 61.3%)." The finding concerns detectors, not graders, and should not be cited as evidence that language-model graders penalize non-native writers. It is strong evidence that detectors of machine-written text should never be used as integrity signals.

### 2.10 Integrity and the signals a spoken examination can collect

#### 2.10.1 Proctoring

The evidence that unproctored online examinations are inflated is consistent. In one online course, students in proctored tests "scored, on average, 17 points lower [95% CI: 14, 20]" and "used significantly less time" than in unproctored tests (Alessio et al., 2017, abstract). In two courses, proctoring "was associated with a decrease in average performance," which the authors "interpret ... as evidence that cheating took place in the online courses prior to proctoring" (Dendir & Maxwell, 2020, abstract). Both inferences are from score changes; neither study observed cheating, and neither shows that any particular signal identifies a person who cheated.

Proctoring has its own costs. Coghlan et al. (2021) argue that "facial recognition functions in OP systems sometimes have more difficulty recognizing darker skin tones," that "some test-takers may simply have idiosyncratic exam-taking styles, or disabilities and impairments" that trigger flags, and that institutions should ask whether alternative types of assessment could be used instead. In one product at one university, students with darker skin and Black students were significantly more likely to be flagged for instructor review, and women with the darkest skin tones most of all, though the darkest-skin subgroup was small (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022). The study often cited for such disparities, Buolamwini and Gebru (2018), measured commercial gender classification, in which "darker-skinned females are the most misclassified group (with error rates of up to 34.7%)" while "the maximum error rate for lighter-skinned males is 0.8%"; it is evidence about facial analysis in general, not about proctoring. Among 631 students, higher trait test anxiety was associated with lower scores, especially for highly anxious students taking proctored online examinations (Woldeab & Brothen, 2019, abstract).

#### 2.10.2 Gaze

A spoken examination with a webcam can estimate where the student is looking. Webcam gaze estimation is coarse. The open-source tracker most often used reported an average error of 174.9 pixels in its best online configuration and an average visual angle of 4.17 degrees against a commercial eye tracker, measured with four participants (Papoutsaki et al., 2016). A second study found "an offset of about 172 px (15% of screen size, 3.94° visual angle)" in the laboratory, with online data "slightly less accurate (18% of screen size, 207 px)" and more variable (Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2018, abstract). Reading, by contrast, is a matter of fine detail: "the average fixation duration in reading is about 200-250 ms" and "the average saccade length (for alphabetic writing systems) is about 7-9 letter spaces" (Rayner, 2009). A tracker with an error of several degrees cannot resolve word-level reading. It cannot reliably tell reading notes from reading the question, looking away to think, or looking at nothing.

#### 2.10.3 Voice

Acoustic features of a spoken answer are sometimes offered as signs of stress or deception. The evidence gives them no support. The National Research Council found that "although proponents of voice stress analysis claim high levels of accuracy, empirical research on the validity of the technique has been far from encouraging," and that the research offered "little or no scientific basis for the use of the computer voice stress analyzer" (National Research Council, 2003, pp. 167–168). In a laboratory test of one commercial system, "the 'true positive' (or hit) rates for all examiners averaged near chance (42-56%)," with false positive rates of 40% to 65%, and the system "operated at about chance levels in the detection of truth, deception, and the presence of high and low vocal stress states" (Harnsberger et al., 2009, abstract). In a field test with 319 arrestees, "the programs were not able to detect deception at a rate any better than chance"; sensitivity averaged 15%, and the systems produced on average about nine false flags for every true one (Damphousse et al., 2007). Even the acoustic correlates of stress are inconsistent: "increase in fundamental frequency is the most commonly reported effect of stress in well-controlled trials. The trend, however, is not universal" (Giddens et al., 2013, abstract).

The wider literature on deception points in the same direction. A meta-analysis of 158 cues found that liars "are more tense" and "tell less compelling tales," but "many behaviors showed no discernible links, or only weak links, to deceit" (DePaulo et al., 2003, abstract). People judging truth and lies "achieve an average of 54% correct lie-truth judgments," though they are "more accurate in judging audible than visible lies" (Bond & DePaulo, 2006, abstract). The only cues with some support concern the content of what is said. That observation matters for the design of an examination: if anything distinguishes an answer the student cannot stand behind, it is more likely to be found in the content of the student's replies to follow-up questions than in the sound of the student's voice.

### 2.11 The regulatory position in the European Union

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act classifies as high-risk "AI systems intended to be used to evaluate learning outcomes, including when those outcomes are used to steer the learning process of natural persons in educational and vocational training institutions at all levels," and "AI systems intended to be used for monitoring and detecting prohibited behaviour of students during tests" (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Annex III, points 3(b) and 3(d)). High-risk systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons" (Art. 14(1)), and those overseeing them must remain aware of "the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output produced by a high-risk AI system (automation bias)" (Art. 14(4)(b)). A synthetic Socratic examiner used for grades in an EU institution falls under both points.

The Act also prohibits "the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons" (Art. 5(1)(f)). An emotion recognition system is one for "identifying or inferring emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data" (Art. 3(39)), and biometric data includes personal data relating to "behavioural characteristics" (Art. 3(34)). The Commission's guidelines, which are non-binding, read "emotions" "in a wide sense" (para. 247), list voice and eye tracking among behavioural biometrics (para. 251), and give an educational example directly on point: eye tracking used "to detect if unauthorized material is used ... is not prohibited, because the system does not identify or infer emotions. By contrast, if the system is also used to detect emotions, such as emotional arousal and anxiousness, this would fall within the scope of the prohibition" (European Commission, 2025, para. 255). They add that safety means "protection of life and health and not to protect other interests, for example property against theft or fraud" (para. 258), so integrity is not a safety reason. A system that labels acoustic features of a student's voice as "strain," and especially one that uses them to decide how hard to question the student, is exposed to that reading. The Act states that it applies from 2 August 2026 (Art. 113); whether later amendments have changed the dates for the high-risk rules is recorded in Appendix A as an open item.

### 2.12 Synthesis and gaps

The six literatures converge on a small number of findings that frame the rest of the dissertation.

1. **The elenchus tests coherence; the rubric must test correctness.** A contradiction between two of a student's own claims is evidence that the student's account does not hold together. It does not identify which claim is wrong, and only the student's own assertions may serve as premises (Section 2.2).
2. **Following the answer is an old and disciplined method, and the discipline is the point.** The clinical interview shows how to follow an answer without suggesting one, including by presupposition (Section 2.3).
3. **Explaining to a listener changes the explainer.** A Socratic examination is reactive. It slows people down and usually improves their performance, so late gains are not proof of prior knowledge (Section 2.3).
4. **Reliability comes from sampling.** More objectives and more independent judgments improve reliability more than a better examiner does; adaptive follow-up is compatible with comparable scores only if scoring is anchored to fixed objectives and rubrics (Section 2.4).
5. **The largest fairness risks are construct-irrelevant: anxiety and, above all, speech recognition** (Section 2.5).
6. **An examination can be a learning event,** but only with feedback between attempts and only for what was probed (Section 2.6).
7. **Understanding is shown by application to situations new to the student,** and an unaided examination measures sequestered transfer (Section 2.7).
8. **Help belongs in a separate mode.** If it is ever counted, it must be scripted and the same for everyone (Section 2.8).
9. **Machine judges have measurable biases, and so do humans.** Panels of different model families, rubric anchoring, blind human grading, and reconciliation rather than override are each supported; humans shown a machine verdict defer to it (Section 2.9).
10. **Integrity signals are weak.** Gaze is too coarse to resolve reading; voice-stress analysis performs at chance; detectors of machine text are unreliable and biased. Under EU law, inferring emotional states of students is prohibited (Sections 2.10 and 2.11).

Three gaps remain, and each shapes a later chapter.

- **No study of the central practice.** No source found in this review tests language models grading spoken, transcribed Socratic answers, multi-turn follow-ups, or per-objective scoring in an oral examination. The agreement figures of Section 2.9 describe neighbouring tasks. Chapter 7 designs the study that would supply the missing evidence.
- **No direct evidence that follow-up questioning defeats relayed answers.** The claim that a person relaying answers from another source will contradict themselves under questioning, which motivated Gadfly's design, has no support in the sources reviewed. The evidence on surveillance supports Socratic follow-up as an integrity control only indirectly, by showing that the alternatives are weak (Section 2.10). The claim is treated as a hypothesis in Chapters 5 and 7.
- **No evidence on some students.** No source on neurodivergent students in oral or voice examinations was verified. Chapter 6 records the gap.

The next chapter turns the first four findings into a formal model of the examination as evidence-gathering by questioning.

## Chapter 3 — A Formal Model of Evidence-Gathering by Questioning

### 3.1 The examination as sequential observation

Chapter 2 ended with four findings that bear on the design of any oral examination: the elenchus tests coherence and not truth, following an answer must not suggest one, explaining to a listener changes the explainer, and reliability comes from sampling. This chapter turns them into a model precise enough that a method can be checked against it. The model is deliberately simple. It is not offered as a psychometric model to be fitted to data, which would need the measurement-theory literature that this research pass has not yet located and verified (Appendix A, Section A.7). It is offered as a way of stating, without ambiguity, what an examiner is doing when it chooses a question, what a grader is doing when it reads the answers, and which features of a procedure let the path the conversation took, rather than the student, decide the grade. Every result in the chapter follows from the definitions given here and from the sources already reviewed in Chapter 2; none depends on a source that has not been read.

**Objectives and states.** An examination covers a fixed set of learning objectives $o_1, \dots, o_m$, chosen before it begins. For each objective $o_j$ the student has an unobserved state of understanding $\theta_j$. The model takes the state to lie on an ordered scale of levels, $\theta_j \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$, which Gadfly names Recognition, Basic Explanation, Conceptual Understanding, Application, and Transfer and Critical Reasoning (Chapter 4). The ordering is a reporting convention, not a claim about cognition. The revised taxonomy relaxed "the requirement of a strict hierarchy" (Krathwohl, 2002), and some processes ranked lower are more demanding than some ranked higher, so the model uses the order only to say that evidence for a higher level is evidence of more than evidence for a lower one, on the objective as the instructor defined it.

**Insufficient evidence is a verdict, not a state.** The report for an objective is a value $\hat{\ell}_j \in \{0, 1, \dots, 5\}$. The value 0 is not a level of understanding. It is the verdict that the examination did not gather enough reliable evidence to state a level. Keeping it out of the state space is the first design decision of the model, and much of what follows depends on it: a student about whom the examination learned too little is not the same as a student who understands little, and a procedure that reports both as a low score has confused the examination's failure with the student's.

**Questions and answers.** A question is a triple $q = (j, \mu, w)$: the objective it serves, the move it makes (one of the moves listed in Section 1.2), and its wording. The student's answer is an utterance $u$. What the examination records, and what every judge reads, is not $u$ but a transcript $a$ produced from it by a speech recognizer and, where needed, corrected by the student or a reviewer. The model therefore has two channels between the state and the evidence: the response channel $P(u \mid \theta, q)$, which carries information about understanding, and the recording channel $P(a \mid u)$, which should carry none and which, for some speakers, adds error that Section 2.5 showed to be systematic (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024).

**Histories, the examiner and the grader.** After $t$ questions the examination has a history $h_t = (q_1, a_1, \dots, q_t, a_t)$. Two functions act on it. The examiner's policy $\pi$ maps a history to the next question or to a decision to stop. The grader $\gamma$ maps a finished history to a report $\hat{\ell}_1, \dots, \hat{\ell}_m$ for each objective. The two have different jobs and different standards of success. A good examiner policy makes the history informative; a good grader reads the history correctly. An examiner may use any information it likes to choose the next question, including, as Chapter 4 shows Gadfly does, how the last answer was delivered. A grader may use only what bears on understanding. The chapter keeps the two apart throughout, because most of the ways an adaptive examination can go wrong are cases in which something legitimately used by the examiner leaks into what the grader reports.

**A simple response model.** To make the argument concrete, the model gives each move a probed level $\lambda(\mu)$: the level that an adequate answer to that move is evidence for. A question at level $\lambda$ is answered adequately with probability $s$ by a student whose state is at or above $\lambda$, and with probability $g$ by a student below it:

$$
P(\text{adequate} \mid \theta, q) = \begin{cases} s & \text{if } \theta \ge \lambda(\mu), \\ g & \text{if } \theta < \lambda(\mu). \end{cases}
$$

The parameter $s < 1$ allows for slips, for anxiety, for the recording channel and for a judge who misreads a good answer. The parameter $g > 0$ is the probability that a student without the understanding nonetheless produces an answer judged adequate: by guessing, by reciting a memorized form of words, by relaying an answer from another source, or because the question itself contained the answer. The quantity that matters for everything that follows is the gap between $s$ and $g$. A question whose adequate answer is nearly as likely without the understanding as with it carries almost no information, however hard it sounds.

### 3.2 What a question is for

A question is valuable to the extent that its answer is expected to reduce uncertainty about the student's state on the objective it serves. Writing $H(\theta_j \mid h)$ for the uncertainty (entropy) of the examiner's belief about $\theta_j$ after history $h$, the expected value of asking $q$ is its expected information gain:

$$
\mathrm{EIG}(q \mid h) = H(\theta_j \mid h) - \mathbb{E}_{a \sim P(a \mid h, q)} \big[ H(\theta_j \mid h, a) \big].
$$

Under the response model of Section 3.1, a question at level $\lambda$ divides the hypotheses about $\theta_j$ into two groups, those at or above $\lambda$ and those below it, and an answer shifts belief between the groups by the likelihood ratio $s/g$ (if adequate) or $(1-s)/(1-g)$ (if not). Three consequences follow directly.

1. **Ask where the uncertainty is.** A question is most informative when the examiner's belief is split evenly between the two groups it separates. If the examiner is confident that the student is at level 3 or above, another level-2 question tells it almost nothing. The best next question probes the level between what has been established and what is still plausible, so that, in the simplest case, the examination performs a bisection over the ladder. A fixed questionnaire cannot do this; it asks the same levels of everyone, which wastes questions on students whose level is already clear and leaves others unresolved.
2. **A question that can be answered without the understanding is worth little.** As $g$ approaches $s$, both likelihood ratios approach 1 and the information gain approaches zero. Anything that raises $g$ (a question that states or presupposes its answer, a question the student could have prepared for, an answer recited from the source) destroys the value of the question, not merely of the answer. This is the formal content of Piaget's rule against suggestion (Section 2.3) and of the warning that a question containing its answer measures nothing (Section 2.2.2).
3. **Repetition is worth less than it appears.** The likelihood ratios multiply only if answers are independent given the state. Answers in a conversation are not: a follow-up is built from the previous answer, and a student who has just explained a point can restate it. A second question of the same kind on the same point adds less than the first did. This is why a procedure should prefer a different move over a repeated one, and why, in Section 3.5, the minimum number of supporting answers is a floor and not a sufficient condition.

**The moves differ in what they reduce uncertainty about.** The Socratic moves of Section 1.2 are not interchangeable ways of asking harder questions. They reduce uncertainty about different things, and the model sorts them accordingly.

- **Moves that probe the state.** Elicit ("what is it, in your own words?") probes a basic explanation. Probe ("why does that follow?") probes whether the student holds the principle behind a claim, which is the evidence for understanding that the self-explanation literature associates with learners who understand (Chi et al., 1989). Counterexample tests whether an explanation survives a case it handles wrongly; it is Piaget's counter-suggestion, and a conviction that survives it is better evidence than one stated once (Piaget, 1929). Apply asks for the concept in a situation new to the student, which is the defining mark of application (Bloom et al., 1956). Synthesize asks how two objectives connect. Each has a probed level, and each answer moves belief about $\theta_j$.
- **Extrapolation is not application.** A "what would change if" question set inside the scenario the student has already described tests extrapolation, which Bloom et al. (1956) place under Comprehension, "based on what is given rather than on some abstraction brought from the other experiences to the situation." In the model its probed level is below that of Apply. A procedure that counts a within-scenario what-if as evidence of transfer overstates the level its answer supports (Appendix A, correction 15).
- **Clarify reduces uncertainty about the observation, not the state.** A clarifying question asks what the student meant. Its answer does not add a second observation of $\theta_j$; it sharpens the first. In the model, the clarified answer replaces the answer it clarifies, and the pair counts once. Counting a clarification as a separate answer would let an unclear student accumulate evidence by being asked what they meant.
- **Contradiction reduces uncertainty about coherence.** Confronting a student with two of their own claims that conflict does not test a level on the ladder. It tests a different quantity, the coherence of the student's account of the objective, written $\kappa_j$ below. Section 3.4 treats it separately, because what a contradiction shows is different in kind from what an adequate answer shows.
- **Stopping is a decision, not a question.** Close, the move that ends a concept, asks nothing. In the model it is the examiner's decision to stop gathering evidence on an objective, governed by the rules of Section 3.5.

**Relation to measurement theory.** The model resembles, in its simplest form, the item-level models of measurement theory, and its question-selection rule resembles adaptive testing. De Beer (2006) proposes item response theory and computerized adaptive testing as remedies for the measurement problems of dynamic testing, and the resemblance suggests the same remedies here. The measurement-theory sources themselves were not located in this research pass, so the chapter states the model from its definitions and makes no claim about how it relates to that literature, beyond noting that the relation should be established before the model is fitted to data (Appendix A, Section A.7).

### 3.3 Why questions written from the student's own words are informative

The case for Socratic follow-up as a defence of the examination's integrity rests on a simple observation that the model makes exact. A follow-up question written from the student's previous answer is conditioned on information that did not exist until the student gave that answer.

**Prepared answers.** Suppose a student has, before the examination, access to a resource $R$ (notes, a tutor, a language model) that returns an adequate answer $R(q)$ to any question $q$ known in advance. For a fixed set of questions $Q_0$ known before the examination, the student can prepare an answer to each, and for those questions $g \approx s$: an adequate answer is about equally likely whatever the student understands. Prepared questions carry almost no information about a prepared student. A follow-up $q_{t+1} = f(h_t)$, by contrast, depends on the answer $a_t$, which did not exist before the examination. It cannot have been in $Q_0$ and cannot have been prepared, unless the student can predict both their own earlier answer and the examiner's function $f$.

**The condition for discrimination.** That a follow-up cannot be prepared does not make it discriminating. A student who has memorized an explanation can often extend it by paraphrase, and a follow-up that asks only for more of the same can be answered from the wording of the earlier answer. The model states the condition under which a follow-up discriminates between a student who understands and one who is reproducing an answer obtained elsewhere: **an adequate answer to the follow-up must require the principle behind the earlier answer, not its wording.** In terms of the response model, the follow-up must have a low $g$ for a student who holds the earlier answer's words but not its principle. "Why does that follow?", a counterexample to the student's own rule, and an application to a situation new to the student satisfy the condition when they are well made. "Can you say more about that?" does not.

**Live relay is a different case.** The argument so far concerns answers prepared in advance. A student who can consult a resource during the examination, by reading a screen, hearing a helper or querying a model, faces no such barrier: the resource can be asked the follow-up as soon as it is posed. Nothing in the logic of follow-up questioning prevents a live relay from answering every question adequately. Follow-up defeats live relay only if relaying each new question is too slow, too conspicuous, or too hard to keep consistent across a conversation. The last of these is the claim, often repeated, that a person relaying another source's answers will contradict themselves under questioning. Chapter 2 found no verified support for it (Section 2.12; Appendix A, correction 6). The model therefore states it as a hypothesis, H1, for the empirical test of Chapter 7:

> **H1.** Under Socratic follow-up, answers relayed live from another source are less coherent across turns (more contradictions in the ledger, more failed resolutions) than answers produced by students who understand the material, at equal quality of the individual answers.
>
The distinction matters for what the method can claim. It can claim, from the model alone, that follow-up questions defeat prepared answers. It cannot claim, from the model or from any verified source, that they defeat live relay.

**Leaks and presupposition.** The same analysis shows why the non-hinting rule is not a nicety. A follow-up that states an expected point of the answer raises $g$ to nearly $s$ for that point, and so does a question that presupposes it: "What makes the reaction speed up?" presupposes that something does, and supplies the student with the structure of the answer (Section 2.3.1). Once a question has leaked, every later answer on the objective is partly the examiner's work, and the model treats those answers as contaminated: they may be recorded, but they are not independent evidence about $\theta_j$. A replacement question can still gather evidence on what the leak did not give away, for instance whether the student can apply the leaked point, but not on the leaked point itself.

**The state is not fixed.** One assumption of Section 3.1 must be relaxed here. The model treats $\theta_j$ as the student's state, but explaining to a listener changes the explainer: procedures that require describing or explaining thoughts "are significantly reactive, leading to higher performance than silent control conditions" (Fox et al., 2011, abstract). A Socratic examination is such a procedure. The student's state can rise during the examination, and an answer late in a session may show understanding the student did not bring to it. The model therefore reads $\theta_j$ as the state at the time of the answer, and treats improvement during a session as an expected property of the format rather than as suspicious, or as proof of prior knowledge.

### 3.4 The ledger and the logic of contradiction

**The ledger.** The claim ledger after $t$ turns is the set $\Lambda_t$ of propositions the student has asserted as their own. Three kinds of utterance are excluded from it. Assent to a proposition the examiner supplied is not assertion: a student who says "yes" to "so the rate depends on temperature?" has not asserted that it does. A claim the student hedged and then withdrew is not asserted. A restatement of the examiner's question is not a claim at all. The ledger must also record retractions: a student who says "I was wrong earlier" has removed a proposition from $\Lambda_t$, and a ledger that keeps it has misrepresented the student.

**Contradictions.** A contradiction is a subset $C \subseteq \Lambda_t$ that is inconsistent, either by itself or together with background facts that the student has also accepted. Call it minimal if removing any one member makes the rest consistent.

**Proposition 3.1 (a contradiction does not identify the false claim).** *If $C$ is a minimal inconsistent subset of the ledger, then for every $p \in C$ the set $C \setminus \{p\}$ is consistent. The inconsistency of $C$ is therefore symmetric among its members: nothing in $C$ itself determines which member is false.*

The proof is the definition of minimality. The proposition is Vlastos's problem of the elenchus stated for an examination: the refutation shows only that a thesis "is inconsistent with the conjunction of agreed-upon premises" (Vlastos, 1983, p. 49, as cited in Wolfsdorf, 2013), and on the reading Chapter 2 found best supported, the elenchus establishes only that the interlocutor's beliefs are inconsistent (Benson, 1995, as summarized in Wolfsdorf, 2013). To decide which member of $C$ should be given up, the examination needs a standard outside the ledger. In a grounded examination that standard is the instructor's source material, and the rubric built from it. The contradiction itself decides nothing about correctness.

**Proposition 3.2 (only asserted claims may serve as premises).** *If a set $C$ that the examiner presents as a contradiction contains a proposition the examiner supplied and the student merely accepted, the inconsistency may have been produced by the examiner, and it is not evidence about the coherence of the student's own account.*

Again the argument is immediate: an examiner who may introduce premises can make any account look inconsistent. The proposition is the examination's version of Vlastos's requirement that a thesis be debated "only if asserted as the answerer's own belief" (Vlastos, 1983, p. 30, as cited in Wolfsdorf, 2013), and it is why the ledger excludes assent.

**What a contradiction shows.** A contradiction in the ledger is evidence about the coherence $\kappa_j$ of the student's account of an objective. It is weaker evidence than it looks, because an apparent contradiction has several causes, and only one of them is an incoherent understanding:

1. **Incoherent understanding.** The student holds both claims and has not noticed that they conflict.
2. **Recording error.** The transcript $a$ differs from what the student said $u$, so that one of the "claims" was never made (Section 2.5.2).
3. **Extraction error.** The claim was restated, when it entered the ledger, in words that sharpen it beyond what the student meant.
4. **Ambiguity.** The two claims use a term in two senses, and in the senses the student meant them both can be true.
5. **Refinement.** The later claim qualifies the earlier one, and the student would accept the qualified version of both.
6. **Learning.** The student's state changed between the two claims, as Section 3.3 showed it can, and the later claim is the student's corrected view.

These causes predict different responses when the contradiction is put to the student. A student whose understanding is incoherent cannot resolve it, or resolves it arbitrarily. A student whose claim was misrecorded or misread says so. A student who refined or changed their view explains which claim they now hold and why. **The evidence is therefore in the resolution, not in the occurrence.** A contradiction that is detected but not put to the student is an unconfirmed observation about $\kappa_j$; one that is put to the student and well resolved is evidence for coherence, and may be evidence of learning.

**Coherence is not correctness.** A resolution has two properties that the model keeps apart. It can be coherent or not: the student either gives a consistent account or does not. And it can be correct or not: the claim the student keeps can agree with the source or contradict it. A student who resolves a contradiction by keeping the claim the source contradicts, and explains the choice coherently, has shown coherence and an error of fact. A procedure that scores that resolution as a failure to reconcile has counted an accuracy finding as a coherence finding. By Proposition 3.1 a resolution in either direction is a legitimate resolution of the inconsistency; the rubric, not the contradiction, scores whether the direction was right.

**What a contradiction does not show.** It does not show which claim is false (Proposition 3.1). It does not by itself show a lack of understanding (causes 2 to 6). And it does not show dishonesty: the claim that relayed answers contradict themselves is hypothesis H1, not an established fact.

**Detection is fallible.** In a working system, contradictions are found by a detector, not given. Let the detector flag a non-contradictory pair with probability $\alpha_c$ and miss a real contradiction with probability $\beta_c$. A flagged contradiction is then evidence about $\kappa_j$ only to the extent that $\alpha_c$ is small, and the absence of a flag is evidence of coherence only to the extent that $\beta_c$ is small. A detector that compares only some pairs of claims (for instance, only claims about the same concept that share enough words) has a large $\beta_c$ by construction, and its silence should be reported as "no contradiction detected," not as "consistent."

### 3.5 Stopping rules

An examination must decide, for each objective, when it has gathered enough evidence to stop. The decision has two parts: when an objective may be reported at a level, and what to report when it cannot be.

**Independent evidence.** Let $E_j$ be the set of answers on objective $o_j$ that count as independent evidence: each judged, each made without help, each given before any leak on the objective (or after it only for what the leak did not reveal), and each counted once with any clarification of it. An answer in $E_j$ is positive at its probed level $\lambda$ if judged adequate, and negative if judged inadequate.

**Sufficiency.** The model states four conditions under which an objective may be reported at level $\ell$:

- **S1 (corroboration).** At least $k \ge 2$ answers in $E_j$ are positive at level $\ell$ or above. By the response model, a single positive answer multiplies the odds that $\theta_j \ge \ell$ by at most $s/g$; with $k$ conditionally independent positives the factor is $(s/g)^k$. One answer never establishes a level, because a guess, a recitation or a lucky phrasing can produce one adequate answer, and $k = 2$ is the least that makes such an accident visible. Because answers in a conversation are not fully independent (Section 3.2), $k$ is a floor.
- **S2 (ceiling tested).** Either $\ell$ is the top of the scale, or the next level up has been probed and failed, enough times that a single slip at that level does not set the ceiling. Without S2 the report is a lower bound, not a level.
- **S3 (nothing pending).** No detected contradiction on the objective is waiting to be put to the student, and no possible misconception has gone untested. An objective with an open question about coherence or a misconception is not settled.
- **S4 (enough answers in all).** $|E_j| \ge k$, so that an objective cannot be settled on fewer independent answers than the corroboration rule requires.

If all four hold, the objective is reported at the highest such $\ell$. If any fails when the objective must close (its question budget is spent, the session's time is up, or the examiner moves on to protect the remaining objectives), the report is 0: insufficient evidence. The rule makes the distinction of Section 3.1 operational. An honest "I don't know" is evidence, and it counts as a negative answer at the level it was asked; running out of time is not evidence, and it produces a verdict of insufficient evidence rather than a low level.

**Proposition 3.3 (stopping on a running estimate biases the report).** *Suppose an examiner keeps a running estimate of $\theta_j$, updated after each answer, and stops the objective as soon as the estimate crosses a fixed mastery threshold, reporting mastery. Then a student whose state is just below the threshold is reported as having reached it with positive probability, because a run of adequate answers, each possible with probability $g$ at levels above the student's state, can carry the estimate across the threshold, and once it is across, the procedure never asks the question that would have brought it back.*

The argument is the familiar one against optional stopping: a rule that stops on favourable evidence and continues on unfavourable evidence samples favourable runs preferentially. The symmetric rule, stopping as soon as the estimate falls below a low threshold, biases reports downward for students who start badly, which in a spoken examination includes anxious students whose first answers are their weakest (Section 2.5.1). The conditions S1 to S4 avoid the bias because they require corroboration and a tested ceiling before any level is stated, whatever the running estimate says. A running estimate may still be useful to the examiner, to choose the next question; Proposition 3.3 concerns only its use as the grader's stopping rule.

**Allocating the budget across objectives.** An examination has finite time. If the examiner spends it on the objectives it reaches first, the later objectives close for lack of time and are reported as insufficient evidence, and the report depends on the order of the objectives rather than on the student. The model therefore requires the examiner to reserve, for every objective not yet started, at least enough capacity for $k$ answers, and to leave an objective inconclusive, explicitly, rather than consume that reserve. An inconclusive objective is reported as such, never as zero understanding.

**The same rule for everyone.** The stopping rule must be the same for every student. If the conditions for stopping differ between students (for instance, if some students are allowed more questions on an objective because their delivery seemed uncertain), then the probability of reaching a level differs between students of the same state, which is the problem of the next section.

### 3.6 Adaptive paths and comparable scores

This section states the chapter's central result. Adaptive follow-up is the method's main strength and the main threat to its reliability, because no two students receive the same questions (Joughin, 2010; Section 2.4.3). The question is under what conditions scores from different paths can be compared.

**Paths.** The path of an examination is the sequence of moves, wordings and objectives that the examiner chose. It depends on the student's state, which is why it is informative. It also depends on things that are not the student's state: the examiner's random choices, how the student's earlier answers happened to be worded, errors of the online judge that steers the conversation, and, in a system that uses them, signals about how the answers were delivered. Write $\rho$ for the features of the path that do not depend on $\theta$.

**Comparability.** Scores are comparable across paths if the expected score of a student depends on the student's state and not on $\rho$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\ell}_j \mid \theta_j, \rho] = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\ell}_j \mid \theta_j] \quad \text{for all } \rho.
$$

**Proposition 3.4 (conditions for comparable scores).** *Scores from adaptive paths are comparable only if (a) every objective is scored against the same rubric, whatever path was taken; (b) the score depends on the evidence about the objective and not on the length or difficulty of the path that produced it; and (c) the number of follow-ups each student received, and what they probed, is reported rather than silently absorbed into the score.*

The proposition is best understood through what goes wrong when each condition fails.

**Violating (a): a different standard for different paths.** If the rubric applied depends on the path (because the judge is shown different rubric points for different moves, or because the instructor's rubric was rebuilt between students), two students with the same state are judged against different standards, and the score measures the standard as well as the student. Condition (a) is also why every objective should open with the same move, and ideally the same question: the opening answer is the one observation that every student's evidence has in common.

**Violating (b): the score depends on the path.** Two aggregation rules that look natural both fail.

- *Best answer counts.* If the objective's score is the best of its judged answers, then a longer path gives more draws, and since each answer below the student's level has probability $g$ of being judged adequate at a higher level, the expected maximum rises with the number of questions. Students who were asked more questions score higher at the same state.
- *Mean judged score.* If the score is the mean of the judged answers, then a harder path lowers it. Under the response model, a student at level 3 answers level-3 questions adequately with probability $s$ and level-5 questions with probability $g$; a student pressed with more level-5 questions has a lower mean at the same state. Students who were probed harder score lower.

The second failure is the more dangerous, because a system can press some students harder for reasons unrelated to their understanding. If the examiner presses harder after slow or hesitant answers, and slowness and hesitation vary with anxiety, accent, second-language speaking and stuttering (Sections 2.3.2 and 2.5), then the students pressed harder are disproportionately those groups. Under a mean-score rule their scores fall, and a path feature that the examiner was entitled to use becomes a score gap between groups. The examiner may press harder; the grader must not let the pressing count against the student.

A rule that satisfies (b) reports a level from the evidence, as in Section 3.5: the corroborated level, with its ceiling tested. Under that rule a harder question that is failed above the established level tests the ceiling, which is what the level means, and does not lower the level established below it. Longer paths are not fully neutral even then, because more questions above the student's level are more chances for $k$ chance positives; the probability of that accident is small when $g$ is small and $k \ge 2$, and the residual effect is the reason for condition (c) and for a cap on the questions per objective.

A subtler violation of (b) concerns coverage. If an objective is scored on several criteria, and criteria for which the path gathered no evidence are left out of the score and the rest reweighted, then a path that never reached the harder criteria is scored only on the easier ones. Leaving such criteria out is right, since no evidence is not evidence of no understanding, but the coverage must then be reported, or the score of a short path and a long one are not on the same footing.

**Violating (c): the path is hidden.** Even a well-designed aggregation rule leaves some dependence on the path, and the reader of a score cannot judge it without seeing the path. Reporting, for each objective, the number of questions asked, the moves used, the criteria reached and the answers excluded (as leaked, recited or assisted) lets a reviewer see when two scores rest on very different evidence. Hiding it turns path differences into score differences that no one can inspect.

**The separation of examiner and grader, restated.** Proposition 3.4 is the formal form of the separation drawn in Section 3.1. The examiner may adapt to anything; the grader may respond only to evidence about the objective. Every condition of the proposition is a way of preventing the examiner's adaptivity from reaching the grade.

### 3.7 Sampling and reliability

The model so far concerns one objective. A grade concerns many, and Chapter 2 found that reliability in oral examination comes mostly from sampling: "one measure is no measure" (van der Vleuten et al., 2010), and in the membership examination of the Royal College of General Practitioners the content sampled accounted for more score variance than the examiners did (Wass et al., 2003). The model can show why, and what it implies for a synthetic examiner.

**A decomposition.** Write the score of student $p$ on objective $j$, as judged by judge $r$, as

$$
X_{pjr} = \mu_p + \nu_{pj} + \varepsilon_{pjr},
$$

where $\mu_p$ is the student's general standing on the subject, $\nu_{pj}$ is the student's particular strength or weakness on objective $j$ (the content specificity behind the argument of van der Vleuten et al., 2010, that competence is specific rather than generic), and $\varepsilon_{pjr}$ is the error of the judgment. Averaging over $m$ objectives, each judged by $r$ independent judges, gives a total whose reliability, as an estimate of $\mu_p$, has the form

$$
\rho(m, r) = \frac{\sigma^2_{\mu}}{\sigma^2_{\mu} + \sigma^2_{\nu}/m + \sigma^2_{\varepsilon}/(m\,r)}.
$$

This is the form of a generalizability coefficient. The literature behind that form was not among the sources verified for this dissertation, so it is used here only as the algebra of averaging independent errors, and its standard treatment remains to be cited (Appendix A, Section A.7).

**What the form shows.** Improving the judges can only shrink the last term. The content term $\sigma^2_{\nu}/m$ falls only as more objectives are sampled. When content specificity is large compared with judge error, as Wass et al. (2003) found for human examiners, more objectives improve reliability more than better or more numerous judges.

An illustration makes the point, with a caution. Wass et al. (2003) attributed 41% of the variance in oral scores to candidates, 32% to the content of the orals and 27% to examiners and general error. Their components were estimated for whole orals of five topics, scored by human examiners, so mapping them onto $\sigma^2_\mu$, $\sigma^2_\nu$ and $\sigma^2_\varepsilon$ for single objectives is an approximation that serves only to show the shape of the trade-off. On that mapping, one objective with one judge gives $\rho = 0.41$. Doubling the judges gives $0.41 / (0.41 + 0.32 + 0.135) \approx 0.47$; doubling the objectives with one judge gives $0.41 / (0.41 + 0.16 + 0.135) \approx 0.58$; five objectives give about 0.78 and ten about 0.87. The numbers are not predictions for any synthetic examiner. The shape is the result: the judge is the cheaper thing to improve and the less effective one.

**A minimum number of objectives.** For a target reliability $\rho^*$, the minimum number of objectives is the smallest $m$ with $\rho(m, r) \ge \rho^*$. The variance components a synthetic examiner needs are not in any source reviewed here; they can come only from a double-marked pilot of the system itself (Chapter 7). Until they are estimated, the model gives a rule of procedure rather than a number: an examination that samples fewer objectives than the pilot shows are needed should be reported as formative, and its per-objective levels should never be used alone for a high-stakes decision. Each per-objective level is itself a single-point measure in van der Vleuten's sense, and it is more reliable as one entry in a profile than as a verdict on its own.

### 3.8 Combining judges

A synthetic examination has two kinds of judge: the language models of the panel, and the human examiner. The model treats both as noisy raters of the same evidence and asks how their judgments should be combined.

**Panel members.** Let panel member $i$ belong to model family $F(i)$, and let its judgment of an objective be

$$
Y_i = \theta + b_{F(i)} + e_i,
$$

where $b_F$ is a bias shared by models of the same family (for instance, a tendency to reward length, or a preference for some phrasings) and $e_i$ is the member's own error. The mean of $r$ judgments has error $\bar{b} + \bar{e}$. Averaging shrinks $\bar{e}$ as $r$ grows, but if every member comes from the same family, $\bar{b} = b_F$ and averaging does nothing to it. Drawing members from different families makes the biases partly independent, so that they too average down. This is the formal reason for a panel of "three models being drawn from three disparate model families," which agreed with human judgments better than a single large model did (Verga et al., 2024). Two biases are of particular concern. Position bias, documented for judges comparing two answers presented in a given order (Wang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023), does not arise when each answer is judged on its own, which is one reason the method prefers single-answer scoring to pairwise comparison; whether it returns when a judge reads several answers to the same objective in a fixed order is untested, and the model treats a fixed order as a possible source of correlated bias. Self-preference, which correlated with a model's ability to recognize its own outputs (Panickssery et al., 2024), may arise when the family that wrote the questions also judges the answers; the model marks this as a possible correlated bias, untested for examinations.

**The human examiner.** Let the human's judgment be $H = \theta + b_H + e_H$. Neither the panel nor the human is a gold standard: human agreement on open answers ranged from 0.61 to 0.85 in quadratic-weighted kappa in the largest public comparison (Shermis, 2014), and much lower in older studies of essay marking (Meadows & Billington, 2005).

**Anchoring.** If the human sees the panel's verdict before judging, the human's judgment becomes $H' = (1 - w) H + w \bar{Y}$ for some $w > 0$. Zheng et al. (2023) found that human evaluators shown a model's judgment were "willing to change their choices in 34% of cases" (p. 7). Anchoring has three consequences in the model. The combined score counts the panel twice. The errors of the two judgments become correlated, so their agreement overstates their accuracy. And the disagreement $|\bar{Y} - H'| = (1 - w)\,|\bar{Y} - H|$ shrinks, so a rule that triggers review when disagreement is large is triggered less often exactly when it is needed. Blind human judgment is therefore not a courtesy to the examiner; it is what makes the disagreement between the two judgments measurable.

**The combination rule.** A combined score $C = \alpha \bar{Y} + (1 - \alpha) H$, with $\alpha$ fixed before the examination, uses both judgments. Fixing $\alpha$ in advance matters for the same reason as the stopping rule: a weight chosen after the scores are seen can be chosen to produce a desired grade. Setting $\alpha = 1$ makes the panel the grader, which the evidence on machine scoring does not support for high-stakes decisions (Section 2.9.2), and which the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act's requirement of effective human oversight, with awareness of "automation bias" (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 14(4)(b)), excludes for systems that evaluate learning outcomes. Setting $\alpha = 0$ discards the panel.

**Reconciliation.** Averaging hides a large disagreement. If $D = |\bar{Y} - H|$ exceeds a threshold $\tau$, at least one of the two judgments is probably far from the student's state, and the average of a far-off judgment and a sound one is not sound. The model therefore requires that disagreements beyond $\tau$ be resolved on the evidence, not by formula, and not by either source overriding the other. Resolution by the human who gave $H$ amounts to an override by the human, since that person will tend to re-assert their judgment; resolution by accepting $\bar{Y}$ is an override by the panel. The model requires a reviewer independent of both judgments, who reads the evidence and records why the two differed.

**Aggregate thresholds and single answers.** The widely used acceptance thresholds for automated scoring, a quadratic-weighted kappa or correlation of .70 or more, a degradation from human–human agreement of less than .10, and a standardized mean difference of less than .15 (Williamson et al., 2012, as cited in Casabianca et al., 2023), describe agreement over a set of answers (Appendix A, correction 21). In the model they are used to choose $\alpha$ and $\tau$ for an examination from pilot data, and to decide whether the panel may be used at all. They say nothing about whether a particular student's two scores agree; that is what $\tau$ and reconciliation are for.

**Online and offline judgment.** A synthetic examiner uses machine judgment twice: during the examination, to judge each answer so that the examiner can choose the next question, and afterwards, to grade each objective. The two uses have different standards. An error in the online judgment changes the path, which conditions (a) to (c) of Proposition 3.4 are designed to tolerate. An error in the offline grade changes the score. If the offline grader is shown the online judgment, the two errors become correlated, by the same mechanism as anchoring. The model therefore treats the online judgment as part of the examiner, not of the grader.

### 3.9 What the model leaves out

The model is deliberately incomplete, and its omissions mark where Chapters 5 to 7 must take over.

- **The recording channel is treated only as noise.** Section 3.1 separated the utterance $u$ from the transcript $a$, but the model does not represent the fact that the error $P(a \mid u)$ differs between groups of speakers (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024). Because the judges read $a$, a recognizer error is a change in the evidence that falls systematically on identifiable groups. Chapter 5 treats it as a source of construct-irrelevant variance.
- **The parameters $s$ and $g$ are treated as the same for everyone.** In practice $s$ may fall with anxiety, which is higher where tests are perceived as difficult or carry high stakes (von der Embse et al., 2018) and to which a spoken examination adds speaking anxiety (Section 2.5.1); $s$ also falls for speakers the recognizer serves badly. A model fitted to data would need group-specific parameters, and a difference in $s$ between groups at the same state is exactly the kind of unfairness Chapter 5 must measure.
- **Understanding is treated as one scale per objective.** Real understanding has several dimensions (knowing the facts, explaining the relationships, applying them, recognizing misconceptions), and a single ordered scale is a reporting convenience. Gadfly records evidence by dimension as well as by level (Chapter 4), and the model's single scale should be read as a summary of that richer record.
- **The student's state changes.** Section 3.3 noted that a Socratic examination is reactive. The model reads each answer as evidence about the state at the time it was given, but it does not say how the report should weigh early answers against late ones. That is left as a question for the empirical test.
- **The examiner's own conduct is not modelled.** The model treats questions as experiments chosen by a policy. It does not model the effect on the student of how they are asked. The *Apology* records that Socratic examination made those examined hostile to the examiner (Plato, 1966, 21d), and a confrontational examiner may lower $s$ for anxious students. Chapter 5 returns to this.

### 3.10 Summary of the model's requirements

The model yields the following requirements. Chapter 4 checks the Gadfly method as built against each, and its requirements table (Section 4.11) uses these numbers.

- **M1. Separate the examiner from the grader.** The examiner may adapt to anything; the grade may respond only to evidence about the objective (Sections 3.1, 3.6).
- **M2. A common opening and a common rubric.** Every objective opens with the same move, ideally the same question, and is scored against the same rubric whatever path follows (Proposition 3.4(a)).
- **M3. No question may contain or presuppose its answer.** Answers given after a question that did are contaminated and are not independent evidence on what was revealed (Sections 3.2, 3.3).
- **M4. A clarification refines an answer; it is not a second answer** (Section 3.2).
- **M5. Transfer only from situations new to the student.** A what-if inside the student's own scenario is evidence of extrapolation, not application (Section 3.2).
- **M6. Only asserted claims enter the ledger,** and retractions are recorded (Section 3.4, Proposition 3.2).
- **M7. A contradiction is evidence about coherence, and its resolution is the evidence.** It is put to the student before it counts; it does not decide which claim is false; and a coherent resolution toward the claim the source contradicts is scored as an error of accuracy, not as a failure of coherence. A detector's silence is reported as "no contradiction detected" (Section 3.4, Proposition 3.1).
- **M8. No level on one answer.** A level is stated only with corroboration ($k \ge 2$), a tested ceiling and nothing pending; otherwise the verdict is insufficient evidence, reported as distinct from low understanding (Section 3.5).
- **M9. A stopping rule that is the same for everyone and does not stop on a running estimate,** with capacity reserved for every objective not yet started (Section 3.5, Proposition 3.3).
- **M10. Path-invariant scoring.** No best-answer or mean-score aggregation; the level comes from the corroborated evidence; the number of questions, the moves, the criteria reached and the exclusions are reported for each objective (Proposition 3.4(b), (c)).
- **M11. Enough objectives.** An examination that samples fewer objectives than the pilot shows are needed for the target reliability is reported as formative (Section 3.7).
- **M12. Judges from different families, judging single answers, with no fixed ordering of evidence;** the online judgment kept apart from the offline grade (Section 3.8).
- **M13. Blind human judgment, a combination weight fixed in advance, and reconciliation of large disagreements on the evidence by a reviewer independent of both judgments;** aggregate agreement thresholds applied to sets of answers, not to single answers (Section 3.8).
- **M14. A verified recording channel.** Transcripts are confirmed or corrected before they are graded, and uncertain recognition is flagged rather than scored as wrong content (Sections 3.1, 3.9).
- **M15. Path drivers that are not evidence must not reach the score.** Any signal the examiner uses to choose questions that varies with anxiety, accent or language background must be unable to change the score through the path (a consequence of M10), and its effect on paths should be measured by group (Section 3.6).

## Chapter 4 — The Gadfly Method

### 4.1 From model to method

Chapter 3 stated a model of examination as sequential evidence-gathering and drew from it fifteen requirements, M1 to M15. This chapter describes the method as Gadfly builds it, part by part, and checks each part against those requirements. It is a description of a working system, not of a design, and it is written with two disciplines.

**The code as it is.** Every statement about what Gadfly does is a statement about its source code as committed on the main line of its repository at commit `e691015` (21 November 2026), and names the file where the behaviour can be checked. Where the project's own documentation, Chapter 1's summary, or an earlier outline described the system differently from the code, the code is taken as authoritative and the difference is recorded as a correction in Appendix A (items 30 to 36). Two kinds of pending change are excluded. A batch of bug fixes was in progress in the project's working tree at the time of writing and had not been committed; it changes some details described below, among them how an answered Synthesize question can settle an objective and how a contradiction that was put to the student but not yet judged is counted (Section 4.4). And the evidence-to-design review of the project (Gadfly repository, `docs/EVIDENCE_MAP.md`) proposed sixteen changes, numbered GAD-T258 to GAD-T273, none of which had been approved. Where one of them bears on a requirement, this chapter names it, but describes the system without it. Both the bug-fix batch and fourteen of the sixteen changes were committed later the same day; Section 5.2 records what they changed, and Table 5.1 restates this chapter's requirements table against the later commit.

**Sources as verified.** Every claim about what the evidence supports is cited at the level recorded in Appendix A, and the chapter cites no source that Chapters 2 and 3 do not.

The method is presented in the order in which an examination runs. Section 4.2 describes how the instructor's material becomes the standard of truth. Sections 4.3 to 4.5 describe the examiner: how it chooses and words each question, how it keeps and uses the ledger of the student's claims, and how it decides that an objective is settled. Section 4.6 describes the panel of language models that judges answers during and after the examination. Section 4.7 describes speech and the verification of transcripts. Section 4.8 describes the signals of recitation, gaze and voice, which Gadfly records as context. Section 4.9 describes human grading, and Section 4.10 the separate practice mode. Section 4.11 gathers the checks into a table.

### 4.2 Grounding

**Requirement.** No judgment without a cited passage. Every question, rubric point and verdict should rest on the instructor's material, which is the examination's standard of truth (M2, M7).

**As built.** The instructor supplies the source material, which Gadfly divides into numbered passages. A panel of language models extracts a concept map from them (`Gadfly.Sources/ConceptMapBuilder.cs`): each concept has a key, a title, a definition, key facts, plausible misconceptions and the numbers of the passages it rests on, and relations between concepts (prerequisite, part of, contrasts with, causes, example of). The extraction prompt requires that "every definition and fact must be supported by the cited passages" and forbids outside knowledge (`Gadfly/Engine/ExamPrompts.cs`, the concept-map prompt). Long material is processed in batches; within a batch a concept is kept only if a majority of the panel's members that answered found it, and concepts found in several batches are merged.

For each concept a second panel pass writes the rubric (`Gadfly.Sources/RubricBuilder.cs`): the points a student who understands the concept should be able to explain, each weighted from 1 to 3; acceptable alternative ways of putting them; and misconceptions to watch for, each citing the passages that refute it. The prompt asks for "understanding (why and how), not ... recalling wording." Rubric items are kept when a majority of the members proposed them. Retrieval for questions and judging combines word matching with embedding similarity over the passages (`Gadfly.Sources/GroundingRetriever.cs`).

The instructor can edit both the concept map and the rubrics. Edited items are marked and never overwritten when the material is processed again, and a changed rubric receives a new version number that is recorded with every judgment made under it. The instructor can also define learning objectives directly, each grounded in one or more concepts and assessed on named criteria, each tied to one of the platform's assessment dimensions (factual knowledge, conceptual understanding, logical reasoning, application, knowledge transfer, misconception recognition, consistency, metacognition) (`Gadfly/Engine/Objectives.cs`). An examination without objectives receives one implicit objective per concept.

**Assessment.** The grounding is thorough. Every rubric point is tied to passages, and the versioning of rubrics means that the standard a judgment was made against can be recovered, which condition (a) of Proposition 3.4 needs. One gap should be recorded. Chapter 1 described the instructor as reviewing and editing the concept map and rubrics before any examination. The code lets the instructor do so and protects the edits, but no step was found that requires the instructor to approve the map or the rubrics before an examination runs on them (Appendix A, correction 35). A rubric written by the panel and never read by a person can be the standard of truth for a graded examination.

### 4.3 The examiner

The examiner is the part of Gadfly that decides what to ask next and words it. It is built in two layers: a deterministic planner that chooses the objective and the move, and a panel of language models that writes the question for that move.

#### 4.3.1 Objectives, budget and order

**Requirement.** The examination samples a fixed set of objectives, reserves capacity for each, and uses the same stopping rule for everyone (M9, M11).

**As built.** A session runs under a policy (`Gadfly/Engine/SessionPolicy.cs`) that fixes the objectives, a time budget (20 minutes by default, which an accommodation can multiply by up to 4), a cap on questions in the session (40 by default) and a cap on questions per objective (6 by default), and the minimum number of independent supporting answers before a level counts as shown (`MinCorroboratingAnswers`, 2 by default, and never fewer than 2). The planner examines objectives one at a time in the instructor's order (`Gadfly/Engine/QuestionPlanner.cs`). It stays with the objective in progress until that objective is settled or reaches a limit, but only while doing so leaves room for every objective not yet started: it estimates its remaining capacity from the turn limit and from the time left at the session's own pace so far, and if the capacity is no longer more than the number of unstarted objectives times the corroboration minimum, it leaves the current objective inconclusive and opens the next. Its stated reason records the choice ("Leaving ... inconclusive to keep time for N more objective(s)"). The planner is deterministic: the same session, ledger and time always produce the same decision, so that a session can be replayed for audit.

**Assessment.** This meets the allocation rule of Section 3.5 directly: capacity is reserved for unstarted objectives, and an objective that cannot be settled is left inconclusive rather than allowed to consume the time of later ones. The default policy does not set a minimum number of objectives for a summative examination, and the platform does not mark an examination with few objectives as formative, so M11 is not yet met; a proposed change would add such a minimum (GAD-T267).

#### 4.3.2 The opening question

**Requirement.** Every objective opens with the same move, ideally the same question (M2).

**As built.** Every objective opens with Elicit, which asks the student to explain the objective in their own words. The move is fixed. The wording is not: each opening question is newly written by the panel, from the objective, the grounding passages and the conversation so far.

**Assessment.** M2 is met for the move and for the rubric, which is the same for every path, but not for the wording. Two students opening the same objective receive differently worded questions, so the one observation that every path shares is not literally the same observation. Section 2.4.3 conditioned comparability on every objective opening "with the same question"; the build meets the weaker condition (Appendix A, correction 31). A fixed, instructor-approved opening question per objective would meet the stronger one at little cost.

#### 4.3.3 The moves and how the planner chooses among them

**Requirement.** Each question should be the one expected to tell the competing hypotheses about the student's level apart (Section 3.2), and its probed level should match what an adequate answer actually shows (M5).

**As built.** The examiner's moves are defined in `Gadfly/Engine/Moves.cs`. Beside the eight of Chapter 1's list (Elicit, Clarify, Probe, Counterexample, Contradiction, Apply, Synthesize and Close), the build adds four that the project's plan calls strategies: CausalReasoning ("what causes this outcome?"), ScenarioVariation (change one condition of a situation already discussed and ask what changes and why), AssumptionTesting ("what must be true for your explanation to hold?") and IndependentExplanation (rebuild the explanation in the student's own reasoning, without textbook wording). Two further moves, Hint and GuidedExplanation, exist only in the practice mode (Section 4.10).

Each move has a probed level on the five-level ladder and a set of dimensions its answers bear on (`MoveProfiles` in `Gadfly/Engine/Objectives.cs`): Elicit probes level 2 (Basic Explanation); IndependentExplanation, Probe and CausalReasoning level 3 (Conceptual Understanding); Apply and ScenarioVariation level 4 (Application); Counterexample, AssumptionTesting and Synthesize level 5 (Transfer and Critical Reasoning). Clarify and Contradiction probe no level.

After the opening question, the planner chooses each follow-up by a fixed order of rules (`QuestionPlanner.FollowUp`):

1. **A contradiction first.** If the claim ledger holds a contradiction between two of the student's claims on this objective that has not yet been put to them, the next move is Contradiction, never twice running (Section 4.4).
2. **A replacement after a leak.** If a question on this objective was found to have revealed expected answer points, the next move is a replacement question drawn from the moves whose answers need reasoning a leaked point does not supply (ScenarioVariation, Apply, AssumptionTesting, Counterexample).
3. **Help, in practice mode only** (Section 4.10).
4. **A clarification, when the meaning could change the judgment.** If the last answer's meaning is uncertain and its score lies in the middle band, where clarification could change the judgment, the next move is a neutral Clarify, never twice running and at most twice per objective. A clearly wrong or clearly strong answer is not clarified.
5. **A possible misconception is tested.** If the response analysis found a possible misconception not yet tested, the next move is Counterexample or AssumptionTesting.
6. **Otherwise, the most informative strategy.** The planner keeps three hypotheses about the student's level on the objective: the corroborated level, the highest level shown once (the tentative top), and the highest level still plausible (the ceiling, just below the lowest level failed above the tentative top). It sets a target level between them (corroborating a level shown once, or bisecting between the corroborated level and the ceiling) and scores each candidate move by how close its probed level is to the target, with penalties for moves already used on the objective and for repeating the last move, and small bonuses for testing a misconception or for reaching a dimension not yet evidenced. The highest-scoring move is chosen, and its reason, including the runner-up, is recorded.

When at least two objectives have been settled at Conceptual Understanding or above, and enough time remains, the planner may ask one Synthesize question connecting two related objectives. Close is defined as a move but the planner never issues it: an objective ends when the evidence rules of Section 4.5 settle it or a limit closes it (Appendix A, correction 32).

**Assessment.** The planner is a close approximation to the question-selection rule of Section 3.2. It does not compute an expected information gain, but its target-level rule performs the bisection over the ladder that information gain recommends under the model's simple response model, and it records why each question was asked. Two departures from the model should be recorded.

- **ScenarioVariation is profiled as application.** A what-if inside a situation already discussed is extrapolation, which Bloom et al. (1956) place under Comprehension, not application. Profiling it at level 4, on the Application dimension, lets an answer to it count as evidence of application (Appendix A, correction 15). M5 is not met. A proposed change would count transfer only from Apply (GAD-T268).
- **The clarification rule meets M4.** A clarification's answer is merged into the answer it clarifies and judged once, in its clarified form (Section 4.5); it is never counted as a second answer.

#### 4.3.4 Wording and the rule against hinting

**Requirement.** No question may contain or presuppose its answer (M3).

**As built.** The question for each move is written by a panel of four language models from different providers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and DeepSeek, the "trusted" providers named in `Gadfly/Socrates/LegionTierResolver.cs`). Several members draft a question and the panel fuses or chooses the sharpest draft (`Gadfly/Engine/QuestionGenerator.cs`). The prompt (`ExamPrompts.Question`) tells the examiner that "this is examination mode: you never teach, lecture, correct, confirm or deny, and you never reveal or hint at the answer." It limits each question to one question of at most 40 words, forbids yes-or-no questions, asks the examiner to "build on the student's own words where you can, so the question could not have been prepared in advance," and forbids including "rubric points, definitions, causes, examples or facts the student has not already said themselves."

Every drafted question then passes a second vote, the leak guard (`ExamPrompts.QuestionGuard`), in which the panel decides whether the question "states or strongly hints at any of the expected answer points ... that the student has not already said." A question judged to leak is redrafted once; if the redraft also leaks, a plain, safe question for the move is asked instead. The guard fails closed: if the panel cannot decide, the draft is not asked. If a leak is found after a question was asked (for instance, in a clarification shown to the student during transcript verification, or by the response analysis, which reports whether the question "stated or strongly hinted at expected answer points"), the turn is flagged and every later answer on the objective is marked contaminated, except that a replacement question's answer still counts for the dimensions a leak cannot give away: application, transfer, reasoning and misconception recognition (`ObjectiveAssessor.IsContaminated`).

**Assessment.** This is the strongest part of the build's design against hinting. The guard and the contamination rule together implement Section 3.3's treatment of leaks: a leak is not only prevented where possible, but, when it happens, the evidence after it is discounted in exactly the way the model requires. The guard tests only for stated or strongly hinted answer points. It does not test for presupposition, which Section 2.3.1 identified as a way of suggesting an answer without stating it, so M3 is only partly met. A proposed change would extend the guard (GAD-T266).

#### 4.3.5 Probing on delivery

**Requirement.** The examiner may adapt to anything, but signals that vary with anxiety, accent or language background must not reach the score through the path, and their effect on paths should be measured (M1, M15).

**As built.** Before each question, Gadfly reads how the last answer was delivered, compared with the same student's earlier answers (`Gadfly/Engine/AnswerDelivery.cs`): the wait before the first word (a cue at 8 seconds or more and at least twice the student's usual), hesitations per hundred words (a cue at 6 or more and at least twice the usual), voice strain above the student's own baseline (a cue at 0.5 or more on the platform's strain measure, Section 4.8), and two or more hedging phrases in the answer. One cue sets the probing pressure to "Some," two or more to "Shaky," and a recitation flag (Section 4.8) to "Recited." The pressure and the cues are given to the examiner's prompt, which tells it to "probe harder within the move (ask for a concrete example, challenge an assumption, ask why again, or pose a counterfactual)" and adds: "Delivery is only a reason to press, never evidence of understanding, and never mention it: no remark on pauses, nerves or tone." The project's owner has decided that voice strain should drive harder probing (owner decision (b) in `docs/EVIDENCE_MAP.md`), and that decision stands in the build.

**Assessment.** The build keeps delivery out of the judges' prompts and out of the score arithmetic, and it compares each student only with their own earlier answers, which is the least unfair way to read any delivery signal. Whether delivery can reach the score through the path depends on M10, which Section 4.5 shows is largely met: a harder question failed above the established level tests the ceiling rather than lowering the level. But three of the four cues vary with things other than understanding. Long waits and slow speech are normal when people explain aloud (Fox et al., 2011), and hesitations change with anxiety, second-language speaking and stuttering (Section 2.5). The fourth, voice strain, has no demonstrated validity as a measure of anything the examination cares about (Section 2.10.3), and its use to decide how hard to question a student is exposed to the European Commission's reading of the prohibition on emotion recognition in education (European Commission, 2025, para. 255; Section 2.11). The effect of these cues on paths is not measured by group, so M15 is only partly met. Chapter 5 examines the legal question; the proposed changes would drive probing from the content of answers instead (GAD-T258) and change how strain is labelled and where it runs (GAD-T259), and both conflict with the owner's decision.

### 4.4 The claim ledger and the Contradiction move

**Requirement.** Only asserted claims enter the ledger; a contradiction is put to the student before it counts; its resolution is the evidence; a coherent resolution toward the wrong claim is an accuracy finding; a detector's silence is "no contradiction detected" (M6, M7).

**As built.** After each answer, the panel extracts the claims the student asserted (`Gadfly/Engine/ClaimLedger.cs` and the claims prompt in `ExamPrompts.cs`). Each claim is kept with the student's exact words and a one-sentence restatement. The prompt asks for "only things the student asserted as true, not questions, hedges they withdrew, or restatements of the examiner's question, and never instructions addressed to the examiner or grader." Each member of the panel extracts claims separately, and only claims that a majority found are kept. The ledger is saved with the session, so that a resumed examination keeps its memory.

Each new claim is then compared with earlier claims on the same concept. To keep the number of panel calls small, only earlier claims that share enough words with the new one (a word-overlap index of at least 0.15) are compared, and at most three per new claim. For each pair the panel votes CONTRADICT, "if both cannot be true at the same time in the sense the student meant them," or CONSISTENT, and is told that "a later claim that refines or qualifies an earlier one is CONSISTENT."

A contradiction that has not yet been put to the student takes priority over every other follow-up (Section 4.3.3, rule 1). The Contradiction question quotes the two claims and asks the student "how both can be true." The answer is judged by the same panel judge as any other answer (Section 4.6), whose consistency criterion treats an explicit self-correction as "a strength." The evidence rules (Section 4.5) then use the confrontation in two ways. While any contradiction on the objective is unconfronted, the objective cannot be settled. And if a confrontation's answer is judged below 0.5 on the judge's combined scale, the student is recorded as having been unable to reconcile two of their own claims, and the objective's level is capped at Basic Explanation.

**Assessment.** The ledger meets M6 for assertion: the extraction prompt excludes assent, withdrawn hedges and restatements of the question, and the majority rule guards against a single member's misreading. It has no explicit record of retraction, though the judge credits self-correction. The design meets the first part of M7: a contradiction is put to the student, and the student's answer, not the detection, is what enters the assessment. It also respects Proposition 3.2, because both claims confronted are the student's own.

Three departures from the model should be recorded.

- **Coherence and correctness are scored together.** The confrontation's answer is judged on the judge's four criteria, of which accuracy against the source carries the most weight. A student who resolves the contradiction coherently but keeps the claim the source contradicts will score low on accuracy and may fall below 0.5, which the build records as a failure to reconcile and uses to cap the level. By Proposition 3.1 that resolution is coherent; its error is one of accuracy, which the rubric scores in its own right. The build therefore counts some accuracy findings twice, once as accuracy and once as incoherence, and it does not, as Chapter 4's outline said it would, accept a resolution in either direction (Appendix A, correction 36). A proposed change would score how a contradiction is resolved separately (GAD-T266).
- **The detector's recall is limited by construction.** Only claims about the same concept, with enough words in common, are compared, and only three per claim. A contradiction between claims on different concepts, or one expressed in different words, cannot be found. The build reports an objective with no detected contradiction as consistent; by Section 3.4 it should report "no contradiction detected." The panel's confidence in each contradiction is recorded but not used.
- **A confrontation that was asked but not judged.** On main at the time of writing, a contradiction that has been put to the student counts as resolved even if no usable judgment of the answer exists yet, so that the objective can be settled without the evidence the confrontation was meant to produce. The uncommitted bug-fix batch noted in Section 4.1 treats such a confrontation as still pending.

The model's caution about what a contradiction shows is reflected in the reviewer-facing language of the build, which never treats a contradiction as evidence of dishonesty. The hypothesis that relayed answers contradict themselves (H1, Section 3.3) appears in the project's earlier documentation as a design claim; the research pass recorded it as unsupported (Appendix A, correction 6), and nothing in the build relies on it.

### 4.5 Evidence and stopping

**Requirement.** A level only with corroboration, a tested ceiling and nothing pending; insufficient evidence reported as distinct from low understanding; a stopping rule that is the same for everyone and does not stop on a running estimate; path-invariant scoring with the path reported (M8, M9, M10).

**As built.** The rules that decide whether an objective is settled are plain, deterministic code with no language model involved (`Gadfly/Engine/ObjectiveAssessor.cs`). Each judged answer on an objective becomes an outcome with the level its move probed. A judged score of 0.6 or more is positive for that level, below 0.4 negative, and in between "mixed," which counts as positive for Basic Explanation and negative for any higher level the move probed. An answer is left out of the independent evidence if it came after a leak (as Section 4.3.4 describes), if it came after help in the practice mode, or if the recitation check flagged it (Section 4.8). A clarification is merged into the answer it clarifies. An honest "I don't know," which the panel recognizes as aporia, is scored 0 and counts as a negative answer at the level it was asked. An answer that no member of the panel judged usably is left out until a human grades it.

From the independent outcomes the assessor computes three quantities (`ObjectiveAssessor.Climb`): the corroborated level, the highest level with at least the corroboration minimum of positive answers at or above it, with the extra condition that Transfer and Critical Reasoning also needs a positive answer at Application; the tentative top, the highest level shown once; and the ceiling. An objective is **sufficient** when it has at least the minimum of independent answers, its tentative top is corroborated or outweighed by repeated failures, its ceiling has been tested (the level is the top of the scale, or two answers above it failed, or one failed and the objective has at least two more independent answers than the corroboration minimum), and nothing is pending: no unconfronted contradiction and no untested misconception. The level is capped at Basic Explanation if the student could not reconcile a contradiction (Section 4.4) or a misconception persisted after it was tested. If an objective reaches its question limit, or the examiner moves on, or the session ends before it is sufficient, it is **inconclusive**, and its reported level is Level 0, which the platform defines as "The examination does not provide enough reliable evidence to determine understanding" (`Gadfly/Assessment/AssessmentRules.cs`) and which it states "means 'not enough reliable evidence', never 'no understanding'." Each objective carries a plain-language explanation of why it is, or is not yet, sufficient.

The engine also keeps a running mastery estimate for each concept, updated after each judged answer in the manner of a rating system (`Gadfly/Engine/MasteryModel.cs`), and the session report shows it. It plays no part in deciding when an objective is settled or what level is reported.

**Assessment.** This part of the build meets M8 and M9 almost exactly as Section 3.5 states them. One answer never establishes a level; the ceiling must be tested; open questions of coherence and misconception block a conclusion; and running out of time produces a verdict of insufficient evidence, labelled as such, not a low level. The rule is the same for every student, and the running estimate, whose use as a stopping rule Proposition 3.3 warns against, is kept out of the decision. The level ladder also satisfies the core of M10: the level is the corroborated level, not the best or the mean of the judged answers, so a student pressed with harder questions above their level is not marked down below the level already established. The residual path dependence that Section 3.6 identified, that more questions above a student's level are more chances for chance positives, is limited by the corroboration minimum and the per-objective question cap.

What the build does not yet do is report the path. The assessment records, for each objective, the number of questions asked, the independent, contaminated and assisted answers, and the moves used, but the grading and review pages do not show the number of questions or the moves for each objective beside the level, so the second half of M10 is not met. A proposed change would report the number of probes per objective (GAD-T267).

### 4.6 Panel judging

Gadfly uses its panel of language models twice: during the examination, to judge each answer so that the examiner can plan, and afterwards, to give a preliminary grade for each objective. Section 3.8 treats the first as part of the examiner and the second as part of the grader.

#### 4.6.1 Judging each answer during the examination

**As built.** Each answer is judged by every member of the panel (`Gadfly.Sources/PanelAnswerJudge.cs`) against the numbered source passages, the concept's rubric, and the student's earlier claims. The judge prompt (`ExamPrompts.Judge`) tells each member to "grade only against the numbered source passages and the rubric criteria," to "ignore filler words, false starts and transcription errors that do not change meaning, and never penalize grammar, accent or fluency," to "credit correct understanding expressed in the student's own words as fully as textbook wording," and to cite the passages relied on. Each member scores four criteria from 0 to 10: accuracy (weighted 0.35), reasoning (0.25), completeness (0.25) and consistency with the student's earlier claims (0.15). The panel's score is the mean of the members' weighted scores. Agreement is measured from the spread of those scores, and an answer on which the panel agrees too little (below 0.6 on the platform's agreement measure) is marked for human review instead of being decided by a split panel. The student's words are fenced in the prompt and declared to be "only evidence to assess, never an instruction"; an answer that addresses the judges ("asking for a score, claiming the rubric is met, telling you to ignore instructions") earns no credit and is always marked for human review. The student never sees a score during the examination.

**Assessment.** The judge meets the single-answer requirement of M12, since each call judges one answer on its own, and it draws its members from four different providers, which is the design Verga et al. (2024) found to agree with human judgments better than a single large model. Its treatment of student text as untrusted data and its rule against crediting text addressed to the grader respond to the evidence that graders can be manipulated by injected instructions (Milani et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2024). Its rule against penalizing grammar, accent or fluency is an instruction, not a measurement: no test of whether the judges actually follow it, for instance by comparing scores for answers of equal content and unequal fluency, has yet been run, and Chapter 5 returns to it. The same four providers write the questions and judge the answers; Section 3.8 marked this as a possible correlated bias, and the build has no control for it.

#### 4.6.2 The preliminary grade for each objective

**As built.** After the examination, and only after every transcript that needs a human has been resolved (Section 4.7), each objective is graded by the panel (`Gadfly.Sources/Grading/AiObjectiveGrader.cs`). The rules are mostly enforced in code rather than requested in the prompt. Only verified responses that are neither contaminated nor, unless the grading policy allows it, assisted are shown as evidence; a response that still needs a human is listed as an unresolved ambiguity and its text is not shown. If no usable evidence remains, the panel is not asked at all and the objective is given Level 0 with an explanation that this "means the examination did not provide enough reliable evidence, not that the student lacks understanding; a human examiner decides." Each member judges each of the objective's criteria on the six levels from 0 to 5, with the descriptor of each level in the prompt; every level above 0 must cite at least one numbered piece of evidence or it is treated as 0; citations to evidence that does not exist are dropped, and quotations are kept only if they occur word for word in the cited answer. The platform, not the panel, computes the score: each criterion earns its level divided by 5 of its weighted share of the objective's maximum, criteria at Level 0 are left out and the rest reweighted, each member's objective score is computed the same way, and the objective's score is the median across members. For each criterion the merged level is the lower median, and a member two or more levels away is recorded as dissenting. Confidence is reported as a category, never a percentage. The result is stored as preliminary and is never published on its own.

**Assessment.** The grader's controls go beyond what most published uses of language-model grading describe: evidence is restricted to verified, uncontaminated answers; a level must be justified by cited evidence; invented quotations are removed by code; dissent is recorded; and Level 0 is kept distinct from zero understanding. Three points fall short of the model.

- **Coverage is not reported.** Leaving criteria at Level 0 out of the score, and reweighting the rest, is right, since no evidence is not evidence of no understanding. But it means a path that never reached an objective's harder criteria is scored on its easier ones alone (Section 3.6). The criterion levels are stored and visible to reviewers, but the score itself does not show how much of the objective it covers.
- **Evidence is shown in a fixed order, and the level comes before the reason.** The numbered evidence is always presented in the order the answers were given, and the output format asks for each criterion's level before its rationale. Section 3.8 marked a fixed order as a possible source of correlated bias, and the project's evidence review proposes randomized order and evidence before score (GAD-T269). M12 is partly met.
- **The online judgment reaches the offline grade.** Each piece of evidence shown to the grader carries a "reviewer note" with the stance the response analysis recorded for it during the examination (supports, contradicts, misconception or uncertain). Section 3.8 argued that the online judgment belongs to the examiner, and that showing it to the grader correlates their errors. The grader is told the stance as context, which is not the same as being shown the online score, but the separation that M1 and M12 ask for is incomplete.

### 4.7 Speech and the recording channel

**Requirement.** Transcripts are confirmed or corrected before they are graded, and uncertain recognition is flagged rather than scored as wrong content (M14).

**As built.** Gadfly's examinations are spoken: the examiner, a talking avatar, asks each question aloud, and the student answers aloud. Under the owner's decision (a) in `docs/EVIDENCE_MAP.md`, the microphone and the camera are required for the whole examination and there is no typed-answer mode. An answer is ended by a period of silence (4 seconds by default, configurable between 2 and 15 seconds, `Gadfly.Server/Voice/VoiceSettings.cs`). The session clock starts when the examiner begins the first question.

After each answer, a response verifier (`Gadfly.Sources/Verification/ResponseVerifier.cs`) decides whether the student should be asked to clarify. Cheap deterministic checks come first, among them low confidence from the speech recognizer on the whole answer or on individual words; then the panel looks for ambiguity, multiple interpretations and unrecognized terms, asking only when the uncertainty could materially affect grading. The clarification is worded so that it cannot lead: it quotes the student's own words, and it passes the same leak checks as a question. Each response moves through recorded verification states (captured, verification required, awaiting the student's confirmation, confirmed, corrected, clarified, unresolved, flagged for human review; `VerificationMachine` in `Gadfly/Assessment/AssessmentRules.cs`). The platform distinguishes three kinds of change: a transcription correction, which "never reduces the score"; a clarification of meaning, which is stored beside the original rather than over it; and a substantive revision, which is treated as a new response under the examination's rules. A response that is unresolved or flagged blocks the panel's grading until a human resolves it, and the grade's status records the wait ("Transcript Verification Pending").

**Assessment.** The verification design meets M14 for the treatment of uncertain recognition: a word the recognizer was unsure of becomes a question to the student, not a wrong answer, and an unresolved transcript is never graded by the panel. Two requirements from Chapter 2 are not met. The error rate of the speech recognizer is not measured by accent, dialect or fluency group (Section 2.5.2), so the build cannot show that its recording channel treats groups alike; a proposed change would measure it and mark low-confidence segments for graders (GAD-T265). And there is no typed-answer accommodation. Section 2.5.2 concluded that such an accommodation is a requirement, not a convenience, for students whom speech recognition serves badly or for whom speaking is disabling; under the owner's decision there is none, and a proposed change would let an instructor grant one per student (GAD-T260). The fixed 4-second silence that ends an answer adds time pressure that falls hardest on anxious, second-language and disfluent speakers (Section 2.5.1); a proposed change would adapt it to each student's own pauses (GAD-T264).

### 4.8 Recitation, gaze and voice: signals as context

**Requirement.** Integrity signals are recorded for a human reviewer, never fail a student automatically, never enter the score, and are kept within what the evidence on each can support (Sections 2.10 and 2.11; M15).

Gadfly collects three kinds of signal during a spoken examination. They differ in what they measure and in how far the evidence supports them, and the build treats them differently.

#### 4.8.1 The recitation check

**As built.** After each answer, a deterministic check (`Gadfly/Engine/RecitationCheck.cs`) compares the answer with the source passages the judge saw and with the student's own delivery. It flags an answer of at least 15 words as closely following the source when at least 60% of its six-word runs appear word for word in the source, or when it contains a copied stretch of at least 20 words. It flags a spoken answer as fitting a read-out pattern when it is at least 60 words long, fluent (no long pauses and at most 2 hesitations per hundred words) and began only after a silent wait of at least 15 seconds and at least twice the student's usual. A flagged answer is not accepted as the student's own understanding: it is left out of the evidence, the next question asks the student "to say it differently in their own words, to apply it to a new case they have not seen, or to explain why it is so," and the examiner is told never to "say or hint that the answer sounded read or copied." A reviewer sees the flag, the overlap, the copied stretch and which pattern fired, with the note that it is "a prompt for review, never a cheating verdict." If the next question is a clarification of the recited answer, the student's reworded answer replaces it.

**Assessment.** The recitation check is the integrity control the evidence supports best, because it acts on content and responds with a follow-up question rather than a verdict. In the terms of Section 3.3, it identifies answers with a high $g$, answers that could be produced without the understanding, and replaces them with a question whose adequate answer requires the principle. It does change the evidence, by excluding the flagged answer, and so it is an integrity signal that does reach the grade, though only by withholding credit for words the student may not own, never by penalizing them (Appendix A, correction 33). The source-overlap test is a measure of wording, not of behaviour. The read-out pattern is weaker: a long pause followed by a fluent answer is also what careful thinkers and some confident speakers do, and its rate of false flags on honest students has not been measured; a proposed change would measure it and suppress it under an accommodation (GAD-T272).

#### 4.8.2 Gaze

**As built.** In the spoken examination, the camera is analysed on the student's device (`Gadfly.Server/wwwroot/js/examiner-gaze.js`). It estimates whether the student is looking at the examiner's face, against a baseline learned at the start, and detects the absence of a face, a second face, and a reading-like pattern of eye movement. While the student is answering, looking away for 2 seconds begins to fade the exam screen toward a heavy dim and blur over 3 seconds, with a warning to look back, and the screen restores as soon as the gaze returns. A look away of 5 seconds or more, a reading pattern, no face or a second face makes the examiner say aloud, "Please look at me while you answer," at most once every 15 seconds. These behaviours are the owner's decision (c). Each episode is sent to the server with its start and end. "Only those notes leave the device: never a frame, an image, a landmark or a face signature." The server accepts only a fixed list of episode types, bounds their number and rate, and decides their severity itself, never taking it from the browser; webcam signals are "annotations for a human to weigh, at most Low, and never an automatic penalty" (`Gadfly.Server/Integrity/WebcamIntegrityRecorder.cs`). Reviewers see each episode with a plain explanation (`Gadfly.Server/Review/IntegrityExplanations.cs`). A look away is explained as "often thinking," a reading pattern as "evidence to weigh, never proof," and a fade as a record of "what the student saw, not a finding." Blind grading does not hide these episodes, or the recitation flags, from the human examiner: they are shown on the review page while the examiner grades (`Gadfly.Server/Review/ReviewService.cs`), though voice strain is hidden (Section 4.8.3).

**Assessment.** The build keeps gaze where the evidence puts it. Webcam gaze error is about four degrees of visual angle (Papoutsaki et al., 2016; Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2018), far too coarse to resolve reading at the scale of words (Rayner, 2009), and the build records gaze at low confidence, never scores it and never penalizes a student for it. Its use is also, on the Commission's example, outside the prohibition on emotion recognition, since detecting the use of unauthorized material "is not prohibited, because the system does not identify or infer emotions" (European Commission, 2025, para. 255). Three concerns remain. First, the fade and the spoken remark act on the student automatically, during the answer. A fade that begins after 2 seconds may fire on honest thinking: explaining aloud is slow (Fox et al., 2011), and how often students look away while they think was not among the sources verified. It adds pressure that falls hardest on anxious students, who already do worse under proctoring (Woldeab & Brothen, 2019). And in one proctoring product students with darker skin were flagged for review more often (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022), so the rate at which the camera loses a face or misreads a gaze should not be assumed equal across groups. The fade therefore changes the conditions of the examination, and so potentially the evidence, even though the gaze record never enters the score. A proposed change keeps the decision but adds a grace period, an accommodation switch and measurement of fade rates by group (GAD-T261). Second, the human examiner sees the gaze episodes while grading. The outline of this chapter proposed that integrity context be shown to reviewers only after the content-based score, so that it cannot prime the judgment of the content, by the same mechanism of anchoring that Section 3.8 described for the panel's verdict; the build shows it during grading. Third, the reviewer-facing text describes looking away while answering as "a well-supported sign of thinking." That may be true, but no source in the verified ledger supports it, and the dissertation does not repeat it (Appendix A, correction 34).

#### 4.8.3 Voice

**As built.** The browser measures acoustic features of each spoken answer (pitch level and variability, jitter, loudness) and the speech library supplies speech rate and pauses (`Gadfly.Server/Voice/VoiceStrain.cs`). These are compared with the same student's first spoken answers in the session, which form the baseline. Reviewers see a line such as "Possible vocal strain and hesitation compared with this student's first answers," with a caveat about what it does not mean. The student sees a short description of their own voice in their live transcript, labelled "a description of your voice, not a judgement of the answer." A strain value of 0.5 or more above the baseline is one of the delivery cues that set the probing pressure (Section 4.3.5). Strain never enters the score, and blind grading hides it.

**Assessment.** The evidence gives voice strain no support as a measure. Voice-stress analysis has performed at about chance in laboratory and field tests (Harnsberger et al., 2009; Damphousse et al., 2007), the National Research Council found "little or no scientific basis" for it (National Research Council, 2003), and even the acoustic correlates of stress are inconsistent (Giddens et al., 2013); jitter, which the build reads as strain, tends to fall rather than rise under stress where any effect is found (Appendix A, correction 25). Its baseline is the student's first answers, when warm-up nerves and easier opening questions both differ from later ones. The build's use of strain is confined to steering questions and informing reviewers, never to grading, which is the least harmful use available; but the steering use is the one most exposed under the AI Act (Section 2.11), and the build's privacy documentation, which states that nothing infers emotion, is exposed while it continues (Appendix A, correction 28). Chapter 5 takes up both questions.

### 4.9 Human grading

**Requirement.** Blind human judgment; a combination weight fixed in advance; large disagreements reconciled on the evidence by a reviewer independent of both judgments; only a human approves and publishes; aggregate agreement thresholds for sets of answers (M13).

**As built.** Grading follows a fixed sequence of statuses that the code will not let a grade skip (`GradeStatusMachine` in `Gadfly/Assessment/AssessmentRules.cs`): examination completed, transcript verification pending, AI grading pending, human review pending, reconciliation required, awaiting final approval, finalized, published, and then under appeal or amended. A student can see a grade only once it is published.

The grading policy for an examination (`Gadfly/Assessment/CombinedScoring.cs`) fixes, before grading begins, the weights of the panel's and the human's scores (0.4 and 0.6 by default), two disagreement thresholds (10 and 20 percentage points of the objective's maximum by default), and whether human grading is blind (on by default). The human weight cannot be set to 0: "a human examiner must take part in grading." The policy in force is copied into the grade record when the panel's grading starts, and a later change to an examination's policy needs an approved, audited change and does not silently re-grade work already graded (`Gadfly.Server/Grading/GradingCore.cs`).

While grading is blind, an examiner who can enter human scores sees neither the panel's grade nor the exam-time judge's evidence (its stances, notes, scores, votes and summaries) until the human evaluation is in (`Gadfly.Server/Grading/BlindGrading.cs`; `GradingService.AiVisible`). The combined score of each objective is the weighted sum of the two scores, and the examination's score is the weighted mean across objectives, computed in exact decimal arithmetic. The difference between the two scores sets a band: below the first threshold, standard review; between the thresholds, additional review; at or above the second, reconciliation is mandatory and blocks finalization. Reconciliation records the cause of the disagreement, chosen from a fixed list, and a documented resolution, with resolved scores if they change; a second reviewer, a member holding the Second Reviewer role, can be requested and is recorded when involved. Approval needs the permission to approve final grades and is refused while any mandatory disagreement is open. Publication is a separate act. A student can appeal a published grade, and every grading action, including every refused attempt, is written to an append-only audit log. Roles carry permissions from an explicit matrix (`Gadfly/Assessment/Permissions.cs`): the Examiner role can enter human scores but cannot approve, while the instructor and administrator roles can approve and publish.

**Assessment.** The human-grading design meets most of M13. The weights are fixed before grading and cannot exclude the human; the human grades blind by default, which Section 3.8 showed is what makes disagreement measurable; large disagreements block finalization; and only a person can approve and publish. Three points fall short.

- **Reconciliation need not be independent.** The model requires that a large disagreement be resolved by a reviewer independent of both judgments. In the build, a second reviewer is optional. A reconciliation can be recorded by any holder of the permission to reconcile, which the instructor and administrator roles have, and no check prevents the person who entered the human score from reconciling the disagreement with it and then approving the grade. The separation of the permission to score from the permission to approve is a separation between roles, not between persons, and the instructor role holds both (Appendix A, correction 30).
- **Blindness is a policy default, and partial.** An examination's policy can turn blind grading off, and owners and administrators, who cannot enter human scores, always see the panel's view. The default is right; the guarantee is configurable. And blind grading hides the machine's judgment but not the integrity context: the examiner grades with the gaze episodes and recitation flags in view (Section 4.8.2).
- **No calibration thresholds.** The disagreement bands compare one student's two scores. Nothing yet checks, for an examination as a whole, whether the panel's agreement with human graders meets the aggregate thresholds of Section 2.9.3 before its scores are given any weight; a proposed change would add them (GAD-T270). Until the project's own double-marked pilot has been run (Gadfly repository, `docs/PILOT.md`), no agreement figure for the panel exists.

### 4.10 Practice mode

**Requirement.** Help belongs in a separate mode, kept apart from scored examinations in data and display; if help is ever counted, it is the same instructor-written ladder of prompts for every student, given only after an inadequate answer (Section 2.8).

**As built.** A session runs in one of two modes (`AssessmentMode` in `Gadfly/Assessment/AssessmentRules.cs`). In examination mode the examiner never teaches. In Learning Mode, after a weak answer, an honest "I don't know" or a misconception, the planner gives a Hint, and if that is not enough, a GuidedExplanation, at most twice per objective by default, and then asks a reassessment question (`QuestionPlanner.FollowUp`, rule 3). The panel writes each hint and explanation; the leak guard does not run on them, since they teach on purpose. Every answer from the first hint on is marked as assisted evidence. The assessor reports two levels: the independent level, from unassisted answers only, and an assisted level that includes answers given after help. The panel's grader excludes assisted answers unless the grading policy explicitly allows them (`AllowAssistedEvidence`, off by default).

**Assessment.** The build keeps help apart from the scored evidence, as Section 2.8 requires: assisted answers are labelled, reported separately and excluded from grading by default. The help itself is interactionist, improvised by the panel for each student, rather than a fixed ladder. That is defensible for learning, but it means the amount and kind of help differ between students, so counts of help or assisted levels cannot be compared between them, and the evidence that dynamic assessment predicts best when help is not contingent on the student's response (Caffrey et al., 2008) favours a fixed ladder for any use beyond practice. A proposed change would make the ladder fixed and written by the instructor, with the panel deciding only when to give each prompt (GAD-T271).

Read as a whole, the table shows a pattern that the rest of the dissertation develops. The parts of the method that decide what the evidence is and when it is enough (the evidence rules, the stopping rule, the treatment of leaks and recitation, the verification of transcripts, the blind combination of machine and human judgment) meet the model's requirements closely, and in several respects go beyond what published uses of language-model grading describe. The shortfalls are concentrated in three places: what the method reports about the path that produced each score; the independence of the human side of grading; and the signals of delivery, gaze and voice, which the build keeps out of the score but lets act on the examination itself, in two cases by the owner's decision. None of these shortfalls is a matter of model capability. Each is a procedural choice, which is the thesis of Chapter 1 seen from the inside: the soundness of the evidence depends less on how capable the examiner's models are than on conditions that the method, and the institution that uses it, must choose to meet.

## Chapter 5 — Validity, Fairness and Integrity

### 5.1 What the score means

Chapter 4 asked whether Gadfly does what the model of Chapter 3 requires. This chapter asks a different question: if it does, what may the resulting score be taken to mean, for whom might it mean something different, and what may the system's integrity signals be taken to show? The question is one of validity in the ordinary sense of the word: whether the inferences drawn from a score are warranted by the evidence behind it. The chapter states that argument in its own terms, as a chain of inferences, and cites for each link only the sources recorded in Appendix A, at their recorded levels. It does not draw on the measurement literature in which validity arguments are formally developed, because none of that literature was among the sources verified (Appendix A, Section A.7).

A score from a synthetic Socratic examination supports a decision only through five inferences, each of which can fail on its own.

1. **From utterance to transcript.** What the judges read is a transcript of what the student said. The inference is that the transcript says what the student meant. It fails when the speech recognizer mis-hears, and the evidence is that it mis-hears some groups of speakers more than others (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024).
2. **From transcript to judged answer.** The inference is that the panel's judgment of an answer reflects the answer's content against the rubric, not its length, its fluency, its position, or instructions hidden in it. It fails through the known biases of language-model judges (Section 2.9) and through the quality of the rubric itself, which in Gadfly a panel writes and an instructor may, but need not, review (Section 4.2).
3. **From judged answers to an objective's level.** The inference is that the level reported for an objective reflects the student's understanding of that objective, and not the path the examiner happened to take. It fails when the path can change the level (Section 3.6), and when the examination itself changes what the student understands (Section 2.3.2).
4. **From objective levels to a grade.** The inference is that the objectives examined are a large enough sample of the subject for the grade to generalize. It fails on small samples, because content contributes more score variance than examiners do (Wass et al., 2003; van der Vleuten et al., 2010).
5. **From the grade to a decision.** The inference is that the people and procedures that turn the panel's and the human's judgments into a published grade do so accountably: blind where blindness matters, with disagreements resolved on the evidence, and with a person, not the system, deciding. It fails through automation bias (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 14(4)(b); Zheng et al., 2023) and through procedures that let one person both judge and ratify the same judgment (Section 4.9).

The rest of the chapter takes these links in turn. Section 5.2 records what has changed in Gadfly since Chapter 4 was written, because much of what this chapter can say depends on it. Sections 5.3 and 5.4 treat the first two links as matters of fairness: construct-irrelevant variance, and the fairness of machine judgment. Section 5.5 sets out the argument that the examination is its own main integrity control, and what the evidence does and does not support in it. Section 5.6 sets out the limits of surveillance. Section 5.7 sets out the four decisions the project's owner has made against parts of the evidence, with the evidence on both sides. Section 5.8 sets out the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act duties that bear on the system, and Section 5.9 the accountability of the whole. Section 5.10 summarizes.

#### 5.2.1 What landed

- **The bug-fix batch** (`d78d879`, GAD-T275). Two fixes bear on the evidence rules. A contradiction put to the student whose answer has not been judged now stays pending, so it blocks the objective from settling instead of counting as resolved (`Gadfly/Engine/ObjectiveAssessor.cs`), and the planner now counts a contradiction as confronted only when one question has put both of its claims to the student (`QuestionPlanner.FollowUp`). A good answer to a Synthesize question, which is asked only once an objective is settled, can now raise the settled level when corroborated but can no longer reopen the objective. Both errors made the delivery cues fire more often than designed. Section 5.3.3 returns to what that shows.
- **Contradiction and presupposition** (GAD-T266). A confrontation is now settled by the judge's consistency criterion, which on a Contradiction move scores whether the student sees, settles and reasons out the conflict, "either revision being legitimate", rather than by the overall score, whose accuracy component depends on which claim the student kept (`ObjectiveAssessor.ResolutionScore`). Correctness is left to the rubric and the source. The question prompt, the leak guard and the response analysis now also test whether a question's form presupposes an answer point (exam prompt set version 6).
- **Transfer only from Apply** (GAD-T268). Only a positive answer to Apply, a situation new to the student, now counts as evidence of transfer, and the top level (Transfer and Critical Reasoning) requires one. A ScenarioVariation what-if is recorded as extrapolation, at the Application dimension, and Synthesize no longer counts as transfer. Each Apply question carries a deterministic label naming the respect in which its situation differs from the source, from the context dimensions of Barnett and Ceci (2002), nearest first, with a note that the examination measures sequestered transfer (Bransford & Schwartz, 1999) (`Gadfly/Engine/TransferLabels.cs`).
- **Probe counts and formative labelling** (GAD-T267). Each objective's report shows how many follow-up questions it received. A policy setting, `MinSummativeObjectives` (4 by default), sets the fewest objectives an examination must examine for its scores to be labelled summative; Learning Mode sessions and shorter examinations are labelled formative in both reports (`Gadfly/Engine/Objectives.cs`).
- **Grading-panel bias controls** (GAD-T269). The grading prompt now says that "length, repetition and restatement earn nothing," that grammar, accent, fluency and non-native phrasing are never penalized and polish never rewarded, and that each criterion's evidence and quotation come before its level. The order of the rubric criteria, and of each criterion's level descriptors, is shuffled on every call. A probe, `PanelBiasCheck`, submits padded and less fluent paraphrases of the same answers to a panel so that their scores can be compared (`Gadfly.Sources/Grading/AiObjectiveGrader.cs`, `PanelBiasCheck.cs`).
- **Panel calibration and reconciliation triggers** (GAD-T270). A grading panel is accepted for an examination only after a blind, double-marked calibration set of at least 30 answers shows a quadratic-weighted kappa of at least .70 between panel and human, no more than .10 below the agreement between the two human markers, and an absolute standardized mean difference under .15, with the panel compared against each marker and the weaker comparison counting (`Gadfly/Assessment/PanelCalibration.cs`). Each evaluation is an audit event; the latest is frozen into each session's grading policy and shown on the grading page. Until a panel is accepted, its grades for that examination are labelled provisional. Single objectives now also go to reconciliation when the panel members are split by more than one level, or when the score sits on a grade boundary (`Gadfly/Assessment/ReconciliationTriggers.cs`).
- **Speech recognition fairness** (GAD-T265). Words the recognizer was unsure of, in transcripts the student did not check, are named to the grading panel, which is told never to treat them as wrong content, and marked for the human grader. A student's transcript correction is shown to the grader with the number of words it changed. An analytics view reports word error rate against student-checked transcripts, the share of answers corrected and the share of unsure words, by a disfluency measure computed from the transcript or by a grouping the institution pastes in and that is never stored, withholding small groups and warning of disparities (`Gadfly.Server/Analytics/SpeechRecognitionAudit.cs`).
- **Time** (GAD-T263, GAD-T264). The examination clock now stops while the examination is paused because the microphone, camera or examiner connection was lost, with the paused time added back up to a cap (10 minutes, or half the budget). The silence that ends an answer now adapts to the student's own pauses within the answer (one and a half times their 80th-percentile pause, no shorter than the configured minimum of 4 seconds and no longer than a new maximum of 12), and both bounds lengthen under the extra-time accommodation.
- **Gaze** (GAD-T261). A look away that begins in the first 10 seconds of an answer now starts the fade only after 6 seconds, not 2, and the spoken request moves later by as much. An instructor can declare an eye-contact accommodation on an assignment, audited, under which the screen never fades and the examiner never asks for eye contact or remarks on reading-like eye movement; face presence and a second face are still checked, and episodes are recorded as accommodated, at the lowest severity. An analytics view reports look-aways, reading-like episodes, fades, time faded and spoken requests per 10 answering minutes, by accommodation or by a pasted, unstored grouping, withholding groups under 5 students and flagging rates that differ by more than a quarter (`Gadfly.Server/Analytics/GazeFlagRates.cs`).
- **The read-out pattern** (GAD-T272). The timing-based read-out pattern of the recitation check no longer fires for a student with an extra-time accommodation and can be switched off per examination. A command-line measure counts how often it fires over a set of transcripts, including where it was suppressed, so that its rate of false flags on honest students can be measured. The source-overlap test is unchanged.
- **Voice in the European Union** (GAD-T259). An organization marked as being in the EU or EEA, and any student marked as living there, now get no voice measurements unless an administrator turns them on for everyone, an audited act that the project's privacy documentation says should follow legal advice. When they are off, the browser's voice numbers are discarded before the answer is stored, nothing is compared, and neither the voice comparison nor its pause-and-speech-rate stand-in can raise the probing pressure. The other timing cues, the wait before the first word and hesitations per hundred words, which come from the speech recognizer, still can (`AnswerDelivery.Read`); the project's documentation says that "nothing about the voice" then steers probing, which is broader than the code (Appendix A, correction 40). Everywhere, the reviewer's line now shows raw acoustic differences and names no state: it no longer says "strain," "stress" or "hesitation." The privacy documentation's statement that "Nothing infers emotion," which Appendix A recorded as exposed (correction 28), was withdrawn. Outside the EU, the strain measure still drives probing, as the owner decided.
- **Fixed hint ladder** (GAD-T271). In Learning Mode, each hint and guided explanation is now the next rung of the objective's ladder, written by the instructor (or a fixed default ladder), and no model writes or rewords it; the planner decides only when to give it. - **Clear scores** (GAD-T262). Released results now say, for each objective, which rubric criteria were scored below their maximum, with the panel's reason and the descriptor of the next level, which misconceptions to correct, and how the objective's score changed since the student's earlier attempt.

### 5.3 Construct-irrelevant variance

A score is construct-irrelevant to the extent that it varies with something other than the understanding it is meant to measure. Section 2.5 identified the main sources in a spoken examination: anxiety, second-language speaking, accent and dialect, disfluency, and, above all, the speech recognizer. They can reach a Socratic examination's score by three routes: through the transcript, which is the evidence the judges read; through the judges, if they reward or penalize how something was said; and through the path, if a signal that varies with them changes which questions a student receives.

#### 5.3.1 Through the transcript

The recognizer is the largest documented source. Its error is not noise around the evidence but a change in it: a word the recognizer mis-hears is, to the judges, a word the student said. In a test of five commercial recognizers the average word error rate was 0.35 for black speakers and 0.19 for white speakers, and the gap was as large on identical phrases (Koenecke et al., 2020, abstract); six leading recognizers were biased against the speech of people who stutter (Mujtaba et al., 2024, abstract). Both studies were read at the abstract level, and neither tested the recognizer Gadfly uses, so they establish the kind of risk, not its size for this system.

Gadfly's design answers this route in three ways, two of them new. Uncertain recognition becomes a question to the student before grading, and an unresolved transcript blocks the panel's grading (Section 4.7). Words the recognizer was unsure of, if the student did not check them, are now named to the panel with an instruction not to treat them as wrong content, and marked for the human grader. And word error rate can now be compared by group. This is the measurement Section 2.5.2 called for, and its form deserves scrutiny, because the comparison can mislead in two ways. First, the error rate is measured against transcripts the students themselves checked and corrected. Students who did not check their transcripts contribute no reference, and if the students the recognizer serves worst are also the least likely to find and correct its errors, perhaps because correcting is slow or tiring, the measured gap will understate the true one. Second, the built-in grouping is by a disfluency measure computed from the transcript, which is the recognizer's own output, so a recognizer that drops disfluencies will place some disfluent speakers in the fluent group. The pasted grouping avoids the second problem but depends on the institution holding, and being permitted to use, the relevant information. Neither problem makes the measure useless; both mean that a small measured gap is weaker evidence of fairness than a large one is of unfairness.

#### 5.3.2 Through the judges

The judges are told to ignore fluency and form, and, since GAD-T269, that length and restatement earn nothing and that polish is never rewarded. Section 4.6.1 observed that these are instructions, not measurements. The build now includes the instrument that would turn them into measurements: a probe that submits padded and less fluent paraphrases of the same answers to the panel and compares the scores. This is the test the outline of this chapter proposed, of "paraphrased answers of equal content and unequal fluency." It has not been run on a calibration set, and until it has, the claim that the panel scores content rather than delivery rests on the prompt. The evidence that judges can be swayed by length (Zheng et al., 2023) and that detectors of machine text misclassify the writing of non-native speakers (Liang et al., 2023) gives reason to expect some effect; Liang et al. concerns detectors, not graders (Appendix A, correction 22), so the size of any effect on Gadfly's graders is an open question, not a known one.

The human examiner is not exempt. Joughin (2010) warns that "examiners can mistake a student's articulateness for knowledge" (p. 7), and nothing in the build tests the human for it. The calibration set of GAD-T270 compares panel and human with each other, so a bias both share would not appear in it. A paraphrase probe run on human examiners as well as on the panel would detect a shared bias; Chapter 7 includes one.

#### 5.3.3 Through the path

The third route is the one specific to adaptive examination. Section 3.6 showed that a signal which steers the examiner can change a score only if the scoring rule lets the path matter, and Section 4.5 showed that Gadfly's evidence rules let it matter little: a level is the corroborated level, a harder question failed above it tests the ceiling rather than lowering the level, and the number of questions per objective is capped. The residual effect is that more questions above a student's level are more chances for a mixed or negative answer to block corroboration of a level shown once, and more chances for a chance positive.

Two things have changed. The number of follow-ups per objective is now reported, so a reviewer can see when one objective ran long. And the delivery cues themselves were found, in the bug-fix batch, to have been miscomputed: hesitations double-counted and waits partly made of clock skew. The fix matters less than what it shows. A delivery signal is a measurement with its own error, and its error is invisible unless its behaviour is measured. The gaze rates of GAD-T261 and the read-out count of GAD-T272 measure two such signals; nothing yet reports, by group, how often the probing pressure was raised, or on what cue. Until it does, the claim that delivery cues do not reach the score through the path rests on the scoring rule alone, which is strong but not complete.

#### 5.3.4 Time and anxiety

Anxiety is associated with poorer performance across a large literature (Hembree, 1988; von der Embse et al., 2018), and scores rise when it is treated (Hembree, 1988). A spoken examination adds speaking anxiety, and an examination in a second language adds language anxiety (Horwitz et al., 1986). Design choices that add time pressure fall hardest on these students (Section 2.5.1). Two such choices that Chapter 4 recorded have been changed: the clock stops during a pause the student did not cause, and the silence that ends an answer now adapts to the student's own pauses within a floor and a ceiling. Both changes reduce construct-irrelevant variance by the mechanism the literature names. Neither has been measured: whether an adaptive end-of-answer silence cuts off fewer answers from anxious or second-language speakers is an empirical question, and the parameters chosen (the 80th percentile, a factor of one and a half, a ceiling of 12 seconds) are design choices, not values from a source.

Three pressures remain by decision. The camera and microphone are required throughout (decision (a)); looking away fades the screen and prompts a spoken request (decision (c)); and, outside the EU, a voice measure that varies with stress steers harder questions (decision (b)). Each is discussed in Section 5.7. Here it is enough to note that each adds pressure where the evidence says anxiety does harm, and that the examination's confrontational move, putting two of a student's own claims to them, is itself the kind of exposure the *Apology* records as making those examined hostile (Plato, 1966, 21d). The prompt asks the examiner to quote the two claims and ask "how both can be true," which is neutral in form; whether students experience it as neutral is not known.

### 5.4 Fairness of machine judgment

#### 5.4.1 What the agreement thresholds can and cannot show

Since GAD-T270, Gadfly will not accept its grading panel for an examination until a blind, double-marked calibration set meets the aggregate thresholds used for automated scoring: a quadratic-weighted kappa of .70 or more, a degradation of less than .10 from human–human agreement, and a standardized mean difference of less than .15 (Williamson et al., 2012, as cited in Casabianca et al., 2023). This meets the requirement of Section 3.8 that the thresholds be applied to sets of answers, not to single ones (Appendix A, correction 21), and the build applies them conservatively, counting the panel's weaker comparison with the two human markers.

Three cautions apply. First, the thresholds are rules of practice, not derived optima, and they come from essay scoring, where human agreement ranged from 0.61 to 0.85 (Shermis, 2014). Second, agreement is not validity: a model "may do a good job of matching human scoring behavior, but for reasons unrelated (or unsatisfactorily related) to the construct of interest" (Shermis, 2014). A panel that matched human graders because both rewarded articulate delivery would pass. Third, an aggregate threshold can hide a group difference: a panel can meet all three thresholds overall and differ from human graders for one group of students. The calibration evaluation does not break its statistics down by group. The standardized mean difference by group is the obvious addition, and it requires the same institutional data on groups that the speech-recognition and gaze measures need.

A fourth point concerns what follows from failing. A panel that has not been accepted for an examination keeps its weight in the combined score; its grades are labelled provisional, and the human still grades blind, disagreements are still reconciled and a person still approves. That is defensible, since the panel is never the sole grader, but it means the calibration gate informs the people who grade rather than constraining the arithmetic. Section 3.8 used the thresholds "to decide whether the panel may be used at all." The build uses them to decide whether the panel may be trusted, and leaves its use to the people.

The reconciliation triggers that came with the calibration gate are of a different kind. Sending an objective to reconciliation when the panel's members are split by more than one level follows directly from Section 3.8: a split panel is a sign that the evidence is hard to judge, and the mean of a split panel is not a sound judgment. Sending it when the score sits near a grade boundary is a reasonable precaution, since an error there changes a decision. The project's code and research notes support the boundary trigger by citing Wang et al. (2024) for judgments being least stable near a cut, but that finding concerns pairwise comparisons, whose verdicts were least stable when the two answers were close in quality; it is an analogy for a rubric boundary, not evidence about one (Appendix A, correction 41). Both triggers are design proposals whose limits, as the code itself says, should be tuned on calibration data.

#### 5.4.2 Biases of the panel

The biases Section 2.9 documented are position, length, self-preference and susceptibility to injected instructions. The build now addresses each in part. Position bias was documented for pairwise comparison (Wang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023), which Gadfly does not use; the shuffling of criteria and levels removes a fixed order where it was easiest to remove, but the student's answers are still presented in examination order, because the citations depend on their numbering. A shuffled presentation that kept stable labels would remove the remaining fixed order at no cost to citation. Length is addressed by instruction and by the paraphrase probe. Injection is addressed by fencing the student's words as data and by flagging any answer that addresses the grader (Section 4.6.1; Milani et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2024). Self-preference is not addressed. The same four providers write the questions and grade the answers, and self-preference correlated with a model's ability to recognize its own outputs (Panickssery et al., 2024, preprint abstract). Whether a model grades answers to its own questions more kindly is untested; it is a cheap thing to test, by comparing grades from members whose family did and did not write the question.

#### 5.4.3 Monitoring score gaps

The outline of this chapter called for monitoring of score gaps by speaker group. The build now monitors two inputs to the score by group, the recognizer's error and the gaze episodes, but not the scores themselves. A gap in scores between groups is not, by itself, evidence of unfairness, since groups can differ in what they have learned. A gap that appears only in the panel's scores, or only in the human's, or that persists between students of equal standing on some independent measure, is. The comparison of the two graders' scores by group can be made from data the build already keeps, and it is the one fairness check that bears directly on the grade. Chapter 7 includes it.

### 5.5 Integrity by design

The argument that a Socratic examination is its own main integrity control can be stated in the terms of Section 3.3. A question is informative about understanding to the extent that an adequate answer is hard to produce without it. A follow-up written from the student's own previous answer cannot be prepared in advance, so it raises the cost of answering without understanding: a student relaying answers from another source must relay the follow-ups too, and in real time. On this argument, the examination does not need to catch a student relaying answers; it needs only to ask questions whose answers a relay is less likely to supply.

What the evidence supports in this argument is mostly negative. The alternatives are weak. Proctored online examinations produce lower scores than unproctored ones (Alessio et al., 2017; Dendir & Maxwell, 2020), but both findings are inferences from score changes, and neither shows that any signal identifies a person who cheated (Appendix A, correction 27). Detectors of machine-written text are neither accurate nor reliable (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023) and misclassify non-native writers (Liang et al., 2023). Gaze is too coarse to resolve reading (Section 2.10.2), and voice-stress analysis performs at about chance (Section 2.10.3). And the only cues to deception with some support concern the content of what is said, in that liars "tell less compelling tales" (DePaulo et al., 2003, abstract). If anything distinguishes an answer the student cannot stand behind, it is more likely to be found in how they answer a follow-up than in how they look or sound.

What the evidence does not yet support is the positive claim. No source in the verified ledger shows that conversational follow-up defeats relayed answers, or that a person relaying answers will contradict themselves under questioning (Appendix A, correction 6). A case study of online oral examination found it workable against impersonation (Akimov & Malin, 2020), and students surveyed after interactive orals saw them as deterring misconduct (Sotiriadou et al., 2020), but both were read at the abstract level and neither measured relayed answers. The claim that Socratic follow-up is the strongest integrity control available is therefore a hypothesis, and a testable one: Chapter 7 designs a condition in which participants relay answers from another source.

Two features of the build bear on the hypothesis directly. The first is the recitation check, which is the integrity control the evidence supports best because it acts on content and responds with a question rather than a verdict (Section 4.8.1). Since GAD-T272, its weaker half, the read-out pattern, is suppressed for students with extra time and its false-flag rate can be measured. The second is a limit. The design was written for a student who reads or types an answer from another source, and a voice examination with a camera makes both harder. It is not designed for a student who receives a relayed answer by ear, through a hidden earpiece, and speaks it in their own voice. Against that case the follow-up is the only control, and whether it works is exactly the untested hypothesis. The audio signal for a second voice would not hear an earpiece.

### 5.6 The limits of surveillance

#### 5.6.1 What each signal can show

The evidence assigns each kind of signal a ceiling, and the build's treatment of each can be read against it.

- **Gaze.** Webcam gaze error is about four degrees of visual angle (Papoutsaki et al., 2016; Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2018), while reading moves in jumps of seven to nine letters (Rayner, 2009). A webcam can tell that a student looked away; it cannot reliably tell reading notes from thinking. The build records gaze at the lowest severities, never scores it, and, since GAD-T261, gives a thinking grace at the start of each answer, an accommodation that removes the fade, and rates by group. The ceiling is respected in the record. It is not respected in the fade, which acts on the student during the answer on the strength of a signal that cannot tell thinking from reading (Section 5.7.3).
- **Face.** Face detection in one proctoring product flagged students with darker skin for review more often (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022), and commercial gender classification erred most on darker-skinned women (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018), which is evidence about facial analysis in general rather than about proctoring. The build's camera episodes for an absent face, a second face and a face that no longer resembles the one at the start are all exposed to this kind of disparity. The gaze-rate view of GAD-T261 reports look-aways, reading-like episodes and fades by group, but not absent-face or changed-face episodes, which are the ones the face-detection evidence concerns most directly.
- **Voice.** Voice-stress analysis has performed at about chance in laboratory and field tests (Harnsberger et al., 2009; Damphousse et al., 2007), the National Research Council found "little or no scientific basis" for it (National Research Council, 2003), and the acoustic correlates of stress are inconsistent (Giddens et al., 2013). The build's relabelling of the reviewer's line as raw acoustic differences, naming no state, matches what the measures can support. The probing use outside the EU does not (Section 5.7.2).
- **Text style.** Gadfly asks its panel to estimate how likely it is that a spoken answer "was read aloud from AI-generated or written text rather than composed while speaking," compared with the same student's other answers. The estimate is stored with the exam-time judgment of each answer, which blind grading hides; when the panel's mean estimate is 0.7 or more, it is also recorded as an integrity event, "Answer reads like written text," at low severity, which the human examiner sees while grading (`Gadfly.Server/Sessions/SessionOrchestrator.cs`). Both carry the caution that it is weak evidence that never changes the score. This is a detector of machine-written text in all but name, applied to transcribed speech. Comparing a student with their own other answers is the least unfair form such a signal could take, and it is shown only as context. But the evidence on detectors is the strongest negative evidence in Chapter 2: seven widely used detectors labelled more than half of a set of essays by non-native English writers as machine-generated (Liang et al., 2023), and detectors as a class were found neither accurate nor reliable (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023). Section 2.9.4 concluded that they "should never be used as integrity signals." The text-style estimate is not one of the tools those studies tested, and its within-student comparison may behave differently, but nothing measures how it behaves. It is the integrity signal in the build with the least support and the least scrutiny: it does not appear in the project's evidence-to-design review, and Chapter 4 did not describe it (Appendix A, correction 37).

#### 5.6.2 Signals that act, and signals that inform

The distinction that matters most is not between strong and weak signals but between signals that inform a reviewer and signals that act on the student. A signal that only informs a reviewer, shown with an honest description of its ceiling, after the content has been graded, does the least harm a weak signal can do. A signal that acts on the student during the examination changes the conditions under which the evidence is gathered, and therefore potentially the evidence, even if it never enters the score.

By that distinction the build has four signals that act: the recitation check, which excludes an answer and asks for it again; the delivery cues, which raise the probing pressure; the gaze fade and the spoken request to look at the examiner; and, outside the EU, the voice measure as one of the delivery cues. The first acts on content and is the best supported. The other three act on signals the evidence says are weak, and two of them do so by the owner's decision.

A further point concerns timing. Section 4.8.2 recorded that the human examiner sees the gaze episodes and recitation flags while grading, because blind grading hides the machine's judgment but not the integrity context. The same is true of the text-style estimate once it passes 0.7: its score is hidden with the exam-time judgments, but the integrity event it becomes is not. The research notes recommended showing integrity context only after the content-based score (Gadfly repository, `docs/research/06-integrity-proctoring-gaze-voice.md`), by the anchoring mechanism of Section 3.8. The build hides the machine's judgment of the content from the blind human examiner and shows the machine's integrity context, which is the weaker of the two kinds of evidence and the one more likely to prime a judgment of the student rather than of the answer.

### 5.7 The owner's decisions and their evidence

The project's owner has made four decisions that the evidence review questions. They are recorded in `docs/EVIDENCE_MAP.md` with the evidence on both sides, and that document does not override them. Neither does this dissertation. Each decision is a choice between values that the evidence informs but does not settle, such as how much integrity protection is worth how much burden on which students, and it belongs to the people accountable for the system. What a dissertation can do is state each decision precisely, set out the evidence for and against it at its verified level, say what the build has done since to soften it, and say what evidence would test it.

#### 5.7.1 Decision (a): voice and camera only, with no typed answers

**The decision.** Examinations are spoken, with the camera on, and there is no typed-answer mode, "to stop cheating."

**The evidence for it.** Unproctored remote examinations are inflated (Alessio et al., 2017; Dendir & Maxwell, 2020), so some protection is justified. A spoken answer, followed up from the student's own words, is harder to paste from another source than a typed one: a typed answer can be produced by copying, a spoken one must at least be read aloud, and reading aloud leaves traces the recitation check can catch. The camera supplies context, such as an absent or second face, that a typed mode would not.

**The evidence against it.** Speech recognition is the largest documented fairness risk in a voice examination (Section 5.3.1), and it falls on identifiable groups: speakers of some dialects and people who stutter (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024). Joughin (2010) recommends alternative arrangements for students for whom anxiety is disabling and adjustments for speech or hearing impairment. A student who cannot use the voice examination, for any of these reasons, has under the decision no route to the assessment at all. Section 2.5.2 concluded that a typed-answer accommodation is "a requirement rather than a convenience" for such students. The integrity argument is weaker than it looks: the strongest integrity control the evidence supports, the follow-up written from the student's own answer, works on typed answers too, and with the camera kept on most of the remaining context would be kept.

**What the build has done since.** Several changes reduce the cost of the decision without changing it: transcript verification, unsure words marked for graders, word error rate by group, an adaptive end-of-answer silence, and a clock that stops during pauses. None helps a student for whom speaking is itself the barrier. The proposed accommodation (GAD-T260), which would leave voice as the default and let an instructor grant typing per student, is held.

**What would test it.** Two measurements bear on the decision. The word error rate by group, once a pilot supplies it, shows how much the voice channel costs the students it serves worst. A comparison of follow-up performance under spoken and typed answers, with and without a relayed source, would show how much integrity the voice channel adds over the follow-up alone. Chapter 7 includes both.

#### 5.7.2 Decision (b): voice strain drives harder probing

**The decision.** Voice strain and stress detection drives harder probing, as the owner asked. Since GAD-T259, which the owner approved, the decision applies outside the EU only.

**The evidence for it.** Pressing a student whose answer came out shaky can bring out whether the concept is really understood, and comparing a student with their own baseline, not with other students, is the least unfair way to read any delivery signal (Section 4.3.5). The build confines the measure to steering questions and informing reviewers, never grading, and hides it from blind graders, which is the least harmful use available. And the evidence rules limit how far any signal can reach the score through the path (Section 5.3.3).

**The evidence against it.** The measure has no demonstrated validity for anything an examination cares about. Voice-stress systems performed at about chance in detecting stress, not only deception (Harnsberger et al., 2009, abstract), the field test found about nine false flags for each true one (Damphousse et al., 2007), and the acoustic correlates of stress are inconsistent, with jitter tending to fall rather than rise where any effect is found (Giddens et al., 2013; Appendix A, correction 25). The baseline is the student's first answers, when warm-up nerves and easier opening questions both differ from later ones. Anxiety, accent, second-language speaking and stuttering change the features measured (Section 2.5), so the students most likely to be pressed harder are the ones the anxiety evidence says need less pressure, not more. The same aim, pressing where an answer is weak, can be met from the content of the answer: hedging, vagueness and missing causal links are text, and the only deception cues with some support concern content (DePaulo et al., 2003). In the EU, the use is exposed to the prohibition on emotion recognition in education (Section 5.8.2), which is why the owner approved switching it off there.

**What the build has done since.** In the EU it is off by default, and when it is off neither the voice comparison nor its stand-in steers probing, though the wait before the first word and hesitations still do. Everywhere, the reviewer sees raw differences rather than a named state. The privacy documentation no longer says that nothing infers emotion. Outside the EU, the measure still raises the probing pressure at 0.5 above the student's baseline.

**What would test it.** The decision makes an empirical prediction: that answers given under raised voice-strain pressure are more often weak on content, so that pressing on them finds gaps that pressing elsewhere would not. Gadfly records the cues that set the pressure and the judged score of the next answer, so the prediction can be tested on pilot data by comparing what harder probing finds when the cue is strain and when it is content. The prediction would also have to hold across groups: if the cue fires more often for anxious, second-language or disfluent speakers at the same level of understanding, the decision trades fairness for whatever the prediction gains.

The decision also creates two regimes. A student marked as living in the EU and one who is not may take the same examination under different examiners: one whose questions can be steered by the sound of their voice, one whose questions cannot. The scoring rule makes it unlikely that this changes their grades much, but the two students do not take the same examination, and their paths are not comparable by the standard of Section 3.6.

#### 5.7.3 Decision (c): the gaze fade and "look at me"

**The decision.** When the student looks away from the examiner while answering, the screen fades and the examiner asks, "Please look at me while you answer."

**The evidence for it.** The use is lawful on the Commission's own example: gaze-point tracking in an online examination "to detect if unauthorized material is used ... is not prohibited, because the system does not identify or infer emotions" (European Commission, 2025, para. 255). Unproctored examinations are inflated, so some deterrent is justified. The build keeps the gaze record at low confidence, never scores it and never penalizes a student for it, which matches what the signal can support.

**The evidence against it.** The signal cannot tell reading notes from thinking (Section 5.6.1). Explaining aloud is slow (Fox et al., 2011), so answers contain long stretches of thinking, and to the extent that students look away while they think, a fade that starts after a few seconds will fire on honest thinking. How often students look away while they think was not among the sources verified, and the project's own description of looking away as "a well-supported sign of thinking" is not supported by them (Appendix A, correction 34); the evidence review's statement that people look away while thinking is cited to Fox et al. (2011), whose meta-analysis concerns the time and accuracy of verbal reports, not gaze (Appendix A, correction 42). The objection does not need the claim: it is enough that the signal cannot tell thinking from reading. The fade adds pressure during the answer, and anxious students already do worse under proctoring (Woldeab & Brothen, 2019). Face and eye detection may fail more often for some groups (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022). Because the fade acts during the answer, it changes the conditions of the examination, and so potentially the evidence, even though the gaze record never enters the score.

**What the build has done since.** GAD-T261, which the owner approved, keeps the decision and makes it fairer: a thinking grace at the start of each answer, an instructor-declared accommodation that switches the fade and the remarks off, and rates by group. The accommodation answers the strongest objection for students known to need it. The grace answers part of the objection about thinking, though the 10-second and 6-second values are design choices, not values from a source.

**What would test it.** The rates by group, once there are enough students to compare, show whether the fade falls more often on some groups. Whether the fade deters anything can be tested only in a study with a relay condition, run with and without the fade. And whether the fade costs honest students anything can be tested by comparing, within students, the judged quality of answers during which the screen faded with those during which it did not.

#### 5.7.4 Decision (d): clearing scores holds the attempt out of grading

**The decision.** When an administrator clears an attempt's scores, the grades, reports, disagreements, reconciliations, approvals and appeals are removed, results are withdrawn, and the attempt is held at "Examination In Progress," a status from which nothing moves it on its own, so that the automatic grading does not grade it again seconds later. The attempt and its transcript are kept, and it still counts as used.

**The evidence for it.** An attempt an administrator has decided to clear should not be re-graded by the machine, unseen, as soon as it is cleared. A person should decide whether and when it is graded, which is what effective human oversight asks (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 14(1)). The action is audited.

**The evidence against it.** Grading a learning outcome is a high-risk use under the Act (Annex III, point 3(b)), and removing the records of how a grade was reached, reconciled, approved and appealed removes the trail that oversight depends on. Clearing also withdraws the released feedback that makes a further attempt worth taking, since repeated testing helps only with feedback, and helps least when students mostly fail (Rowland, 2014), while the cleared attempt still counts against the student's limit.

**What the build has done since.** GAD-T262, which the owner approved, keeps the decision and answers two of the objections: the removed records are copied into an audit record before removal, and the student is told that the scores were cleared for review and that the attempt still counts. The third objection, that the attempt counts against the student's limit while its feedback is withdrawn, stands as part of the decision.

**What would test it.** This decision is less an empirical claim than a choice of procedure, and the relevant evidence is about its use: how often scores are cleared, by whom, for what reasons, and with what outcome for the student. The audit records now hold that information.

#### 5.7.5 What the decisions have in common

Three of the four decisions, (a), (b) and (c), resolve a conflict between integrity and fairness in favour of integrity, and in each case the integrity gain rests on signals or arrangements whose effect on integrity is untested while their cost to some students is documented. The fourth, (d), resolves a conflict between two forms of oversight. In each case the build has since added measurement, an accommodation or an audit trail that reduces the cost without reversing the decision, and in each case the empirical test of Chapter 7 can supply the evidence that is now missing. That is the most this dissertation claims about them: that each is a hypothesis about a trade-off, and that each can be tested.

### 5.8 The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act

The analysis here is descriptive and is not legal advice (Section 1.6). It rests on the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and the Commission's guidelines on prohibited practices that were read in the research pass (Appendix A, Section A.1), and it says so where a duty depends on provisions that were not read.

#### 5.8.1 Classification

A system that evaluates learning outcomes in educational institutions, and one that monitors and detects prohibited behaviour of students during tests, are listed high-risk uses (Annex III, points 3(b) and 3(d)). Gadfly is both: its panel grades objectives, and its integrity signals monitor the examination. Article 6(3) allows a listed system to escape the high-risk category where it does not materially influence the outcome of decision-making, for instance by performing a narrow procedural or purely preparatory task. The panel's grade carries a fixed weight in the combined score (0.4 by default), and its judgments during the examination choose the questions. That is material influence on the outcome, and the exemption is doubtful. The integrity signals are a closer case. They never enter the score, but four of them act on the examination (Section 5.6.2), and the recitation check excludes evidence. A system that shapes the conditions of an assessment is hard to describe as purely preparatory.

#### 5.8.2 The prohibition on emotion recognition

The Act prohibits the use of AI systems "to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons" (Art. 5(1)(f)). An emotion recognition system identifies or infers "emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data" (Art. 3(39)), and biometric data includes data relating to "behavioural characteristics" (Art. 3(34)). Recital 18 lists emotions such as embarrassment and shame and excludes physical states such as pain or fatigue; Recital 44 records "serious concerns about the scientific basis" of such systems. The Commission's non-binding guidelines read "emotions" "in a wide sense" (para. 247), list voice and eye tracking among behavioural biometrics (para. 251), and give the decisive example: if an examination system "is also used to detect emotions, such as emotional arousal and anxiousness, this would fall within the scope of the prohibition" (para. 255). Integrity is not a safety reason, since safety means "protection of life and health and not to protect other interests, for example property against theft or fraud" (para. 258).

Each of Gadfly's signals can be read against this text.

- **Gaze, face and reading-like eye movement** are the guidelines' own example of a use that is not prohibited, because they look for unauthorized material, not emotions.
- **The voice measure** is the exposed one. Its inputs, pitch, jitter and loudness, are standard features of vocal stress. Its use outside the EU, to decide that a student's answer was delivered under strain and should be pressed harder, comes close to detecting "emotional arousal and anxiousness." The relabelling as raw differences does not by itself change the analysis, because the guidelines turn on what the system is used to infer, not on what its output is called. The EU default of GAD-T259 removes the exposure where the markers that trigger it are set. Two questions remain. First, the default turns on an organization's marking of itself as in the EU or EEA and on an instructor's marking of a student as living there; whether these markers track the Act's territorial scope depends on provisions not read here. Second, an administrator can turn the measurements on for everyone, including EU students, an act the project's documentation says should follow legal advice. The project's corrected privacy text now goes further than the guidelines, saying that Gadfly processes features "that the EU AI Act guidelines treat as emotion recognition when used in education"; the guidelines classify by the inference, not by the features, so the more accurate statement is that the features are of the kind from which such an inference would be made (Appendix A, correction 39).
- **The text-based delivery cue** (hedging phrases) is derived from the transcript, not from biometric data, and so falls outside the definition. The timing cues (the wait before the first word, hesitations per hundred words) are derived from the student's speech and are closer to the line. GAD-T259 switched off the voice comparison and its pause-and-speech-rate stand-in for EU students, but not these cues, which still raise the probing pressure in the EU (Appendix A, correction 40). They are used as signs of shaky delivery, not labelled as states, and a long wait or many hesitations is not an emotion in the sense of Recital 18; but they vary with anxiety (Section 2.5), and if they were used to infer it, the guidelines' example would apply to them as well.
- **The text-style estimate** infers whether an answer was composed while speaking, which is a claim about behaviour, not emotion. It does not appear to be within the prohibition, though it is within point 3(d) as monitoring.

#### 5.8.3 Human oversight

High-risk systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons" (Art. 14(1)), and those overseeing them must remain aware of "the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output produced by a high-risk AI system (automation bias)" (Art. 14(4)(b)). Gadfly's grading design maps onto these provisions closely. The human weight cannot be set to zero; the human examiner grades blind by default, which is a direct guard against automation bias, given that human evaluators shown a model's judgment changed their own choice in about a third of cases (Zheng et al., 2023); only a person can approve and publish; and, since GAD-T270, the panel's calibration status is shown to graders, so that they can see when its grades are provisional.

Three features work against effective oversight. Blind grading is a policy default that an examination's policy can turn off. A reconciliation can be recorded, and the grade then approved, by the same person who entered the human score (Appendix A, correction 30), which makes the human side of oversight a single person's judgment ratified by that person. And the human examiner grades with the integrity context in view, which invites the anchoring that blind grading is meant to prevent. The calibration label adds a new consideration: a grader told that the panel is "calibrated for this exam" may defer to it more, which is the automation bias Article 14(4)(b) names. Blindness protects against this during grading; it does not protect the reconciliation that follows.

#### 5.8.4 Duties not reviewed here

Classification as high-risk brings further obligations for providers and deployers of such systems. The project's own privacy documentation names risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight, accuracy documentation and registration before deployment. The provisions that establish these obligations were not read in the research pass, so this dissertation does not state them or assess the build against them. The application dates of the high-risk rules remain an open item: the Regulation as published states that it applies from 2 August 2026 (Art. 113), with later dates for some provisions, and whether amendments since have changed those dates was not checked (Appendix A, Section A.6).

### 5.9 Accountability

Accountability asks whether the people affected by a grade can see how it was reached, contest it, and know who decided. Gadfly's design is strongest on the record and weakest on independence.

**The record.** Every grading action, including every refused attempt, is written to an append-only audit log; the grading policy in force is copied into the grade record; the panel's calibration status is frozen into each session's policy; and since GAD-T262 even cleared scores leave an audit copy. The planner is deterministic and records why each question was asked, so a session can be replayed. The prompt set in force is versioned. Few published uses of language-model grading describe records this complete.

**The student's view.** A student sees a grade only once it is published, and, since GAD-T273, sees for each objective which criteria fell short, why, what the next level looks like, and how the objective's score changed since the earlier attempt. That is the feedback the retrieval-practice literature says a further attempt needs to be worth taking (Rowland, 2014; Dunlosky et al., 2013), and it is specific to what was examined, which is the only material on which a retake will improve (Pan & Rickard, 2018). Where voice measurements are on, the student also sees a short description of their own voice in their live transcript. The student does not see the integrity context recorded about them, which reviewers do see, and so cannot answer it except through an appeal.

**The separation of recommending and deciding.** The panel recommends; a person decides. That separation is real in the arithmetic, since the human weight cannot be zero and only a person can approve. It is weaker in the people. The permission to score is separated from the permission to approve by role, not by person, and the instructor role holds both (Section 4.9). An examination can therefore be graded, reconciled and approved by one person. This is the most important remaining gap in the accountability of the design, because it is the one the record cannot repair: an audit log that shows the same name in every row records the absence of independent judgment, but does not supply it.

### 5.10 Summary

This chapter has asked what a score from a synthetic Socratic examination may be taken to mean. Its answers bear on three of the research questions of Section 1.4.

On comparability (RQ2), the method as committed now meets almost every requirement the model sets for keeping the path out of the score, and reports the number of questions each objective received. What it does not yet report is how much of each objective the score covers, and what it does not yet measure is whether the signals that steer the path fire more often for some groups.

On combining machine and human judgment (RQ3), the method now gates trust in its panel by aggregate calibration, applies the thresholds to sets of answers as the evidence requires, and grades the human blind by default. The remaining weaknesses are that calibration informs rather than constrains the panel's weight, that self-preference is untested, and that one person may judge, reconcile and approve.

On integrity signals (RQ4), the evidence and the law agree on the shape of the answer. Signals that inform a reviewer, at an honest confidence and after the content has been graded, do little harm. Signals that act on the student during the examination change the evidence, and need evidence of their own. The examination's follow-up questions are the best-supported integrity control, but the support is indirect, and the claim that they defeat relayed answers remains a hypothesis. Of the signals that act, the recitation check is supported; the delivery cues, the gaze fade and, outside the EU, the voice measure are not, and the last is the one most exposed under the Act. Three of the four owner's decisions trade a documented cost to some students for an untested integrity gain. The dissertation records them as decisions, not errors, and Chapter 7 designs the study that would test each.

None of the remaining shortfalls is a matter of model capability. Every change made to Gadfly on the day this chapter describes was procedural: an evidence rule, a label, a threshold, an accommodation, an audit record. That is the thesis of Chapter 1 seen once more: the soundness of the evidence depends on conditions the method and the institution must choose to meet. Chapter 6 sets out the limits of the argument that has led here.

## Chapter 6 — Limitations and Objections

The argument of Chapters 3 to 5 is that a synthetic Socratic examination can gather evidence fit to inform a grade under conditions the literature makes explicit, and that Gadfly meets many of those conditions and fails some for procedural reasons. This chapter sets out the limits of that argument: what it rests on, what it cannot show, and the objections a reader may fairly raise. Sections 6.1 to 6.7 state the limitations. Section 6.8 states the objections and the replies the dissertation can give, and Section 6.9 summarizes.

### 6.1 No empirical evaluation

The dissertation reports no study. It states a model, describes a system against it, and designs a test (Chapter 7). Every claim about what Gadfly's scores mean is therefore a claim about what they would mean if the conditions held, not a finding that they do.

The point has sharpened since Chapter 4 was written. The changes described in Section 5.2 added a set of instruments to the build: a calibration gate for the grading panel, a probe of the panel with padded and less fluent paraphrases, word error rates by group, gaze episode rates by group, and a count of read-out flags. Each is the right instrument for a question Chapter 2 raised, and none has yet been run on real examinations. An instrument that exists but has not been used is easy to mistake for evidence, especially in a system's own documentation and interface: a grading page that can show a panel's calibration status invites the reader to assume there is one. Until the project's double-marked pilot has been run (Gadfly repository, `docs/PILOT.md`), there is no agreement figure for the panel, no word error rate for any group, no rate of false read-out flags and no fade rate by group, and no expected value for any of them should be quoted.

### 6.2 Evidence from neighbouring tasks

No study found in the research pass tests language models grading spoken, transcribed Socratic answers, multi-turn follow-ups, or per-objective scoring in an oral examination (Section 2.12). The evidence on machine judgment comes from neighbouring tasks: pairwise preferences between chat responses (Zheng et al., 2023), question answering and chatbot ranking (Verga et al., 2024), short written answers (Henkel et al., 2024), reading comprehension (Henkel et al., 2023), essays (Mizumoto & Eguchi, 2023; Pack et al., 2024) and handwritten physics solutions (Kortemeyer, 2023). Three differences limit how far that evidence carries.

- **The evidence is a transcript.** The judges read the output of a speech recognizer, which differs by speaker group (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024). No study above had a recognizer between the student and the judge.
- **The evidence is a conversation.** An answer to a follow-up depends on the question, which depends on the previous answer. A judge that scores each answer alone, as Gadfly's does during the examination, and a grader that reads the answers to one objective together, as Gadfly's does afterwards, face a task none of these studies posed.
- **The evidence is spoken explanation.** Spoken answers contain false starts, fillers and repairs that written answers do not, and the instruction to ignore them has not been tested (Section 5.3.2).

The same limit applies to the human baseline. The figures for human agreement come from essay marking (Shermis, 2014; Meadows & Billington, 2005), and agreement between human examiners of spoken Socratic answers may be higher or lower. The acceptance thresholds Gadfly now applies were set for automated essay scoring in large-scale testing (Williamson et al., 2012, as cited in Casabianca et al., 2023), and their suitability for this task is itself an assumption.

### 6.3 Sources read at the abstract level

Forty-three of the 83 works in the reference list were read only at the abstract or publisher-page level, and one only at the record level (Appendix A). The rule that a claim attributed to such a work is confined to what its abstract establishes keeps the dissertation from misreporting them, but it does not make abstracts into full evidence. Four works carry more weight than an abstract can bear comfortably (`references/UNRETRIEVED.md`, Section 5):

- **Wass et al. (2003)** supplies the variance components behind the argument that reliability comes from sampling (Sections 2.4.2 and 3.7) and the illustration in Section 3.7. If the full paper qualified the components, for instance by the design of the orals or the estimation method, the shape of the argument would survive, since van der Vleuten et al. (2010), read in the relevant sections, makes the same point, but the illustration would need to change.
- **Koenecke et al. (2020)** supplies the size of the racial gap in speech recognition. The direction of the finding is not in doubt, but its size for the recognizers Gadfly uses is unknown in any case, and the full paper might show conditions under which the gap narrows or widens.
- **Huxham et al. (2012)** supplies the finding that students scored higher in oral than in written formats with no group visibly disadvantaged. The dissertation already treats the second half as too weak to rely on (Appendix A, correction 9).
- **Fox et al. (2011)** supplies the finding that explaining to a listener is reactive and that all verbal reporting slows people down, on which Sections 2.3.2, 4.3.5 and 5.7.3 rely. The finding is stated in the abstract in the terms the dissertation uses, but the moderators and the conditions of the studies were not read.

Other works read at the abstract level bear on single points rather than on the argument: the meta-analyses of anxiety (Hembree, 1988; von der Embse et al., 2018), the studies of voice-stress analysis (Harnsberger et al., 2009; Giddens et al., 2013), and the studies of language models scoring student work (Section 2.9.2). In each case reading the full text could qualify a figure but is unlikely to reverse a direction. The one case in which it could reverse a direction is Caffrey et al. (2008), whose finding that dynamic assessment predicted best when help was not contingent on the student's response supports the fixed hint ladder; if the full review qualified that finding, the argument for the ladder would rest on comparability alone, which is enough for it (Section 2.8.2).

Six primary works are cited only through secondary sources, and the measurement-theory literature on which Chapter 3's algebra draws was not located at all (Appendix A, Sections A.3 and A.7). The second is the larger limitation. Chapter 3 uses the forms of expected information gain, a threshold response model, optional stopping and a generalizability coefficient without the literature in which they are standard. The model's requirements are stated so that they follow from its own definitions, but its relation to item response theory, computerized adaptive testing and generalizability theory, which would let it be fitted to data, is unestablished.

### 6.4 Populations the evidence does not cover

The evidence reviewed covers some students well and others not at all.

- **Neurodivergent students.** No source on neurodivergent students in oral or voice examinations was verified (Section 2.12). Several of Gadfly's mechanisms act on behaviour that differs systematically for some neurodivergent students: eye contact, which the gaze fade enforces; the timing of speech, which the delivery cues and the read-out pattern read; and the confrontational move of the elenchus. The eye-contact accommodation and the extra-time suppression of the read-out pattern give instructors a remedy for students known to need it, but a remedy that depends on a declared accommodation does not reach students who have not declared one, and the dissertation can say nothing about the size of the effect.
- **Students who cannot use the voice channel.** Under decision (a) there is no route for a student for whom speaking, or being recorded, is the barrier (Section 5.7.1). The evidence on speech recognition covers dialect and stuttering; it says nothing about students with other speech or hearing impairments, or about students taking an examination in a language they speak with limited fluency.
- **Adults in dynamic assessment.** The dynamic-assessment evidence comes from young children, students with disabilities, and decoding and reasoning tasks (Section 2.8.3). Its application to adults answering conceptual questions aloud, which is what Gadfly's Learning Mode does, is untested.
- **Languages other than English.** Every source on speech recognition, judging and anxiety that the dissertation cites concerns English, except the work on second-language anxiety, which concerns learners of a foreign language (Horwitz et al., 1986). The judges' instructions, the hedging phrases the delivery cue looks for, and the recitation check's word-overlap thresholds are all stated for English.
- **Students without stable equipment or a private room.** A voice-and-camera examination assumes a working microphone, a camera, a connection and a room in which a second voice, a second face or a glance at a noise will not be recorded. The clock now stops during pauses the student did not cause (GAD-T263), but nothing in the evidence reviewed measures who is disadvantaged by the requirement itself, and the camera-based evidence of disparities (Yoder-Himes et al., 2022) concerns skin tone, not circumstance.

### 6.5 The examiner changes the examined

Explaining to a listener is the reactive kind of verbal report: it changes performance, usually for the better, and slows it down (Fox et al., 2011; Ericsson & Simon, 1998). A Socratic examination is that kind of report. Three consequences limit the reading of a session as a measurement.

First, the student's state may change during the session. An objective examined late may be examined after the student has, in explaining earlier objectives, come to understand more. The evidence rules treat answers as evidence about a fixed state (Section 3.9), so a level reached late is reported as if the student brought it in. That is a misdescription, though not obviously an unfair one: understanding reached by explaining under questioning is still understanding, and a grade that credits it measures something real. It does mean that the order of objectives, which the instructor sets, can change what is reported.

Second, the examiner's manner may change the student's capacity to answer. The model treats the probability that a student who understands will answer adequately as a property of the student (Section 3.3); the *Apology* records that the elenchus made those examined hostile (Plato, 1966, 21d), and the anxiety evidence suggests that a confrontational examiner may lower that probability for anxious students (Section 5.3.4). Gadfly's examiner is told never to correct, confirm or remark on delivery, and its contradiction question is neutral in form, but how it is experienced was not studied.

Third, the examination is also a learning event (Section 2.6). Released results now give per-objective feedback (GAD-T273), and the retrieval-practice evidence supports the claim that a further attempt with feedback will improve performance on what was examined (Rowland, 2014; Pan & Rickard, 2018). The same fact limits what a second attempt's score means: an improvement may reflect learning from the first attempt's questions and feedback rather than from study, and it will be concentrated on what the first attempt probed. Reporting the score change between attempts, as the build now does, is useful to the student. Treating it as a measure of learning would need its own validation (Section 2.7.2), and the dissertation does not claim one.

### 6.6 Dependence on model providers

Gadfly's examiner and grader are panels of models from four commercial providers. Three limits follow.

**Drift.** Model behaviour changes over time. In the one study of the reviewed set that examined it, interrater reliability "decreased slightly over time" and "all models are subject to fluctuations in their performance" (Pack et al., 2024, abstract). A calibration set that accepted a panel for an examination is evidence about the models as they were when it was run. In the build, a calibration is recorded against a panel name supplied when it is evaluated, and nothing found in the build reopens it when a provider changes the model behind that name. A calibration should be treated as expiring, and re-run when any member changes.

**Reproducibility.** The planner and the evidence rules are deterministic and can be replayed (Sections 4.3.1 and 4.5). The panels are not: the grader samples at a low temperature, and the same answer judged twice by the same provider may receive different levels. The build records the prompt-set version, the members' votes and their dissent, which is what an audit needs, but it cannot reproduce a judgment exactly. Disagreements between members, which the panel design exists to average out, also make any single replay differ from the original.

**Correlated change.** The four providers are separate companies, but their models are trained on overlapping data by similar methods, and the formal case for a panel (Section 3.8) assumes that the biases of different families are partly independent. If the providers' models converge, the independence on which the panel's advantage rests shrinks, and nothing in the build would show it, short of the bias probes and calibration being re-run.

### 6.7 Authorship and the case

This dissertation was written in collaboration with a language model, and its research notes were prepared by language-model agents. A dissertation about synthetic examiners written with a synthetic collaborator invites the objection that its author has an interest in the conclusion and its collaborator a tendency to agree with whatever is asked. The verification ledger is the answer the dissertation gives: every source is recorded at the level at which it was read, every quotation was seen, and every correction is kept (Section 1.7). The ledger does not remove the limitation. It makes it checkable.

A second limitation concerns the case. Gadfly is developed by the organization for which the dissertation is written, and the dissertation's descriptions of it were checked against code written in part by the same kind of agents that helped write the dissertation. Two things temper this. The descriptions were checked against the code, not against the project's documentation, and the check has repeatedly found the documentation wrong: corrections 30 to 37 and 40 are cases in which the project's own descriptions of its system, or Chapter 4's, said more or less than the code does. And the system changed while it was described. Fourteen of the changes Chapter 4 proposed were made on the day Chapter 4 was written (Section 5.2), in part in response to the same evidence review. A dissertation that describes a system its own evidence is changing cannot be a neutral observer of it. What it can be is explicit about which commit it describes, which this one is.

### 6.8 Objections and replies

The objections below are those a reader is most likely to raise. Each is stated in its strongest form, and the reply says how far the dissertation can answer it.

**"An oral examination is inherently unfair to anxious, second-language and disfluent students."** The evidence supports the premise: anxiety is associated with lower scores (Hembree, 1988; von der Embse et al., 2018), and speech recognition errs more for some groups (Koenecke et al., 2020; Mujtaba et al., 2024). The reply is that the evidence supports a weaker conclusion: an oral examination is unfair to these students to the extent that its design adds pressure and treats recognizer error as content, and both can be reduced. Gadfly reduces some (verified transcripts, adaptive silence, a clock that stops during pauses, unsure words marked for graders) and, by decision, adds others (Section 5.7). Whether what remains is acceptable is not something the evidence reviewed can decide. The dissertation does not claim that a Socratic oral examination is fairer than other formats, and the evidence would not support that claim.

**"A machine examiner can be gamed."** Graders can be manipulated by instructions hidden in student work (Milani et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2024), and a spoken answer can carry them as easily as a written one. The reply is that Gadfly treats the transcript as untrusted data, gives no credit for text addressed to the grader and sends it to a human, and never lets the panel's grade stand alone. These are the controls the evidence supports. They have not been tested against a determined student, and Chapter 7 should include an adversarial condition.

**"A student can receive answers through an earpiece and speak them as their own."** This is the strongest form of the integrity objection, and the dissertation cannot answer it with evidence. The camera will not see an earpiece, the audio signal for a second voice will not hear one, and the recitation check will not fire on an answer spoken naturally. The only control is the follow-up question, and the claim that follow-up defeats relayed answers is a hypothesis with no direct support in the sources reviewed (Section 5.5). The relay condition of Chapter 7 is the test.

**"The human examiner will rubber-stamp the machine."** Human evaluators shown a model's judgment changed their own choice in about a third of cases (Zheng et al., 2023), and the Act names automation bias as something overseers must guard against (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 14(4)(b)). The reply is that blind grading by default is a direct guard: the human enters a score before seeing the panel's, so the disagreement between them is measurable and large ones block finalization. The guard is configurable, and it does not extend to reconciliation, which may be done by the same person who entered the human score (Section 5.9). The objection is answered for grading and open for reconciliation.

**"The standard of truth is a rubric a machine wrote."** The rubric for each concept is written by the panel from the instructor's material, and the instructor may edit it but need not approve it before an examination runs on it (Appendix A, correction 35). A grade can therefore rest on a standard no person has read. The reply is that every rubric point is tied to passages of the instructor's material, is versioned, and is recorded with every judgment made under it, so an error in it is recoverable. But recoverable is not the same as reviewed, and the simplest change that would answer the objection, requiring approval before a summative examination, has not been made.

**"Why not examine students in person, on paper?"** Nothing in this dissertation shows that a synthetic Socratic examination is a better measure than a supervised written examination, and the evidence review states that the evidence does not let the project claim it. The dissertation's question is narrower: whether a Socratic oral examination, which has properties a written one lacks, can be conducted at a scale at which oral examination has never been practical, and what the evidence it gathers would then be fit for. A supervised written examination is an alternative, not an answer to that question.

**"The formal model is decoration."** Chapter 3's algebra is used without the measurement literature in which it is standard (Section 6.3), and the model has not been fitted to data. The reply is that the model's work in this dissertation is to derive requirements, not to estimate parameters, and that each requirement follows from its definitions and was checked against code. Whether the model predicts anything is a question for Chapter 7.

### 6.9 Summary

The argument of this dissertation rests on evidence from neighbouring tasks, on a set of sources of which about half were read at the abstract level, and on a description of a single system that changed while it was described. It reports no study. It cannot say how accurate Gadfly's panel is, how much its recognizer disadvantages any group, how often its integrity signals misfire, or whether its follow-up questions defeat relayed answers, and it cannot speak for neurodivergent students, students who cannot speak the examination, or examinations in other languages. What it can say is what each of those questions would take to answer. The next chapter designs the study.

### A.4 Corrections made to the working draft

Numbered, cumulative and never renumbered. Items 1 to 28 were found by the research pass (GAD-T254) in popular retellings of the literature or in the project's own documentation, and the dissertation was drafted to avoid them; items from 29 were found while drafting.

1. **The elenchus shows inconsistency, not falsity.** A contradiction probe tests whether a student's claims fit together; it cannot decide which is wrong (Section 2.2).
2. **There is no single "Socratic method."** Vlastos's standard elenchus fits only part of the dialogues (Wolfsdorf, 2013).
3. **The *Meno* slave-boy passage is not a model of non-leading questions.** Many questions at 84d–85b lead; Piaget's rule against suggestion is the better model (Sections 2.2, 2.3).
4. **"Thinking aloud changes reasoning" is overstated.** Plain think-aloud did not change accuracy; explaining to a listener did, and every form of verbalizing slowed people down (Fox et al., 2011).
5. **Nisbett and Wilson (1977) did not show verbal reports are worthless.** Reports can be accurate when the cause is salient and plausible.
6. **"Someone relaying another source's answers tends to contradict themselves"** has no support in the sources reviewed. It is treated as a hypothesis (Sections 1.6, 2.12).
7. **"Structure makes oral exams reliable" is overstated.** Reliability comes mostly from sampling; in Wass et al. (2003) content accounted for more score variance than examiners.
8. **The accent-credibility effect is not settled.** Replications of the finding usually cited for it are mixed; the dissertation does not rely on it.
9. **Huxham et al. (2012) does not show that anxiety has no effect on oral scores.**
10. **"Test anxiety causes poor performance" (Hembree, 1988)** is a causal reading of mostly correlational data; the stronger evidence is that scores rise when anxiety is treated.
11. **Roediger and Karpicke (2006) shows retention, not understanding.** Restudying led at five minutes, and the final test was free recall of the same passage.
12. **The testing effect can vanish** without feedback when practice-test success is 50% or lower (Rowland, 2014).
13. **Testing benefits do not spread to untested material** (Pan & Rickard, 2018).
14. **Chi et al. (1989) is correlational,** and answering an examiner is explaining to another, not self-explanation in the strict sense.
15. **Bloom's taxonomy is not a strict ladder,** and a "what would change if" question inside the student's own scenario is extrapolation, not application.
16. **The ZPD passage says "more capable peers."** "More knowledgeable other" is not in it, and the term "scaffolding" is not Vygotsky's. The 1978 text is an edited translation.
17. **"Dynamic assessment predicts better than static tests" is overstated** (Caffrey et al., 2008; Fuchs et al., 2011).
18. **Prompt counts and gain scores are not "learning potential"** when students start at different levels (De Beer, 2006).
19. **Zheng et al. (2023) does not show self-enhancement bias,** and its headline agreement figure is for pairwise preferences with ties excluded.
20. **"Machines grade essays as well as humans" needs its context:** human–human agreement was only 0.61–0.85, and construct validity and gaming were left open (Shermis, 2014).
21. **The .70 / .10 / .15 thresholds apply to a set of answers, not to one answer** (Williamson et al., 2012, as cited in Casabianca et al., 2023).
22. **Liang et al. (2023) is about detectors, not graders.**
23. **Gender Shades measured gender classification, not face detection.** The direct proctoring evidence is Yoder-Himes et al. (2022).
24. **The webcam tracker's error of about four degrees** was measured against a commercial tracker with four participants; it does not mean webcam tracking matches a commercial eye tracker.
25. **Jitter tends to fall, not rise, under stress** where any effect is found, and findings are mixed (Giddens et al., 2013).
26. **The voice-stress "15%" in the NIJ field study** is the average share of lies detected, not overall accuracy (Damphousse et al., 2007).
27. **Dendir and Maxwell (2020) inferred cheating from a score drop;** they did not observe it.
28. **The project's privacy documentation says "Nothing infers emotion."** The Commission's guidelines give detecting "emotional arousal and anxiousness" during examinations as an example of prohibited emotion recognition in education (Section 2.11).
29. **Memon et al. (2010) does not support a cost argument.** The first draft of Section 1.1 said institutions abandoned oral examinations partly because of their cost. The abstract, the level at which the work was read, says only that some countries moved away from oral examinations in postgraduate medical certification because of continuing concerns about reliability and fairness. The cost point now rests on the testing time estimated by van der Vleuten et al. (2010).
30. **Large grading disagreements need not be reconciled by a second reviewer.** Section 1.2 said that large disagreements block finalization "until a second reviewer reconciles them." In the build, the block is real, but the second reviewer is optional: any holder of the permission to reconcile (the instructor and administrator roles) can record the cause and resolution, and nothing prevents the person who entered the human score from reconciling and then approving the grade. Section 1.2 now says so, and Section 4.9 records the shortfall against requirement M13.
31. **Objectives do not open with the same question.** The outline of Chapter 4 and the project's evidence-to-design review (`docs/EVIDENCE_MAP.md`) described "a fixed opening question per objective." The build fixes the opening move (Elicit) and the rubric, but each opening question is newly worded by the panel. Section 4.3.2 describes the build; the stronger condition of Section 2.4.3 is not met.
32. **Close is not a move the examiner chooses.** Section 1.2 listed Close among the moves the examiner chooses. The planner never issues it: an objective ends when the evidence rules settle it or a limit closes it. Section 1.2 now says so (Sections 4.3.3, 4.5).
33. **"Integrity signals ... never enter the grade" needed qualifying.** Section 1.2 said so without qualification. No signal enters the score arithmetic, but in the build delivery cues, including voice strain, steer how hard the next question presses (owner decision (b)); the gaze fade and spoken remark act on the student during answers (owner decision (c)); and an answer flagged by the recitation check is left out of the evidence. Section 1.2 now says so, and Sections 4.3.5 and 4.8 examine each.
34. **"Looking away is a well-supported sign of thinking" is not supported by the verified ledger.** The project's reviewer-facing explanations (`Gadfly.Server/Review/IntegrityExplanations.cs`) describe looking up or away while answering as "a well-supported sign of thinking." No source in the research pass addresses gaze aversion during thinking; the verified evidence that explaining aloud slows people down (Fox et al., 2011) is about time, not gaze. The dissertation does not repeat the claim (Section 4.8.2), and the literature on it is added to the open items (A.7).
35. **The instructor's review of the concept map and rubrics is possible, not required.** Section 1.2 said "the instructor reviews and edits both." The build lets the instructor edit both and protects the edits, but no step was found that requires approval before an examination runs on them. Section 1.2 now says so (Section 4.2).
36. **The Contradiction move does not accept a resolution in either direction.** The outline of Chapter 4 said the move "accepts a resolution in either direction." In the build the student's resolution is judged on the judge's four criteria, with accuracy against the source weighted most, and a resolution judged below 0.5 caps the objective's level at Basic Explanation as a failure to reconcile. A coherent resolution toward the claim the source contradicts is therefore counted both as an error of accuracy and as incoherence. Section 4.4 records the shortfall against requirement M7.
37. **Gadfly records more integrity signals than Chapter 4 described, among them an estimate of machine-like text.** Section 4.8 described three kinds of signal (recitation, gaze and voice). The build also records browser events (tab hidden, focus lost, full screen left, an extra screen, pasting, copying the question), audio events from the recognizer (a second voice, whispering, a reading-aloud rhythm), a camera episode in which the face in view no longer resembles the face at the start, and a panel estimate of how likely an answer "was read aloud from AI-generated or written text rather than composed while speaking," compared with the student's other answers (`Gadfly.Sources/TextStyleAssessor.cs`); at 0.7 or more it becomes an integrity event that the human examiner sees while grading. None enters the score. The text-style estimate is a detector of machine-style text applied to transcribed speech, and it appears neither in Chapter 4 nor in the project's evidence-to-design review. Sections 5.2.2 and 5.6.1 describe it against the evidence on detectors (Liang et al., 2023; Weber-Wulff et al., 2023).
38. **A minimum of four objectives for summative use is a setting, not an estimate.** GAD-T267 added `MinSummativeObjectives`, 4 by default, with the explanation that one short oral samples too little content to be reliable. The principle is supported (van der Vleuten et al., 2010; Wass et al., 2003), but no source gives four objectives as sufficient for any target reliability, and Section 3.7 holds that the number can come only from a double-marked pilot of the system itself. Section 5.2.2 records M11 as partly met.
39. **The guidelines classify emotion recognition by what is inferred, not by the features used.** After GAD-T259, the project's privacy documentation says that Gadfly "does process features that the EU AI Act guidelines treat as emotion recognition when used in education." The guidelines' example turns on the system being "used to detect emotions, such as emotional arousal and anxiousness" (European Commission, 2025, para. 255); they do not classify acoustic features as such. The accurate statement is that the voice measurements use features of the kind from which such an inference is made, and that steering questions by them comes close to making it (Section 5.8.2). The correction makes the project's statement more precise, not less cautious.
40. **With voice measurements off, the voice still steers probing through timing.** The project's privacy documentation, its evidence-to-design review and the GAD-T259 commit say that when voice measurements are off (by default in the EU) nothing about the voice steers how hard the examiner probes. In the code, the voice comparison and its pause-and-speech-rate stand-in are switched off, but the wait before the first word and hesitations per hundred words, both measured from the student's speech by the recognizer, still raise the probing pressure (`Gadfly/Engine/AnswerDelivery.cs`). Sections 5.2.1 and 5.8.2 describe the build.
41. **Wang et al. (2024) does not show that judgments are least stable at grade boundaries.** The reconciliation triggers of GAD-T270 (`Gadfly/Assessment/ReconciliationTriggers.cs`) and the synthesis of research note 05 cite Wang et al. for judgments being least stable "near a cut." Wang et al. found that pairwise verdicts were least stable when the two answers were close in quality. The boundary trigger is a reasonable precaution by analogy, not a finding (Section 5.4.1).
42. **"People look away while thinking" is cited to a source that does not concern gaze.** The project's evidence-to-design review (mechanic 4) supports the statement "People look away while thinking, and explaining aloud slows them down" with Fox et al. (2011), whose meta-analysis concerns the reactivity and time cost of verbal reports. It supports the second clause only. This is the same gap as item 34, in the project's research documentation rather than its interface (Section 5.7.3).
