# Research note 01: The Socratic elenchus as examination, the clinical interview and think-aloud

Prepared 2026-11-21 for Gadfly (GAD-T254). Gadfly takes its name and its method from Socrates. It asks "what is it?", writes each follow-up from the student's own previous answer, keeps a ledger of the student's claims and confronts the student with two of them that conflict ("can both things be true?"), and asks a neutral clarifying question when an answer is unclear. This note looks at three bodies of work behind those choices. The first is the philosophical literature on the elenchus, especially Vlastos's "problem of the elenchus": does Socratic refutation test knowledge, or only consistency? The second is Piaget's clinical method and Ginsburg's modern restatement of it, which turn "follow the child's own answer and do not suggest" into a research discipline. The third is the psychology of verbal reports: Ericsson and Simon's protocol analysis, Nisbett and Wilson's warning about introspection, and the Fox, Ericsson and Best meta-analysis on whether verbalizing changes reasoning. Every source below was opened during preparation of this note. The level of checking differs from source to source and is stated in each entry. Project Gutenberg was down for maintenance at retrieval time, so the Plato passages come from the Perseus Digital Library's English translations.

How to read the verification levels:
- **Full text read**: the cited passage was checked in the work itself; the entry names the section or page.
- **Abstract/publisher page read**: claims are limited to what the abstract or record page says. Arguments, results and caveats in the body were not checked.
- **Record located only**: a bibliographic record (DOI, index entry) was confirmed, but the work itself was not read. Anything said about the content is labeled as coming from another source.

## A. The Socratic elenchus: what it examines

- **Verified quote or claim:**

- **What it establishes:** In Plato's account, Socrates' questioning is aimed at people who claim or are reputed to have wisdom, and its stated result is to expose a gap between thinking one knows and knowing. - **Relevance to Gadfly:** It supports the product's framing (Elicit "what is X?", then test the claim) and its namesake. It also flags a cost: in the text the examination makes the examined "hateful" toward the examiner (21d). Gadfly's contradiction move should be worded so that it tests the claim without humiliating the student, a point the oral-assessment anxiety literature (note 02) also bears on.
- **Confidence:** High. Primary text read directly at the cited Stephanus sections.

### PlatoMeno
- **APA:** Plato. (1967). *Meno* (W. R. M. Lamb, Trans.). In *Plato in twelve volumes* (Vol. 3). Harvard University Press; William Heinemann. Perseus Digital Library, Perseus:text:1999.01.0178.
- **Link:** https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0178%3Atext%3DMeno%3Asection%3D82b (and sections 82e, 84a, 84b, 84c, 84d, 85a, 85b, 85c, 85d)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read for 82b, 82e and 84a-85d of the slave-boy passage. Sections 83a-83e were not opened. Edition statement as printed by Perseus: "Plato in Twelve Volumes, Vol. 3 translated by W.R.M. Lamb ... 1967."
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - 82e: "Do you observe, Meno, that I am not teaching the boy anything, but merely asking him each time?"
  - 84a-b: "at any rate he thought he knew then, and confidently answered as though he knew, and was aware of no difficulty; whereas now he feels the difficulty he is in, and besides not knowing does not think he knows."
  - 84d: "be on the watch to see if at any point you find me teaching him or expounding to him, instead of questioning him on his opinions."
  - 85a: "Has not each of the inside lines cut off half of each of these four spaces? Boy Yes."
  - 85c: "if he were repeatedly asked these same questions in a variety of forms, you know he will have in the end as exact an understanding of them as anyone."
- **What it establishes:** The passage is the classic picture of questioning that draws a correct answer out of a learner. It also separates confident-but-wrong belief, felt perplexity (the "torpedo's shock"), and true opinion that is not yet knowledge. Asking the same matter "in a variety of forms" is offered as the route from true opinion to understanding.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Two design points. First, it supports Gadfly's habit of re-asking a concept through different moves (Probe, Counterexample, Apply/Transfer), because one correct answer may be only true opinion. Second, it is a caution for the non-hinting rule: many of Socrates' questions in 84d-85b are leading yes/no questions (85a is an example), so the passage that names the ideal of "not teaching" also shows how easily questioning slips into supplying the answer. "Aporia reached" as a Close condition fits 84a-c, where perplexity is treated as progress rather than failure.
- **Confidence:** High. Primary text read at the cited sections; the observation about leading questions is this note's own reading of those sections.

### Vlastos1983
- **APA:** Vlastos, G. (1983). The Socratic elenchus. *Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy, 1*, 27-58. Revised version reprinted in G. Vlastos, *Socratic studies* (M. Burnyeat, Ed.), Cambridge University Press, 1994.
- **Link:** https://sites.temple.edu/dwolf/files/2020/06/Socratic-philosophizing.pdf (Wolfsdorf2013, which quotes Vlastos with page numbers); https://aporia.byu.edu/pdfs/potter-the_elenchus_and_ineradicable_truth.pdf (Potter, D. (1993), *Aporia* 3, which quotes Vlastos with page numbers); https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/does-socrates-have-a-method-rethinking-the-elenchus-in-plato-s-dialogues-and-beyond/ (O'Connor's NDPR review, 2002); https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=20325 (Gerson's BMCR review 1994.10.04 of *Socratic Studies*)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Record located only. The article itself was not opened (no open copy found; Crossref returns only the separate 1982 *Journal of Philosophy* abstract, doi:10.2307/2026548). Volume, year and pages (1, 1983, 27-58) are confirmed by Potter's bibliography and Gerson's BMCR review. Vlastos's wording below is quoted as it appears in Wolfsdorf2013 and Potter 1993, both of which give his page numbers. It was not checked against the original.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Definition, as quoted in Wolfsdorf2013 (citing Vlastos p. 30): "a search for moral truth by adversary argument in which a thesis is debated only if asserted as the answerer's own belief and is regarded as refuted only if the negation of his thesis is deduced from his own beliefs."
  - The "standard elenchus", as laid out in Wolfsdorf2013 (citing p. 39): "(1) Socrates' interlocutor asserts a thesis p, which Socrates considers false and targets for refutation. (2) Socrates secures the interlocutor's agreement to a premise set Q ... (3) Argument is from Q not to it. (4) Socrates argues and the interlocutor agrees that Q entails not-p. (5) Socrates claims that not-p has been proven true, and thus p false."
  - The problem, as quoted in Potter 1993 and Wolfsdorf2013 (citing p. 49): "how is it that Socrates claims to have proved a thesis false when, in point of logic, all he has proved in any given argument is that the thesis is inconsistent with the conjunction of agreed-upon premises for which no reason has been given in that argument?"
  - Vlastos's solution, as quoted in Potter 1993 (citing p. 52), relies on the assumption "Any one who ever has a false moral belief will always have at the same time true beliefs entailing the negation of that false belief." Paraphrase of Wolfsdorf2013: Vlastos himself, after Kraut's criticism, conceded in "Afterthoughts" (1983b) that this picture fits only the *Gorgias*.
  - "Double objective", as quoted in Wolfsdorf2013 (citing p. 37): "to discover how every human being ought to live and to test the single human being that is doing the answering".
- **What it establishes:** Vlastos set the agenda for later work. He held that an elenctic argument, taken on its own logic, shows only that the answerer's thesis is inconsistent with other things the answerer has agreed to. Getting from there to "the thesis is false" needs strong extra assumptions about the answerer's other beliefs being true, and Vlastos later admitted those assumptions are attested mainly in one dialogue. The "sincere assent" rule, that only the answerer's own beliefs count, is part of his definition.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** This bears directly on the Contradiction move ("can both things be true?"). When a student's two claims conflict, the logic alone does not say which claim is wrong, or whether the student understands the concept. It says only that the student's stated set of claims is not jointly coherent. Gadfly's judge should therefore score a detected contradiction as evidence about coherence and confidence, and use the rubric and source material, not the contradiction itself, to decide which side is correct. The "own belief" rule also supports grounding the ledger in what the student actually said, rather than in premises the examiner supplied and the student merely nodded to.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The citation and the content come from two independent secondary sources that agree and give page numbers, but the original was not read.

### Benson1995
- **APA:** Benson, H. H. (1995). The dissolution of the problem of the elenchus. In C. C. W. Taylor (Ed.), *Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy* (Vol. 13, pp. 45-112). Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198250005.003.0004
- **Link:** https://academic.oup.com/book/54602/chapter/422626993 (publisher chapter page); Crossref record; https://sites.temple.edu/dwolf/files/2020/06/Socratic-philosophizing.pdf (Wolfsdorf2013 summary)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (Oxford Academic chapter page: editor, year, pages 45-112, DOI and abstract). The volume number 13 comes from search-result listings, not from the publisher page. The argument is known through Wolfsdorf2013's summary; the chapter body is paywalled and was not read.
- **Verified quote or claim:** Paraphrase of the publisher abstract: the chapter starts from the common reading of the Socrates of the early dialogues as a purely destructive questioner who asks for definitions of virtues and reaches no conclusions. Paraphrase of Wolfsdorf2013's summary: after examining the elenchi in *Euthyphro*, *Charmides* and *Laches*, Benson concludes that Socrates shows only that the interlocutor's beliefs are inconsistent, so Vlastos's problem does not arise in those texts. Benson also calls it a "dogma" that the elenchus is Socrates' only method of argument (Wolfsdorf's quotation of Benson p. 109: "the elenchus is Socrates' only method of argument").
- **What it establishes:** It sets out the "dissolution" view. On this view the elenchus never claimed to prove a thesis false. It tests whether the interlocutor has the knowledge he professes, and inconsistency among his beliefs is enough to show that he does not.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** This is the reading that fits Gadfly best. A detected contradiction in the claim ledger is good evidence that the student does not yet have a secure, coherent grasp of the concept. It is not evidence about which of the two claims is the misconception. It also argues against treating the Contradiction move as Gadfly's only rigorous move: Elicit, Probe and Apply/Transfer do work that refutation cannot.
- **Confidence:** Medium. Bibliographic data are publisher-verified; the argument is known through one reliable secondary summary.

### Wolfsdorf2013
- **APA:** Wolfsdorf, D. (2013). Socratic philosophizing. In J. Bussanich & N. D. Smith (Eds.), *The Bloomsbury companion to Socrates* (pp. 34ff.). Bloomsbury (Continuum). ISBN 9781441112842.

Editors and ISBN were confirmed through library and bookseller listings found by search. The book's catalog year is given as 2013 in one record and 2012 in others. - **Verified quote or claim:**
  - "Mainstream Anglophone scholarship on Socrates' method over the past quarter century has been galvanized by Gregory Vlastos's 1983a article `The Socratic Elenchus'."
  - "Benson argues that Socrates' immediate aim is to refute his interlocutor's claim to possess ethical knowledge and that Socrates does this by exposing inconsistency among a set of his interlocutor's ethical beliefs."

## B. The clinical interview: following the answer without suggesting it

### Piaget1929
- **APA:** Piaget, J. (1929). *The child's conception of the world* (J. Tomlinson & A. Tomlinson, Trans.). Routledge & Kegan Paul.
- **Link:** https://archive.org/details/childsconception01piag (full-text OCR at https://archive.org/download/childsconception01piag/childsconception01piag_djvu.txt)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read for the Introduction, section 1 ("Method of tests, pure observation and the clinical method", pp. 2-10) and section 2 ("The five types of reaction revealed by clinical examination", pp. 10-14). The scanned copy is a later Routledge & Kegan Paul reprint of the 1929 English edition. The imprint page reads "First Published in England 1929", with reprints to the 1970s. The quotes below are from OCR text; obvious OCR errors (for example "ru'e" for "rule") and the French-style space before "!" have been silently corrected.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - p. 4: "The only way to avoid such difficulties is to vary the questions, to make counter-suggestions, in short, to give up all idea of a fixed questionnaire."
  - pp. 3-4: asking "what makes the sun move?" means "the suggestion of an outside agent occurs at once, thus provoking the creation of a myth."
  - p. 9: "It is so hard not to talk too much when questioning a child, especially for a pedagogue! It is so hard not to be suggestive!"
  - p. 9: the examiner "must know how to observe, that is to say, to let the child talk freely, without ever checking or side-tracking his utterance, and at the same time he must constantly be alert for something definitive, at every moment he must have some working hypothesis".
  - p. 9: "When students begin they either suggest to the child all they hope to find, or they suggest nothing at all, because they are not on the look-out for anything".
  - p. 11: "When the child replies after reflection, drawing the answer from the stores of his own mind, without suggestion, although the question is new to him, we shall say there is liberated conviction."
  - p. 13: "The golden rule is to avoid suggestion, that is to say to avoid dictating a particular answer among all the possible answers."
- **What it establishes:** It is the founding statement of the clinical method. The examiner adapts each question to the subject's last answer, holds a working hypothesis, varies the wording and offers counter-suggestions, and does not dictate an answer. Piaget sorts responses into five kinds: random answer, romancing, suggested conviction, liberated conviction and spontaneous conviction. He warns that even the form of a question ("how", "what makes") can plant the content of the answer.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** It is the strongest historical support for two Gadfly choices: follow-ups written from the student's own previous answer, and the neutral clarifying question that never hints. It also gives a sharper test for "never hints": a question hints if its form presupposes the answer (Piaget's "what makes the sun move?"), not only if it states the answer. Piaget's counter-suggestion is close to Gadfly's Counterexample move, used to check whether a conviction is stable or was merely suggested. His five categories offer a ready vocabulary for the judge: a confident answer that falls apart under counter-suggestion is closer to "suggested" or "romancing" than to understanding.
- **Confidence:** High. Primary text read at the cited pages (OCR, checked in context).

## C. Verbal reports: what spoken reasoning can and cannot show

### EricssonSimon1980
- **APA:** Ericsson, K. A., & Simon, H. A. (1980). Verbal reports as data. *Psychological Review, 87*(3), 215-251. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.87.3.215
- **Link:** https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Ericsson+Simon+Verbal+reports+as+data+1980 (Crossref record); https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295x.87.3.215 (OpenAlex record carrying the abstract)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (abstract via the OpenAlex record; the APA PsycNet page did not render; open PDF links returned 404). The body was not read.
- **Verified quote or claim:** Abstract: "Verbalizing information is shown to affect cognitive processes only if the instructions require verbalization of information that would not otherwise be attended to." Also: "The inaccurate reports found by other research are shown to result from requesting information that was never directly heeded, thus forcing subjects to infer rather than remember their mental processes."
- **What it establishes:** It provides the theory that makes think-aloud data usable. Reporting what is currently in attention is trustworthy and leaves the process largely unchanged. Reports go wrong when people are asked for information they never attended to, because they then infer rather than recall.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** It separates two kinds of Gadfly question. "Talk me through how you'd work this out" asks for the content of current thinking. "Why did you think that?" about an earlier answer asks for a reconstruction, which may be inference rather than memory. A Probe asking *why a claim is true* is a request for justification, not for introspection, and should be scored as such.
- **Confidence:** High for the abstract's claims; the detailed model was not read.

### EricssonSimon1998
- **APA:** Ericsson, K. A., & Simon, H. A. (1998). How to study thinking in everyday life: Contrasting think-aloud protocols with descriptions and explanations of thinking. *Mind, Culture, and Activity, 5*(3), 178-186. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327884mca0503_3
- **Link:** https://cecas.clemson.edu/cedar/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Ericsson.pdf (full text PDF, text extracted and read); Crossref record
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read (pp. 178-184 checked, especially "Nonreactive Verbalization of Thinking--Think-Aloud" and the passages on Level 3 verbalization).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - p. 181: "we showed why verbal descriptions of one's thinking directed to another individual are likely to alter the course of thinking"
  - p. 181: participants are told "merely to verbalize their thoughts ... rather than describe or explain them to anyone else."
  - p. 182: "when participants are asked to describe and explain their thinking, their performance is often changed mostly it is improved" (wording as printed).
  - p. 184: "Behavior is changed when students are asked not simply to think aloud but to analyze and explain their own task behavior."
- **What it establishes:** The authors split verbalization into two modes. Pure think-aloud is not directed at a listener and leaves the sequence of thoughts unchanged. Socially directed description and explanation (their "Level 3") changes thinking, usually for the better. They tie the improvement to the self-explanation literature (Chi et al., cited but not opened here).
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** This matters most for the reactivity question. Gadfly is explanation addressed to a listener, so by the authors' own account it falls in the reactive mode: being examined this way is likely to change, and often improve, the student's reasoning during the exam. That is fine for learning but has two consequences for scoring. Later answers in a session may reflect understanding built during the exam, and comparing a student's late answers with early ones (including the voice-strain baseline from the first two answers) mixes changes in reasoning with changes in stress.
- **Confidence:** High. Full text read; it is the original authors' own statement of their theory.

### NisbettWilson1977
- **APA:** Nisbett, R. E., & Wilson, T. D. (1977). Telling more than we can know: Verbal reports on mental processes. *Psychological Review, 84*(3), 231-259. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231
- **Link:** https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295x.84.3.231 (OpenAlex record carrying the abstract); Crossref record; https://web.math.princeton.edu/~sswang/literature_general_unsorted/Nisbett-Wilson-PsychologicalReview1977.pdf (opened, but it is a scanned image PDF with no readable text)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (abstract via OpenAlex). The scanned PDF was opened but could not be read as text, so the body was not checked.
- **Verified quote or claim:** Abstract: "Evidence is reviewed which suggests that there may be little or no direct introspective access to higher order cognitive processes." And: "their reports are based on a priori, implicit causal theories, or judgments about the extent to which a particular stimulus is a plausible cause of a given response." And: "Accurate reports will occur when influential stimuli are salient and are plausible causes of the responses they produce".
- **What it establishes:** People's accounts of *why* they responded as they did are often plausible reconstructions rather than readouts of the process. Such accounts can still be accurate when the real cause is salient and plausible.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** This is a caution for any rubric criterion that treats the student's account of *how they reasoned* as evidence of the reasoning itself. Gadfly should score whether a justification is correct and connected to the source material, not whether it is a faithful introspective report. The same applies to reviewers reading an integrity flag: a student's explanation of a gaze or pause pattern ("I was thinking") is a reconstruction too, and may be accurate or not.
- **Confidence:** High for the abstract's claims; the reviewed experiments were not read.

### Fox2011
- **APA:** Fox, M. C., Ericsson, K. A., & Best, R. (2011). Do procedures for verbal reporting of thinking have to be reactive? A meta-analysis and recommendations for best reporting methods. *Psychological Bulletin, 137*(2), 316-344. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021663
- **Link:** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:21090887&resultType=core&format=json (Europe PMC record, PMID 21090887, full abstract); Crossref record
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (full abstract via Europe PMC). The body, moderator analyses and the Schooler (2011) commentary (doi:10.1037/a0022390, located in Crossref) were not read.
- **Verified quote or claim:** Abstract: "Findings based on nearly 3,500 participants show that the 'think-aloud' effect size is indistinguishable from zero (r = -.03) and that this procedure remains nonreactive even after statistically controlling additional factors such as task type". And: "procedures that entail describing or explaining thoughts and actions are significantly reactive, leading to higher performance than silent control conditions. All verbal reporting procedures tend to increase times to complete tasks." The meta-analysis covers 94 studies. (Inner quotation marks are shown as single quotes.)
- **What it establishes:** Across 94 studies, plain think-aloud does not measurably change accuracy, while being asked to explain or describe does change it (upward). Every form of verbal reporting slows people down.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Three implications. (1) Answering Gadfly's "why" and "what if" questions is an explaining procedure, so some improvement during the session is expected; the report should not read later-turn gains as proof of prior knowledge. (2) Because verbalizing slows everyone, slower speech rate and longer pauses are expected features of a spoken exam. That supports keeping speech-rate and pause metrics low-confidence and comparing each student only to their own baseline. (3) The finding that explanation raises performance is a reason to describe Gadfly to customers as a learning event as well as an assessment.
- **Confidence:** High for the abstract's summary claims; individual task-type effects were not checked.

## Corrections and cautions

- **"The elenchus proves the student wrong."** Overstated. On Vlastos's own statement of the problem (Vlastos1983 via Wolfsdorf2013 and Potter 1993), a refutation shows only that the thesis is inconsistent with other agreed premises. Benson (Benson1995, Benson2000, Wolfsdorf2013) argues this is all Socrates ever claimed: the elenchus tests claimed knowledge through consistency. For Gadfly, "can both things be true?" detects incoherence. It does not identify which claim is the misconception; that needs the rubric and source.
- **"The Socratic method is one uniform procedure."** Vlastos himself withdrew this: in "Afterthoughts" (1983b) he conceded that his account fits only the *Gorgias* (Wolfsdorf2013's report). Later scholars describe several kinds of Socratic argument. Gadfly's "the elenchus" label for the Contradiction move is fine as a nickname, but docs should not claim Gadfly reproduces "the" Socratic method.
- **"In the Meno, Socrates teaches nothing and only asks."** Socrates says so (82e), but in the passage read (84d-85b) many questions are leading yes/no questions ("Has not each of the inside lines cut off half of each of these four spaces?"). The slave-boy passage is a poor model for Gadfly's non-hinting clarifying question. Piaget's "golden rule" against dictating an answer (Piaget1929, p. 13) is the better model.
- **"Thinking aloud changes how people think."** Overstated in its general form. Plain think-aloud is non-reactive for accuracy (Fox2011, r = -.03 over 94 studies), but explanation and description directed at a listener are reactive and usually raise performance (Fox2011; EricssonSimon1998). Gadfly is the second kind. All forms slow people down.
- **"Nisbett and Wilson showed verbal reports are worthless."** Too strong. Their abstract says reports on the *causes* of one's responses are often theory-driven, but can be accurate when the cause is salient and plausible. Ericsson and Simon (EricssonSimon1980) argue that the inaccuracy comes from asking for information that was never attended to. Reports of current thinking are a different case.
- **Untested Gadfly assumption.** docs/SOCRATIC_PLATFORM.md says "Someone relaying another source's answers tends to contradict themselves." None of the sources in this note tests that claim. It is a plausible product hypothesis, not an established finding, and it should not be presented to customers as evidence-based without a separate source or Gadfly's own data.
- **Vlastos's wording in this note is second-hand.** The quotes attributed to Vlastos come from Wolfsdorf2013 and Potter 1993, which agree with each other and give page numbers. They should be checked against *Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy* 1 (or the revised *Socratic Studies* version, which may differ) before being quoted in customer-facing material.

## Synthesis for Gadfly

- **Contradiction probes test coherence, not correctness.** The best-supported reading of the elenchus (Benson1995, Benson2000, as summarized in Wolfsdorf2013) is that it tests a claim to knowledge by exposing inconsistency. Gadfly should score a ledger contradiction as evidence against secure understanding, and should use the rubric and source chunks, never the contradiction alone, to decide which claim is wrong.
- **Confront only with what the student actually said.** Vlastos's "own belief" rule and Benson's doxastic constraint both require that refuting premises be the answerer's sincere beliefs. Gadfly's Contradiction move should quote the two ledger claims back, close to verbatim, and let the student revise either one. A resolution of either kind is legitimate, and the judge should score the quality of the resolution.
- **The non-hinting clarifying question has a solid lineage, and a sharper test.** Piaget's clinical method (Piaget1929) is a direct ancestor: follow the subject's answer, vary the wording, offer counter-suggestions, and never dictate an answer. A question hints if its form presupposes the answer ("what makes X happen?" presupposes an outside cause), not only if it states the answer. The question generator's "never revealing the answer" check should include presupposition.
- **Counterexample doubles as Piaget's counter-suggestion.** A conviction that survives a counter-suggestion is better evidence of understanding than one stated once (Piaget1929; Meno 85c, "repeatedly asked ... in a variety of forms"). That supports re-testing a concept through several moves before marking mastery.
- **Expect the exam to change the student.** Explaining to a listener is reactive and usually improves performance (EricssonSimon1998; Fox2011). Gadfly is an explaining procedure. The report should not treat late-session gains as proof of knowledge the student brought in, and the product can honestly claim learning value as well as measurement.
- **Slowing down is normal.** All verbal reporting increases time on task (Fox2011). Slower speech and longer pauses are expected in a spoken exam, which supports showing the voice-strain and latency signals to reviewers only as low-confidence evidence measured against the student's own baseline, never as verdicts.
- **Score justifications, not introspection.** People's accounts of why they answered as they did can be theory-driven reconstructions (NisbettWilson1977), and questions about earlier thinking ask for information that may never have been attended to (EricssonSimon1980). Rubric criteria such as "depth of reasoning" should judge whether the justification is correct and connected, not whether it is a faithful report of the student's mental process.
- **What the evidence does not support.** None of these sources shows that a Socratic oral exam is a more valid measure of understanding than other methods, or that contradiction rate detects cheating. Those claims need the oral-assessment literature (note 02) and Gadfly's own validation data.
