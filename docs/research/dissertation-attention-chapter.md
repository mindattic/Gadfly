## A. Section 2.11 Attention, eye contact and the embodied examiner

A spoken examination with a synthetic examiner does more than collect signals. It presents the student with a face, asks
that face be attended to, and responds when it is not. Each of those acts has a literature. This section reviews what is
known about where people look while they think and speak, who differs, what re-orients attention without harm, and how
the appearance and conduct of an artificial interlocutor bear on attention and anxiety. It was added after the first
research pass, on 22 November 2026, to answer an open item recorded in Appendix A (A.7, A.8): whether looking away while
thinking has any support.

### 2.11.1 Gaze aversion while thinking

It does. People look away from a face more as the thinking they are doing becomes harder, and looking away helps. In the
study that opened the topic, "the frequency of gaze aversion is related to the difficulty of cognitive processing" and
"averting the gaze improves performance" (Glenberg et al., 1998, abstract). Children questioned either face to face or
across a live video link averted their gaze more on harder questions in both conditions, from which the authors concluded
that "the primary function of averting gaze is to manage the cognitive load" (Doherty-Sneddon & Phelps, 2005, abstract).
Across three experiments with adults, gaze aversion rose with task difficulty and was absent on very easy tasks: "gaze
aversion is an active avoidance strategy" (Abeles & Yuval-Greenberg, 2017, abstract). Five-year-olds trained to look away
"performed significantly better on challenging questions" than untrained children (Phelps et al., 2006, abstract).

The cost of the alternative is specific to faces. Holding eye contact disrupted visuospatial imagination where looking at
other visual material did not (Markson & Paterson, 2009, abstract). Eye contact "shares domain-general cognitive resource"
with verb generation and slowed it when the demands of retrieval and selection were both high (Kajimura & Nomura, 2016,
abstract). A review of gaze in face-to-face interaction records the older observation that "looking at the other indeed
interferes with the production of spontaneous speech" (Hessels, 2020, quoting Beattie).

How much looking away is ordinary can be estimated from three studies read in full. In the interval between the end of a
question and the start of the answer, children looked away for 70% of the time on easy and 92% on hard questions (typical
development), against roughly a quarter of the time while listening and while speaking (Doherty-Sneddon et al., 2012).
Adults in four-minute conversations looked away from their partner's face about 29% of the time while speaking and 10%
while listening; mutual eye contact occupied 12% of the conversation, in instances of about a third of a second (Rogers
et al., 2018). In a video call, speaking reduced looking at the face relative to listening with an effect of partial
η² = .73, whether or not participants believed the call was live (Mansour & Kuhn, 2019). The preferred duration of mutual
gaze with a face on a screen, across 428 people from 56 nationalities, was 3.3 seconds (Binetti et al., 2016).

Eye contact is also not neutral for the person who receives it. It is detected early and modulates concurrent processing
(Senju & Johnson, 2009, abstract), raises skin conductance (Helminen et al., 2011, abstract), and, in the conclusion of
one review, "longer periods of eye contact may be disruptive for cognitive performance" (Hietanen, 2018).

### 2.11.2 Who differs

Where people look while they think differs by culture. Asked questions that required thinking, Canadian and Trinidadian
adults looked up on about four in five trials and Japanese adults looked down on three in four (McCarthy et al., 2006,
Table 1); Canadians looked up only when they knew they were observed (McCarthy et al., 2008, abstract). Japanese
participants rated faces making eye contact as angrier and less approachable than Finnish participants did (Akechi et al.,
2013, abstract).

Differences in gaze to social stimuli are among the best replicated findings about autism (Chita-Tegmark, 2016;
Frazier et al., 2017; both abstract). Two findings bear directly on a rule that requires eye contact. When gaze was
constrained to the eye region, autistic participants showed abnormally high subcortical activation, and the authors
cautioned that "forcing individuals with autism to look in the eyes might be counterproductive and elicit more anxiety"
(Hadjikhani et al., 2017). In first-hand accounts, autistic adults and adolescents wrote that "eye contact interrupts the
flow of my thoughts" (Trevisan et al., 2017). People with social anxiety make less eye contact with emotional faces
(Hedges' g = −0.67 and −0.49; Günther et al., 2021, abstract), mostly when speaking or interacting (Chen et al., 2020,
abstract), and in a video call those higher in gaze anxiety looked at the face less only while talking (Mansour & Kuhn,
2019). No source located in this pass establishes a difference specific to attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder.

### 2.11.3 What re-orients attention

The attention literature distinguishes cues that capture attention through the stimulus from those the observer follows
by intention. Stimulus-driven selection takes priority early whatever the observer intends (Theeuwes, 2010, abstract),
but an observer engaged elsewhere is not necessarily captured by an abrupt onset (Yantis & Jonides, 1990, abstract). The
onset of motion captures attention (Abrams & Christ, 2003, abstract), and in the periphery motion was detected far more
reliably than a change of colour, with slow, small movements rated least distracting (Bartram et al., 2003). The
direction of a luminance change matters: "contrary to an offset transient, an onset transient presented at the attended
location disrupted performance" (Theeuwes, 1991, abstract). Gradual degradation of everything except a target guides the
eye toward the target without being noticed (Hata et al., 2016, abstract).

Systems that respond to a learner's gaze have used the agent's own voice. A tutor that spoke a short line when no gaze
was detected for five seconds "successfully reoriented students' attentional patterns" and improved deep reasoning
(D'Mello et al., 2012, abstract). In 287 classroom students, interventions that used the student's name, restated the
content or asked a question reduced mind wandering and improved retention for some students; the designers held that such
a system must "fail-soft", that students "should not feel like they are being watched", and that an intervention should
never interrupt the tutor's speech, and they criticised the five-second rule because it "would mischaracterize students
who close their eyes to concentrate" (Hutt et al., 2021). Interruptions in the middle of a task rather than at its
boundaries roughly doubled errors and the rise in anxiety (Bailey & Konstan, 2006, abstract), and warnings that vary
resist habituation (Anderson et al., 2016, abstract).

Anxiety changes which cues win. It "increases the extent to which processing is influenced by the stimulus-driven
attentional system" (Eysenck et al., 2007, abstract), and being watched by an evaluator disrupts executive attention
(Belletier et al., 2015, abstract). A cue that announces a growing penalty is therefore most intrusive for the students
least able to absorb it. Questioning itself supports attention: interpolated tests roughly halved mind wandering in
online lectures and lowered anxiety toward the final test (Szpunar et al., 2013, abstract), and mind wandering during
aptitude tests "was consistently associated with worse performance" (Mrazek et al., 2012, abstract). No source located
compares darkening with whitening, or any form of screen degradation, as feedback on where a person is looking.

### 2.11.4 The examiner's appearance and conduct

The uncanny valley "exists only under specific conditions", with the best support for perceptual mismatch between the
realism of different features (Kätsyri et al., 2015, abstract). "A mismatch in the size and texture of the eyes and face
was especially prone to make a character eerie" (MacDorman et al., 2009, abstract), and imperfect human-likeness lowered
trust as well as liking (Mathur & Reichling, 2016, abstract). In a systematic review of 152 studies, the photographic
realism of an agent had mostly null effects on social presence while behavioural realism, such as mutual gaze and
responsiveness, had mostly positive ones (Oh et al., 2018). Gaze direction is read from the contrast between a white
sclera and the iris; with a white sclera it was judged correctly over 98% of the time whatever the iris colour
(Yorzinski et al., 2021). No source located tests whether an unnatural skin colour, a glow, or the colour of the iris
changes how an agent is perceived or how long its eyes are looked at.

The conduct of an artificial interviewer bears on anxiety. Framing an interviewer as a computer "lowers evaluation fears
and impression management" (Lucas et al., 2014, abstract); rehearsing an oral assessment with a virtual human produced
more stress than with a human teacher (Vallefuoco et al., 2025, abstract); and a virtual examiner that became less
friendly raised anxiety (Chittaro & Serafini, 2023, abstract). Listeners' responses shape the speaker's narration
(Bavelas et al., 2000, abstract). Where an agent must make a person wait several seconds, a natural spoken filler with a
thinking gesture improved perceived response time while an artificial processing sound was no better than silence
(Maslych et al., 2025), and symbolic indicators of processing drew participants' eyes away from the agent's face
(Gonzales et al., 2025, abstract, preprint). Speech is the most disruptive kind of background noise (g = −0.43), and
intermittent noise more so than continuous (Szalma & Hancock, 2011).

### 2.11.5 Seeing oneself

Video-call fatigue has been attributed partly to seeing one's own image (Bailenson, 2021), an argument its author states
has yet to be tested directly. In a survey of 9,787 people, "mirror anxiety" predicted fatigue (β = 0.18; Fauville et al.,
2023). The experiments are mixed. Viewing oneself during an online lesson raised appearance anxiety, which was associated
with lower memory for the lesson (Tien et al., 2023, abstract), and self-view raised an electroencephalographic marker of
fatigue (Xu et al., 2024, abstract); a field study found no significant effect of watching oneself (Bennett et al., 2021),
and in another experiment it was being on camera, not seeing oneself, that lowered mathematics scores (Savage & Couture
Bue, 2023, abstract). The consistent finding is that being watched is the stressor; seeing oneself may add to it.

## C. Section 5.6.3 Reminders that inform, and reminders that press

Section 5.6.2 distinguished signals that inform a reviewer from signals that act on the student. The gaze mechanism
belongs to the second kind, and Section 2.11 now allows a finer distinction within it. A reminder can be designed to
inform the student, by a cue that re-orients attention and costs little if it is mistaken, or to press the student, by a
cue that grows with the departure and is felt as a penalty.

The evidence favours the first kind on three grounds. First, the behaviour it responds to is often useful: looking away
is how people manage difficult retrieval (Section 2.11.1), so a cue that fires on looking away during the interval before
an answer, where looking away predominates, fires mostly on honest thinking. Second, the students it fires on most often
are the ones the fairness argument of Section 5.3 is concerned with (Section 2.11.2). Third, cues that escalate are
stimulus-driven threats, which capture anxious students most and take from the working memory the answer needs
(Eysenck et al., 2007); interruptions mid-task raise anxiety more than interruptions at a boundary (Bailey & Konstan,
2006). The best-supported reminder is the examiner's own behaviour: a small movement and, if needed, a short varied line
at a pause in the student's speech, never over it (D'Mello et al., 2012; Hutt et al., 2021).

The integrity purpose survives the distinction. What the gaze signal can contribute to integrity is evidence of a long
look off the screen, which may be reading (Section 5.6.1). A rule that responds only to sustained off-screen gaze, judged
as a share of time over a window rather than at each glance, keeps that purpose while leaving the student's ordinary
looking alone. It is a design that can be tested (Chapter 7); the specific thresholds are design choices, not values from
a source.

## D. Section 5.7.3, new closing paragraph

**What the evidence added after drafting.** The objection above was stated without the claim that people look away while
they think, because no source for it had been verified. Section 2.11 now supplies one, and it strengthens the objection:
looking away rises with the difficulty of the thinking and helps it (Glenberg et al., 1998; Doherty-Sneddon & Phelps,
2005; Abeles & Yuval-Greenberg, 2017), it occupies most of the interval before an answer (Doherty-Sneddon et al., 2012)
and about a third of speaking time (Rogers et al., 2018), and constraining gaze to the eyes is aversive for autistic
people (Hadjikhani et al., 2017). The 2-second start of the fade, and the 6-second start within the first 10 seconds of an
answer, are therefore shorter than the ordinary looking-away of an honest student at exactly the moments the examination
most wants to observe. The owner subsequently proposed replacing the fade with a whitening that grows with the time spent
looking away (GAD-T513). No source compares darkening with whitening, but a brightening is an onset transient, which
disrupts the attended task where an offset did not (Theeuwes, 1991), it removes the face that the cue is meant to draw
the eye toward, and it escalates. The research note recommends instead a graded reminder that begins with the examiner's
own behaviour, a light dimming of the surroundings that leaves her face clear, and a rule that responds only to sustained
off-screen gaze (Gadfly repository, `docs/research/attention-and-gaze.md`). The decision remains the owner's.

## E. Section 6.4, first bullet, amended

- **Neurodivergent students.** No source on neurodivergent students in oral or voice examinations was verified
  (Section 2.13). The general literature now shows that the eye-contact requirement weighs on them: constraining gaze to
  the eyes produced abnormally high subcortical activation in autistic participants (Hadjikhani et al., 2017), autistic
  people report that eye contact interrupts their thinking (Trevisan et al., 2017), and socially anxious people avoid
  faces mostly while speaking (Chen et al., 2020; Mansour & Kuhn, 2019). Several of Gadfly's mechanisms act on behaviour
  that differs systematically for these students: eye contact, which the gaze fade enforces; the timing of speech, which
  the delivery cues and the read-out pattern read; and the confrontational move of the elenchus. The eye-contact
  accommodation and the extra-time suppression of the read-out pattern give instructors a remedy for students known to
  need it, but a remedy that depends on a declared accommodation does not reach students who have not declared one. A
  default that does not penalize ordinary looking away would.
