# Research note 03: Retrieval practice, self-explanation, and transfer versus recall

Prepared 2026-11-21 for Gadfly (GAD-T254). Gadfly asks a student to say what something is and then writes each follow-up from the student's own previous answer: why, what if, what would change, can both things be true. This note covers three bodies of evidence that bear on that design. The first is the testing effect, which asks whether being examined is itself a learning event, and what that implies for formative use and the 50-attempt default. The second is self-explanation and elaborative interrogation, which asks whether why/how prompts draw out deeper understanding. The third is the work on levels of understanding and transfer: Bloom, the revised taxonomy, SOLO, Barnett and Ceci, and Bransford and Schwartz. That work bears on how objectives should be tagged by level and whether "what would change if" questions test near or far transfer. Every source below was opened during preparation. The level of checking differs from source to source and is stated in each entry.

How to read the verification levels:
- **Full text read**: the cited passage was checked in the work itself; the entry names the section or page.
- **Abstract/publisher page read**: claims are limited to what the abstract or record page says. Results, effect sizes and caveats in the body were not checked.
- **Record located only**: a bibliographic record (DOI, catalogue entry) was confirmed, but the content was not read. Anything said about the content is labeled as coming from another source.

## A. The testing effect and retrieval practice

### Roediger2006
- **APA:** Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249-255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- **Link:** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x&resultType=core&format=json (Europe PMC record and abstract); https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf (author PDF, text extracted)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read. Checked: abstract, Experiment 1 results (pp. 250-251, Fig. 1) and Experiment 2 design (SSSS / SSST / STTT conditions).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract: "Testing is a powerful means of improving learning, not just assessing it."
  - Experiment 1, 1-week test: "The tested group recalled 56% of the material, whereas the restudy group recalled only 42%" (d = 0.83).
  - Paraphrase, Experiment 1, 5-minute test: restudying beat testing (81% vs. 75%). The advantage reversed at 2 days and 1 week.
  - Paraphrase, design: undergraduates read short prose passages and either restudied or took free-recall tests without feedback. The final test was free recall of the same passage.
- **What it establishes:** Retrieving studied prose, with no feedback, improved delayed free recall of that prose compared with restudying it, although restudying looked better on an immediate test. The benefit is for retention of the same material. Transfer was not measured.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Supports treating an exam attempt as a learning event and not only a measurement, which is the rationale for formative use and repeated attempts. It also warns that a student's immediate impression, or a score taken minutes later, can understate what testing does for later retention. It does not show that Gadfly's why/what-if questions produce learning; free recall of a passage is a much simpler task.
- **Confidence:** High. Full text read; this is the standard modern demonstration of the effect.

### KarpickeBlunt2011
- **APA:** Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping. *Science, 331*(6018), 772-775. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327
- **Link:** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1126/science.1199327&resultType=core&format=json (abstract); https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf (author lab copy, text extracted)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read. Checked: abstract, Experiment 1 method and results, Fig. 1 caption.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract: "The advantage of retrieval practice occurred even when the criterial test involved creating concept maps."
  - Abstract: "Retrieval practice is an effective tool to promote conceptual learning about science."
  - Fig. 1 caption: retrieval practice "enhanced long-term learning above and beyond elaborative study with concept mapping by one and a half standard deviations (d = 1.50), yet students were largely unable to predict this benefit."
  - Paraphrase: the 1-week final test included both verbatim questions and inference questions that required connecting several concepts from the text. Retrieval practice was better on both.
- **What it establishes:** In this study, free-recall retrieval practice on science texts beat concept mapping on a 1-week test that included inference questions, so the benefit was not limited to verbatim recall. Students' own predictions ran the other way.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Supports the claim that effortful retrieval can build understanding at inference level, not just rote memory. That makes the exam-as-learning-event argument stronger for conceptual subjects. The finding that learners misjudge which activity helps them supports telling students why retakes are worth doing. Gadfly already builds concept maps in GAD-T30; this study is about concept mapping as a study activity, not as an analysis tool, so it says nothing against that feature.
- **Confidence:** High for what the study found; Medium for how far it generalizes (see Corrections and cautions: a published comment and reply exist).

### Rowland2014
- **APA:** Rowland, C. A. (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect. *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(6), 1432-1463. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559
- **Link:** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1037/a0037559&resultType=core&format=json (abstract); https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf (author-uploaded advance copy, text extracted)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read. Checked: abstract, method (inclusion), Results (overall effect), and the moderator table (feedback; "retrievability and reexposure").
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract: "Engaging in a test over previously studied information can serve as a potent learning event"
  - Abstract: "initial recall tests yielding larger testing benefits than recognition tests"
  - Method: "One hundred and fifty-nine effect sizes drawing from data reported in 61 studies"
  - Paraphrase, Results: overall random-effects g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.42, 0.58], with high heterogeneity.
  - Paraphrase, moderator table: with feedback g = 0.73 (k = 52); without feedback g = 0.39 (k = 107). Without feedback and with initial test performance of 50% or less, g = 0.03 (k = 17), with a confidence interval spanning zero.
- **What it establishes:** Testing beats restudy for retention on average, by a medium amount, and recall-type tests help more than recognition tests. The benefit depends heavily on whether the learner either got the answer right on the practice test or got feedback. With no feedback and low initial success, the meta-analysis finds no reliable benefit.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** This is the most important limit on the "exam as learning event" claim. A Gadfly attempt is recall-heavy and open-ended, which is the favourable format. But during the exam Gadfly gives no feedback, and its clarifying questions never hint. So a student who answers most objectives poorly may gain little from the attempt itself. The learning value of the 50-attempt default therefore depends on what happens between attempts: released, objective-level feedback that tells the student what was missing.
- **Confidence:** High. Full text of a large peer-reviewed meta-analysis; the minus signs in some confidence intervals were lost in text extraction, so the zero-spanning interval is reported from the table structure and the paper's own wording that such cases were not reliably different from zero.

### Dunlosky2013
- **APA:** Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14*(1), 4-58. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266
- **Link:** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1177/1529100612453266&resultType=core&format=json (abstract); https://www.whz.de/fileadmin/lehre/hochschuldidaktik/docs/dunloskiimprovingstudentlearning.pdf (copy of the published article, text extracted)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read for the relevant sections: abstract; section 1 (elaborative interrogation, incl. 1.1 and the 1.5 overall assessment); section 2.5 (self-explanation overall assessment); section 8.5 (practice testing overall assessment).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract: "Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice received moderate utility assessments"
  - Section 1.1: elaborative-interrogation prompts "include "Why does it make sense that...?", "Why is this true?""
  - Section 1.5: "some concerns remain about the applicability of elaborative interrogation to material that is lengthier or more complex than fact lists."
  - Section 2.5: "further research is needed to establish the durability of these effects across educationally relevant delays"
  - Section 8.5: "we rate practice testing as having high utility."
  - Section 8.5: "some studies have failed to find this advantage (in most of these cases, accuracy on the practice test has been relatively low)." And: "the advantage of practice testing with feedback over restudy is extremely robust."
- **What it establishes:** Practice testing is rated high utility. Elaborative interrogation (answering "why is this true?") and self-explanation are rated moderate, with stated doubts about complex material and about how long the effects last. The review independently repeats Rowland's point that testing without feedback can fail when practice-test accuracy is low.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Gadfly's "why does that follow?" probe is close to an elaborative-interrogation prompt. The moderate rating means the probe is a reasonable learning move but not a proven one for complex, connected material, which is exactly what Gadfly mostly tests. The feedback finding supports releasing objective-level feedback between attempts.
- **Confidence:** High. Full text of the relevant sections of a widely used review.

### PanRickard2018
- **APA:** Pan, S. C., & Rickard, T. C. (2018). Transfer of test-enhanced learning: Meta-analytic review and synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin, 144*(7), 710-756. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151
- **Link:** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1037/bul0000151&resultType=core&format=json (abstract); https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf (author's copy, text extracted)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read. Checked: abstract, the coding of "elaborated retrieval practice", and the "Three-Factor Framework for Transfer of Test-Enhanced Learning" section.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract: "testing can yield transferrable learning as measured relative to a nontesting reexposure control condition" (d = 0.40, 95% CI [0.31, 0.50]).
  - Abstract: transfer "is greatest across test formats, to application and inference questions, to problems involving medical diagnoses, and to mediator and related word cues"
  - Abstract: transfer "is weakest to rearranged stimulus-response items, to untested materials seen during initial study, and to problems involving worked examples."
  - Abstract: the intercept predictions were "often indicating no positive transfer when none of the aforementioned moderators are present."
  - Paraphrase, framework section: the three factors are response congruency between practice and final tests, elaborated retrieval practice ("broad encoding methods and/or elaborative feedback") and initial test performance. With none of them present, the predicted transfer effect is negative.
- **What it establishes:** Testing transfers to new question formats and to application and inference questions on average, but the benefit is conditional. It is weak or absent for material that was studied but not tested, and it largely disappears without congruent responses, elaborated retrieval or good initial performance.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Directly informs the 50-attempt and formative-use argument. Repeated Gadfly attempts are likely to help on the objectives the student was actually probed on, much less on objectives the session skipped. Elaborative feedback after an attempt is one of the three factors, which again supports objective-level feedback between attempts. Because Gadfly's follow-ups vary from attempt to attempt, attempts are partly a transfer test of earlier attempts, which is the favourable "application and inference" case.
- **Confidence:** High. Full text of a large meta-analysis that is the main source on this exact question.

## B. Self-explanation and elaborative questioning

### Chi1989
- **APA:** Chi, M. T. H., Bassok, M., Lewis, M. W., Reimann, P., & Glaser, R. (1989). Self-explanations: How students study and use examples in learning to solve problems. *Cognitive Science, 13*(2), 145-182. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1
- **Link:** https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-bassok-m-lewis-m-reimann-p-glaser-r-1989-self-explanations-how-students (ASU Learning and Cognition Lab record); https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/chibassoklewisreimannglaser_1.pdf (scanned article, first page viewed as an image); https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/19512.html (SERC description)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read. The abstract on p. 145 of the scanned article was read from the page image. The scan has no text layer and the body was not read.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract, p. 145: "We find that "Good" students learn with understanding: They generate many explanations which refine and expand the conditions for the action parts of the example solutions, and relate these actions to principles in the text."
  - Abstract, p. 145: ""Poor" students do not generate sufficient self-explanations, monitor their learning inaccurately, and subsequently rely heavily on examples."
- **What it establishes:** In think-aloud protocols from physics students studying worked mechanics examples, the more successful students spontaneously generated more explanations that tied steps to principles and monitored their own understanding more accurately. This is the founding observation of the self-explanation literature. It is correlational: it compares students who did and did not self-explain spontaneously (Chi1994, p. 453, says so).
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Supports the idea that an answer tying a step to the principle behind it is a marker of understanding. That is what Gadfly's "why does that follow?" probe tries to draw out, and what a "depth of reasoning" rubric criterion should reward. It does not show that prompting causes the difference; see Chi1994 and Bisra2018 for that.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The abstract only, read from the article's own first page; the paper is foundational and widely replicated in spirit.

### Chi1994
- **APA:** Chi, M. T. H., de Leeuw, N., Chiu, M.-H., & LaVancher, C. (1994). Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. *Cognitive Science, 18*(3), 439-477. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3
- **Link:** https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-de-leeuw-n-chiu-m-h-lavancher-c-1994-eliciting-self-explanations (ASU record); https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/chideleeuwchiulavancher_3.pdf (article PDF, text extracted)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read. Checked: abstract (p. 439) and "Differences Between the Prompted and Unprompted Groups" (pp. 452-454).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract, p. 439: "14 eighth-grade students were merely asked to self-explain after reading each line of a passage on the human circulatory system. Ten students in the control group read the same text twice, but were not prompted to self-explain."
  - Abstract, p. 439: "The prompted group had a greater gain from the pretest to the posttest."
  - Abstract, p. 439: "Understanding was assessed by answering very complex questions and inducing the function of a component when it was only implicitly stated."
  - Paraphrase, p. 453: on the harder Category 3 and 4 questions, which needed inference, the prompted group improved by 22.6% and the unprompted group by 12.5%.
  - p. 453: "The data merely show the additional improvements gained by prompting students to self-explain."
- **What it establishes:** Simply prompting students to explain, with no training, produced larger gains than reading twice, and the gain showed most on inference questions. Students who produced many explanations were more likely to reach the correct mental model. The sample is small (14 against 10).
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Supports the claim that asking a student to explain, rather than restate, engages deeper processing and shows most clearly on inference-level items. It is evidence that Gadfly's why/how follow-ups can be both a learning event and a better probe of understanding than definitional recall. The prompts here followed each line of a text during study, not an exam, so the analogy to an exam follow-up is partial.
- **Confidence:** Medium. Full text read, but a small single study; its main value is as the first experimental test of prompted self-explanation.

### Bisra2018
- **APA:** Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Inducing self-explanation: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 30*(3), 703-725. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x
- **Link:** https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x (record); https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf (copy of the published article, text extracted)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read. Checked: abstract, introduction (definition), the moderator table for inducement format, and the Discussion ("Self-Explanation Outperforms Instructional Explanation"; "How Self-Explanation Is Elicited").
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract: "Self-explanation is a process by which learners generate inferences about causal connections or conceptual relationships."
  - Abstract: "identified 69 effect sizes (from 64 research reports)"; "The overall weighted mean effect size using a random effects model was g = .55." (95% CI .45 to .65, Results.)
  - Introduction: "Chi considers self-explanation to differ from summarizing, explaining to another, or talking aloud."
  - Paraphrase, moderator table: interrogative prompts (a question) g = .56 (k = 36); imperative prompts (an instruction to explain) g = .40 (k = 12).
  - Paraphrase, Discussion: self-explanation beat instructor-provided explanation (g = .35) and beat no extra explanation (g = .67). Multiple-choice prompts (k = 2) and essay-format outcomes did not show a detectable effect.
  - Discussion, paraphrase of Schworm and Renkl (2006): making an instructional explanation available on demand undermined learners' incentive to self-explain effortfully.
  - Abstract: "we recommend that future research explore computer-generation of self-explanation prompts."
- **What it establishes:** Across 64 reports, prompting learners to explain improves learning by a moderate amount, for many subjects, for conceptual and procedural knowledge, and with question-style prompts doing at least as well as instructions. Strong cues (multiple-choice prompts) may undercut the effect, and offering ready-made explanations can reduce the effort learners put in.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Supports question-form why/how follow-ups as a learning move, and supports Gadfly's rule that clarifying questions never hint: hints and supplied explanations are what the review links to weaker self-explanation. The authors' call for computer-generated prompts is the gap Gadfly's question generator sits in, so the evidence is analogical, not a test of Gadfly. The definitional point matters: Gadfly's answers are spoken to an examiner, which Chi distinguishes from self-explanation.
- **Confidence:** High. Full text of a peer-reviewed meta-analysis.

## C. Levels of understanding and transfer

### Bloom1956
- **APA:** Bloom, B. S. (Ed.), Engelhart, M. D., Furst, E. J., Hill, W. H., & Krathwohl, D. R. (1956). *Taxonomy of educational objectives: The classification of educational goals. Handbook I: Cognitive domain*. David McKay.
- **Link:** https://archive.org/details/bloometaltaxonomyofeducationalobjectives (Internet Archive item); https://archive.org/download/bloometaltaxonomyofeducationalobjectives/Bloom%20et%20al%20-Taxonomy%20of%20Educational%20Objectives_djvu.txt (OCR text, read)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read for selected passages (OCR of the 1956 edition): title and copyright pages; the introduction to the six major classes; the Comprehension section on extrapolation; the Application section on writing test items.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Introduction: the six classes are Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis and Evaluation, and "the present one appears to us to represent something of the hierarchical order of the different classes of objectives."
  - Comprehension, extrapolation: "It includes the making of estimates or predictions based on understanding of the trends, tendencies, or conditions described in the communication." It "differs from application, however, in that the thinking is based on what is given rather than on some abstraction brought from the other experiences to the situation".
  - Application: "the emphasis in writing application items is on getting situations new to the student."
- **What it establishes:** The original taxonomy offered a tentative ordering from simple to complex. It also draws a useful line: predicting consequences from the conditions already given counts as Comprehension (extrapolation), while Application needs a principle brought to a situation that is new to the student.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Directly answers whether "what would change if" probes transfer. Under Bloom's own definitions, a what-if that stays inside the scenario the student just described is extrapolation (Comprehension), not transfer. It becomes Application only when the situation is new to the student and needs the principle. The Apply/Transfer move in the platform plan should require a new situation, and the rubric should not score a within-scenario what-if as transfer.
- **Confidence:** High. Primary text read (OCR copy); the classifications quoted are the book's own.

### Krathwohl2002
- **APA:** Krathwohl, D. R. (2002). A revision of Bloom's taxonomy: An overview. *Theory Into Practice, 41*(4), 212-218. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2
- **Link:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2 (Crossref record); https://rvc-web-app01.live01.azure.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/About/Academic%20Quality,%20Regulations%20and%20Procedures/Academic%20Quality%20Assurance%20and%20Enhancement%20Procedures/Module%20Development%20and%20Approval/A%20Revision%20of%20Bloom's%20Taxonomy%20-%20An%20Overview.%20David%20R.%20Krathwohl.pdf (university-hosted copy of the article, text extracted); https://openlibrary.org/search.json?q=taxonomy+for+learning+teaching+and+assessing+anderson+krathwohl (Open Library record for the 2001 book)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read for the article (all pages, with focus on pp. 213-217: renamed categories, Table 3, the hierarchy passage and the Taxonomy Table). For the book it summarizes, Anderson, L. W., & Krathwohl, D. R. (Eds.) (2001), *A taxonomy for learning, teaching, and assessing: A revision of Bloom's taxonomy of educational objectives* (Longman; ISBN 0-321-08405-5 complete edition, 0-8013-1903-X abridged): Record located only (Open Library, which lists Airasian, Cruikshank, Mayer, Pintrich, Raths and Wittrock as co-authors; Open Library gives the first-publish year as 2000, the books are commonly cited as 2001).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - "Synthesis changed places with Evaluation and was renamed Create."
  - "the requirement of a strict hierarchy has been relaxed to allow the categories to overlap one another."
  - "some cognitive processes associated with Understand (e.g., Explaining) are more cognitively complex than at least one of the cognitive processes associated with Apply (e.g., Executing)."
  - Paraphrase: the revision adds a Knowledge dimension (Factual, Conceptual, Procedural, Metacognitive) and classifies each objective in a two-dimensional Taxonomy Table, sometimes in more than one cell.
- **What it establishes:** The revised taxonomy has six process categories (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) crossed with four knowledge types. Its own author says it is not a strict hierarchy. Explaining sits under Understand but can be harder than some Apply processes.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Gives a ready-made tag scheme for objectives: process category plus knowledge type, for example "Understand / Conceptual: explain why X". Because the hierarchy is relaxed, Gadfly should not assume that a higher category always means a harder or more valuable objective, and should not convert category into points mechanically. Many Gadfly follow-ups (why, can both be true) are Understand-Explaining or Evaluate, not Create.
- **Confidence:** High for the article (full text, written by a co-editor of the revision); the 2001 book itself was not read.

### BiggsCollis1982
- **APA:** Biggs, J. B., & Collis, K. F. (1982). *Evaluating the quality of learning: The SOLO taxonomy*. Academic Press. No book-level DOI was confirmed; Crossref lists chapter DOIs, e.g. https://doi.org/10.1016/b978-0-12-097552-5.50007-7 ("Origin and Description of the SOLO Taxonomy").
- **Link:** https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Evaluating+the+quality+of+learning+the+SOLO+taxonomy+Biggs+Collis&rows=3 (Crossref chapter records, ISBN 9780120975525); https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/policy/assessment/SOLO-description.pdf (CUHK teaching guide, secondary); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_of_observed_learning_outcome (secondary, for the level wording)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Record located only. The book was not opened; Crossref confirms the chapter records, authors, year and ISBN. The level descriptions below come from secondary sources.
- **Verified quote or claim:** Paraphrase, from the CUHK guide and the Wikipedia article (not from the book): SOLO has five levels: prestructural, unistructural, multistructural, relational and extended abstract. At the relational level the aspects are "integrated into a coherent whole" (Wikipedia). At the extended abstract level the whole is "conceptualised at a higher level of abstraction" (Wikipedia) and generalized to a new topic or area. The CUHK guide lists verbs such as explain causes, relate and apply for relational outcomes, and theorise, generalise and hypothesise for extended abstract.
- **What it establishes:** SOLO classifies the structure of an observed answer rather than the verb in the objective. That suits scoring a student's actual response, whereas Bloom and its revision suit writing objectives.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Suggests a two-part design: tag each objective with a revised-Bloom cell (what is intended), and score the student's answers against SOLO-like structure levels (what was shown). Gadfly's claim ledger and concept map already capture how many relevant points a student made and how they were connected, which maps naturally onto multistructural (several unconnected points) versus relational (connected). Extended abstract roughly corresponds to a correct answer on a genuinely new situation, which links SOLO to the transfer question.
- **Confidence:** Low for any specific wording attributed to the book (not read); High that the work exists as cited.

### BarnettCeci2002
- **APA:** Barnett, S. M., & Ceci, S. J. (2002). When and where do we apply what we learn? A taxonomy for far transfer. *Psychological Bulletin, 128*(4), 612-637. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.612
- **Link:** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.612&resultType=core&format=json (Europe PMC record and abstract)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read. The APA full text returned an access page and was not read, so the nine dimensions are not listed here.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract: the debate on far transfer has "made little progress toward resolution", which the authors attribute to "a failure to specify various dimensions along which transfer can occur."
  - Abstract: the framework "describes 9 relevant dimensions".
  - Abstract: "Estimation of a single effect size for far transfer is misguided."
  - Abstract: "evidence for transfer under some conditions is substantial," but "critical conditions for many key questions are untested."
- **What it establishes:** "Near" and "far" are not one scale. Transfer has to be described along several dimensions at once, and whether it happens depends on which dimensions differ between learning and test.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** "What would change if" cannot be labeled near or far transfer as a whole. A follow-up should be described by how it differs from the material the student learned (a different situation, a different form of answer, a different subject), and objectives that claim transfer should say which kind. Without the body, this note cannot supply the nine dimension names; a follow-up check of the full text is needed before they go into a tag schema.
- **Confidence:** Medium. Abstract only, from the standard transfer taxonomy.

### BransfordSchwartz1999
- **APA:** Bransford, J. D., & Schwartz, D. L. (1999). Rethinking transfer: A simple proposal with multiple implications. *Review of Research in Education, 24*, 61-100. https://doi.org/10.3102/0091732X024001061
- **Link:** https://aaalab.stanford.edu/papers/Rethinking_transfer_a_simple_proposal_with_multiple_implications.pdf (authors' lab copy, text extracted)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read for the framing sections (pp. 8-9 of the lab copy) and the closing section. The DOI comes from search-result listings and was not opened on a publisher page.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - pp. 8-9: transfer studies use "sequestered problem solving" (SPS), in which there are "no opportunities for them to demonstrate their abilities to learn to solve new problems by seeking help from other resources such as texts or colleagues or by trying things out, receiving ffeedback [sic] and getting opportunities to revise."
  - p. 9: "Our thesis is that the SPS methodology and the accompanying DA theory of transfer are responsible for much of the pessimism about evidence for transfer."
  - p. 9: the alternative emphasizes "preparation for future learning" (PFL): "The better prepared they are for future learning, the greater the transfer (in terms of speed and/or quality of new learning)."
  - Closing section of the chapter: "A potential danger of the PFL perspective is that it could lead to claims such as "I'm teaching for future learning so I don't worry about mastery of content"."
- **What it establishes:** Classic transfer tests ask for unaided, direct application, and that format makes transfer look rarer than it is. The authors propose also measuring how well prior learning lets someone learn something new, with resources and feedback, while still requiring content mastery.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** A Gadfly session is sequestered in their sense: no resources and no hints. So it measures direct application and will tend to understate transfer. Across 50 attempts with feedback in between, the attempt record could show something like preparation for future learning: how quickly a student improves on new follow-ups after feedback. That is a possible later metric, not something the sources validate for Gadfly.
- **Confidence:** High for what the chapter argues (full text of the framing sections); it is a conceptual review, not an effect-size study.

## D. Boundary conditions: complex materials

### vanGogSweller2015
- **APA:** van Gog, T., & Sweller, J. (2015). Not new, but nearly forgotten: The testing effect decreases or even disappears as the complexity of learning materials increases. *Educational Psychology Review, 27*(2), 247-264. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x

- **Verified quote or claim:**

### KarpickeAue2015
- **APA:** Karpicke, J. D., & Aue, W. R. (2015). The testing effect is alive and well with complex materials. *Educational Psychology Review, 27*(2), 317-326. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3
- **Link:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3 (Crossref record); https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3.pdf (publisher landing page; abstract read from its description metadata)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - Abstract: "Van Gog and Sweller's idea of "element interactivity" is not defined in a quantitative, measurable way."
  - Abstract: "The experiments failed to observe retrieval practice effects because of the retrieval tasks, not because of the complexity of the materials."
  - Abstract: "the data provide evidence that there is indeed a small positive effect of retrieval practice with worked examples."

- **What it establishes:** The reply argues that the null results came from weak retrieval tasks (isolated words, immediate massed practice), that relevant positive studies were left out, and that even the worked-example data show a small positive effect.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Read together with van Gog and Sweller, the fair position is that retrieval practice probably still helps with complex material, but the size of the benefit is smaller and depends on the retrieval task being meaningful. Gadfly's open, connected questions are the kind of retrieval task Karpicke and Aue argue works. The disagreement is a reason to measure learning across attempts rather than assume it.
- **Confidence:** Medium. Abstract only, but it is the direct published reply from the leading retrieval-practice lab.

## Corrections and cautions

- **"Testing always helps" is overstated.** Rowland2014 finds no reliable benefit (g = 0.03, k = 17) when there is no feedback and initial test performance is 50% or below. Dunlosky2013 notes the same pattern. Gadfly gives no feedback during the exam and never hints, so for weak students an attempt is not automatically a learning event. Feedback after each attempt is what makes the claim safe.
- **Roediger2006 is about retention, not understanding.** Restudy won on the 5-minute test. The final test was free recall of the same passage by undergraduates. It does not support claims about transfer or deep understanding on its own.
- **Karpicke and Blunt (2011) is sometimes cited as showing concept mapping is useless.** The study compared one form of concept mapping (built with the text available) as a study activity. A published comment, Mintzes, J. J., et al. (2011), *Science, 334*, 453, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1203698, and a reply by Karpicke and Blunt (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1204035) exist. Both were located in Crossref only; their content was not read.
- **Testing benefits do not spread to untested material.** Pan and Rickard (2018) find transfer is weakest to material studied but not tested. A Gadfly session that skips an objective should not be expected to strengthen it.
- **The founding self-explanation result is correlational.** Chi et al. (1989) compared students who did and did not self-explain spontaneously (Chi et al. 1994, p. 453). The causal evidence comes from prompting studies such as Chi et al. (1994), which had 14 prompted and 10 control students, and from Bisra2018.
- **Explaining to an examiner is not self-explanation in the strict sense.** Bisra2018 records that Chi distinguishes self-explanation from "explaining to another". Gadfly's spoken answers are explanations to an examiner under assessment, so the self-explanation evidence supports Gadfly's why-prompts by analogy only.
- **Elaborative interrogation is weaker for complex material.** Dunlosky2013 rates it moderate utility and voices concerns beyond fact lists, which is the case Gadfly mostly deals in.
- **Bloom is not a strict ladder.** Bloom1956 called the order "something of the hierarchical order", and Krathwohl2002 says the revision relaxed the strict hierarchy, with Explaining (Understand) sometimes harder than Executing (Apply). Scoring objectives by category rank alone is not supported.
- **A within-scenario "what if" is not transfer.** By Bloom1956's own definitions, predicting consequences from the given conditions is extrapolation under Comprehension. Application requires a situation new to the student. Barnett and Ceci (2002) add that near/far is not a single scale.
- **The SOLO acronym is expanded inconsistently.** The CUHK guide calls it the "Structural Observation of Learning Outcomes". The Wikipedia article opened for this note uses "structure of observed learning outcomes", and the usual expansion is "Structure of the Observed Learning Outcome". The book's own wording was not checked, so confirm it before using the expansion in product text.
- **The complexity debate is unresolved.** van Gog and Sweller (2015) argue the testing effect fades for high element-interactivity material. Karpicke and Aue (2015) argue it remains, but smaller, when the retrieval task is meaningful. Neither side's evidence was checked study by study here.

## Synthesis for Gadfly

- **The exam can be a learning event, conditionally.** Retrieval practice reliably improves later retention compared with restudying (Roediger2006; Rowland2014, g = 0.50; Adesope2017; Dunlosky2013). Recall-type, open-ended questions like Gadfly's are the format that helps most (Rowland2014). This supports formative use and repeated attempts.
- **Feedback between attempts is what makes repeated attempts pay off.** Without feedback and with low success, testing gives about no benefit (Rowland2014; Dunlosky2013), and elaborative feedback is one of three factors behind transfer (PanRickard2018). The 50-attempt default is defensible only if each released attempt tells the student, objective by objective, what was missing. Feedback stays out of the live session, where the never-hint rule is supported by Bisra2018.
- **Gains will be specific to what was probed.** Benefits transfer weakly to material that was not tested (PanRickard2018). Reports should not imply that a retake improves objectives the session did not reach. Rotating which objectives get the deepest follow-ups across attempts spreads the benefit.
- **Why/how follow-ups are a reasonable probe of deeper understanding and a moderate learning aid.** Prompting explanations improves learning (Chi1994; Bisra2018, g = .55, question-form prompts at least as good as instructions), and explaining steps by principles marks better learners (Chi1989). The evidence is moderate, partly analogical (self-explanation versus explaining to an examiner), and weaker for complex material (Dunlosky2013).
- **Tag objectives on two axes and score on a third.** Use the revised taxonomy cell for intent (process category times knowledge type; Krathwohl2002), and score the structure of the student's answer on SOLO-like levels: separate points versus connected versus generalized (BiggsCollis1982, secondary sources). Do not turn category rank into points, because the hierarchy is relaxed (Krathwohl2002; Bloom1956).
- **Separate "what would change if" into two moves.** A what-if inside the scenario the student described is extrapolation, which Bloom1956 places under Comprehension. A what-if that needs the principle in a new situation is application or transfer. The Apply/Transfer move and its rubric should require the second. Label transfer items by how they differ from the source material, not just "near" or "far" (BarnettCeci2002).
- **Gadfly measures sequestered transfer.** No resources and no hints mean a session measures unaided direct application, which understates transfer (BransfordSchwartz1999). Improvement across attempts after feedback is a candidate measure of preparation for future learning, and would need its own validation.
- **Expect smaller effects with complex material and measure them.** The complexity debate (vanGogSweller2015; KarpickeAue2015) means Gadfly should not promise large learning gains from retakes. A simple check is per-objective score change between attempts, tracked separately for objectives that did and did not get feedback.
