# Research note 02: Oral assessment validity and reliability, speaking anxiety and fairness confounds

Prepared 2026-11-21 for Gadfly (GAD-T254). Gadfly is a spoken, Socratic examiner, so two bodies of evidence bear directly on it. The first covers whether oral examinations (vivas, structured orals, interactive orals) can be reliable and valid, and what makes them so. The second covers whether speaking anxiety, accent, language background and disability distort oral scores, and what follows for Gadfly's voice-only exam room, its rubric-scored LLM panel, its blind human grader and its voice-strain integrity signals. Every source below was opened during preparation of this note. The level of checking differs from source to source and is stated in each entry.

How to read the verification levels:
- **Full text read**: the cited passage was checked in the work itself; the entry names the section or page.
- **Abstract/publisher page read**: claims are limited to what the abstract or record page says. Results, effect sizes and caveats in the body were not checked.
- **Record located only**: a bibliographic record (DOI, index entry) was confirmed, but the content was not read. Anything said about the content is labeled as coming from another source.

## A. Validity and reliability of oral examinations

### Joughin1998
- **APA:** Joughin, G. (1998). Dimensions of oral assessment. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 23*(4), 367-378. https://doi.org/10.1080/0260293980230404
- **Link:** https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Joughin+1998+Dimensions+of+oral+assessment (Crossref record)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Record located only (Crossref metadata; no abstract in the record; the publisher page was not opened). The six dimensions are confirmed only through Joughin's own 2010 guide (Joughin2010, full text) and the Memon et al. abstract (Memon2010). Neither of those is this article.
- **Verified quote or claim:** Paraphrase taken from Joughin2010, pp. 10-12, which presents the same framework: oral assessments vary along six dimensions. They are what is assessed, interaction, authenticity, structure, who assesses, and whether the assessment is purely oral or combined with other modes.
- **What it establishes:** This is the standard framework for describing an oral assessment, and later work in medical education reuses it (Memon2010). The article's own wording and argument were not read, so this note relies on the 2010 restatement.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Gadfly sits at a particular point on each dimension. Interaction is highly dialogic, because each follow-up is written from the student's previous answer. Structure is low to medium, because objectives are fixed but the question path is not. The assessors are an LLM panel plus a blind human. The mode is purely oral, since typed substantive answers are not offered (docs/VOICE.md). The framework gives the instructor-facing docs a shared vocabulary for these choices.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The citation is certain, but the content is known only through the author's later restatement.

### Joughin2010
- **APA:** Joughin, G. (2010). *A short guide to oral assessment*. Leeds Met Press in association with University of Wollongong. ISBN 978-1-907240-09-6. https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/2804
- **Link:** https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/2804 ; https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/2804/1/100317_36668_ShortGuideOralAssess1_WEB.pdf (CC BY-NC PDF, text extracted and read)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read. Checked: "Some disadvantages of oral assessment" (p. 7), "Planning oral assessment: six dimensions" (pp. 10-12), "Reliability" (p. 14), "Fairness" (p. 15) and "Marking and grading" (p. 16).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - p. 7: "anxiety that interferes with a student's performance will not give a true indication of his or her ability."
  - p. 7: "Practising presentations in class and providing rehearsals for vivas may help."
  - p. 7: "Examiners can mistake a student's articulateness for knowledge."
  - p. 7: students with hearing or speech impairments "may also require some adjustment to the assessment process."
  - p. 12: "a high degree of structure can also increase the reliability of the assessment. However, if the assessment is overly structured, the capacity to ask probing follow-up questions can be lost"
  - p. 14: "Reliability is usually significantly increased if students are assessed on several cases."
  - p. 14: "increasing the number of questions asked is likely to increase reliability."
  - p. 15: one form of unfairness "may arise when students are being assessed in other than their first language, if the level of language required by the assessment is higher than that required by the course itself"
  - p. 16, citing Yaphe and Street (2003): examiners tend to "form an early impression of the candidate and then use the subsequent questions and responses to confirm or disconfirm this impression"
- **What it establishes:** This is a practitioner guide by the author of the six-dimension framework. It names four sources of unreliability: inter-case, inter-item (including follow-up questions), inter-rater and intra-rater. Its remedies are more cases, more questions, trained and rotated examiner panels, rubrics and model answers, and recording. It names anxiety, hearing or speech impairment, second-language demands, and articulateness mistaken for knowledge as threats to fair scoring. It is guidance, not new empirical data.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:**
  - Supports the rubric-per-objective panel, the blind second grader and keeping the transcript and recording.
  - Directly challenges the adaptive follow-up design, because inter-item variation in follow-ups is a named source of unreliability.
  - The articulateness warning applies to the LLM panel as much as to human examiners. Rubrics should score the objective, not fluency.
  - The early-impression finding argues for scoring each objective on its own evidence, not on a running impression.
  - The guide recommends rehearsals, which supports the spoken practice question. It also recommends alternative arrangements for some students, which Gadfly's voice-only rule does not currently allow.
- **Confidence:** High. Full text read, and the author is the field's standard reference. It is a guide, so its empirical claims are only as strong as the work it cites.

- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (PubMed abstract). The 15 conditions in the body were not read.
- **Verified quote or claim:** Paraphrase of the abstract:
  - This is a review of oral assessment in postgraduate medical certification.
  - Some countries have moved away from oral exams because of continuing concerns.
  - It discusses the six dimensions and questions whether orals support certification decisions reliably and fairly.

### Wass2003
- **APA:** Wass, V., Wakeford, R., Neighbour, R., & Van der Vleuten, C. (2003). Achieving acceptable reliability in oral examinations: An analysis of the Royal College of General Practitioners membership examination's oral component. *Medical Education, 37*(2), 126-131. https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2923.2003.01417.x
- **Link:** https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=12558883&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PMID 12558883)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (PubMed abstract).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - "Reliability coefficients were: (A) intercase 0.65; (B) pass/fail 0.85."
  - "Structured oral examinations can achieve reliabilities appropriate to high stakes examinations"
  - Paraphrase: the format was two 20-minute, five-topic orals, each with two examiner pairs, sitting 896 candidates. Score variance was 41% candidate, 32% oral content and 27% examiners plus general error. The authors project that four 20-minute orals with two examiners, or five with one, would raise reliability to 0.78 (intercase) and 0.94 (pass/fail).
- **What it establishes:** A structured oral can reach high-stakes pass/fail reliability. The largest error source after the candidate is the content sampled (32%), not the examiners. Reliability rises with more orals and more topics, and the authors project that five orals with one examiner reach the same level as four orals with two.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** This is the strongest quantitative anchor in this note. It means coverage of objectives matters more than adding graders. An exam with only a few objectives or one long thread will have low inter-case reliability whatever the panel's quality. Exam length and the number of objectives sampled should be set with reliability in mind. Per-objective scores from a short exam should be shown as formative.
- **Confidence:** High for the numbers as stated in the abstract. They come from one examination with a generalisability-theory analysis, and the method details were not checked.

### VanderVleuten2010
- **APA:** van der Vleuten, C. P. M., Schuwirth, L. W. T., Scheele, F., Driessen, E. W., & Hodges, B. (2010). The assessment of professional competence: Building blocks for theory development. *Best Practice & Research Clinical Obstetrics and Gynaecology, 24*(6), 703-719. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpobgyn.2010.04.001
- **Link:** https://programevaluationcore.northwestern.edu/docs/assessment-of-professional-competence.pdf (PDF, text extracted and read). The requested 1996 paper (van der Vleuten, C. P. M. (1996). The assessment of professional competence: Developments, research and practical implications. *Advances in Health Sciences Education, 1*(1), 41-67, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00596229) was located on Crossref and PubMed (PMID 24178994). No abstract or full text was available, so this later synthesis by the same first author is used in its place.
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Full text read, in the sections "Competence is specific, not generic", "Objectivity does not equal reliability" and "A feasible sample is required to achieve reliable inferences". The 1996 paper: record located only.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - "one measure is no measure, and single-point assessments are not to be trusted."
  - "estimations show that at least 3-4 h of testing time are required to obtain minimally reliable scores." The source uses an en dash.
  - "even the notoriously subjective, old-fashioned, oral examination can be made reliable by wide sampling across content and examiners."
  - "reliability is a matter of sampling, not of standardisation or structuring of assessment."
  - The paper also states that a multiple choice test "samples more efficiently than a `long case' oral examination".
- **What it establishes:** Content specificity is the dominant source of unreliability. Objectivity and standardisation do not by themselves produce reliability; sampling across content and across judges does. Subjective, holistic expert judgement can be reliable when enough of it is sampled. Orals sample less efficiently per hour than written formats.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** This supports the panel and the blind human grader as sampling across judges, and it supports allowing holistic rubric judgements. It challenges any claim that a single 20-30 minute Socratic thread gives a reliable summative grade. Summative use should combine several sessions or assessment sources. It also tempers Joughin2010's emphasis on structure; see Corrections and cautions.
- **Confidence:** High. Full text read, and it is the field's leading synthesis. Its figures (3-4 h, 8-10 observations) are cited estimates, not new data.

### Huxham2012
- **APA:** Huxham, M., Campbell, F., & Westwood, J. (2012). Oral versus written assessments: A test of student performance and attitudes. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 37*(1), 125-136. https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2010.515012
- **Link:** https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=title%3A%22Oral%20versus%20Written%20Assessments%22... (ERIC record EJ950336 with abstract). The Crossref record was also opened. The publisher and Napier repository full texts returned 403.
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (ERIC abstract). Marks, test statistics and the anxiety measures in the body were not checked.
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - "There was no evidence of particular groups of students being disadvantaged in the oral tests."
  - "This study suggests that oral assessments may be more inclusive than written ones"
  - Paraphrase of the abstract: in a cohort of 99 students randomly assigned to oral or written groups on the same biology questions, and a second cohort of 29 who took both formats, both cohorts scored significantly higher in the oral format. Students reported more nervousness about orals, but many judged them more useful. Orals were often described as more authentic.
- **What it establishes:** This is one of the few randomised oral-versus-written comparisons. Higher nervousness about orals did not translate into lower oral marks in that setting, and no subgroup disadvantage was detected. The sample is small, comes from one institution and one discipline, and the subgroup analyses could detect only large differences.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Gives some reassurance that a spoken format is not inherently penalising, and supports Gadfly's case that speaking gives a better view of understanding. It does not show that anxious, disabled or non-native students are unaffected. Absence of evidence in n = 99 is not evidence of absence. The nervousness finding supports offering a practice run.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The abstract is clear, but it is a single small study. Figures quoted elsewhere, such as an oral mean about 1.9 marks higher, appear in secondary slides and were not verified here.

## B. Speaking anxiety and fairness confounds

### Horwitz1986
- **APA:** Horwitz, E. K., Horwitz, M. B., & Cope, J. (1986). Foreign language classroom anxiety. *The Modern Language Journal, 70*(2), 125-132. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1986.tb05256.x
- **Link:** https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Horwitz+Horwitz+Cope+1986+... (Crossref); https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=title%3A%22Foreign%20Language%20Classroom%20Anxiety%22... (ERIC EJ337666 abstract). The Wiley and JSTOR pages did not load.
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (ERIC abstract).
- **Verified quote or claim:** The ERIC abstract treats "foreign language anxiety as a conceptually distinct variable in foreign language learning" and discusses "the effects of this anxiety on language learning and its pedagogical implications".
- **What it establishes:** Anxiety about speaking in a second language is a distinct construct, separate from general test anxiety. This is the origin of the FLCAS scale. The abstract does not establish effect sizes on oral exam scores.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Students answering in a second language carry an extra, construct-irrelevant anxiety load in a voice-only exam. This supports a practice run, untimed thinking pauses and a text alternative for them. It also means the voice-strain baseline (the first two answers) may already be elevated for these students.
- **Confidence:** Medium. A foundational, highly cited paper (about 6,600 citations on Semantic Scholar), but only the abstract was read.

### Hembree1988
- **APA:** Hembree, R. (1988). Correlates, causes, effects, and treatment of test anxiety. *Review of Educational Research, 58*(1), 47-77. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543058001047
- **Link:** https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Hembree+1988+... (Crossref record with abstract)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (abstract in the Crossref record).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - "Test anxiety (TA) causes poor performance."
  - "Contrary to prior perceptions, improved test performance and grade point average (GPA) consistently accompany TA reduction."
  - Paraphrase: a meta-analysis of 562 studies. Test anxiety relates to lower self-esteem and to fear of negative evaluation, and it varies with ability, gender and grade level.
- **What it establishes:** Across a large literature, test anxiety is reliably associated with lower performance, and reducing it is followed by better scores. That second finding is the stronger evidence that anxiety distorts measured ability, rather than only accompanying low ability.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Anxiety reduction is a validity measure, not just a courtesy. This supports the practice question, clear instructions, a held clock until the first question, and avoiding time pressure. The "fear of negative evaluation" link is especially relevant to a live, face-like examiner avatar.
- **Confidence:** Medium. A large meta-analysis, but the abstract was read only. The causal wording is the author's; see Corrections and cautions.

### vonderEmbse2018
- **APA:** von der Embse, N., Jester, D., Roy, D., & Post, J. (2018). Test anxiety effects, predictors, and correlates: A 30-year meta-analytic review. *Journal of Affective Disorders, 227*, 483-493. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.11.048
- **Link:** https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=29156362&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PMID 29156362)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (PubMed abstract).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - "test anxiety was significantly and negatively related to a wide range of educational performance outcomes"
  - "Self-esteem was a significant and strong predictor of test anxiety."
  - Paraphrase: a meta-analysis of 238 studies since 1988. Relationships are in the small-to-moderate range. Perceived test difficulty and high-stakes consequences are associated with higher anxiety.
- **What it establishes:** It updates Hembree with the same direction and modest size. Stakes and perceived difficulty raise anxiety. The abstract does not compare oral and written formats.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Lowering stakes per session reduces the anxiety confound. Examples are formative modes, multiple attempts (Open Exams allows 50) and clear human review before release. The small-to-moderate size means anxiety is a real but bounded confound. It should be mitigated, not used to dismiss oral scores.
- **Confidence:** High for the direction and the predictors as stated, from a large recent meta-analysis. Medium for applying it to oral exams specifically.

### LevAri2010
- **APA:** Lev-Ari, S., & Keysar, B. (2010). Why don't we believe non-native speakers? The influence of accent on credibility. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 46*(6), 1093-1096. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2010.05.025
- **Link:** https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Lev-Ari+Keysar+2010+... (Crossref); https://news.uchicago.edu/story/foreign-accents-make-speakers-seem-less-truthful-listeners-research-shows (University of Chicago news release). ScienceDirect and the MPG repository PDF returned 403.
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Record located only for the paper itself. The study description comes from the institution's press release, which was read.
- **Verified quote or claim:** From the press release, quoting the authors: "They misattribute the difficulty of understanding the speech to the truthfulness of the statements." Paraphrase of the press release:
  - Trivia statements read by native, mildly accented and heavily accented speakers were rated less truthful when accented. The reported means were 7.5, 6.95 and 6.84.
  - When listeners were told the study was about accent, the penalty disappeared for mild accents but remained for heavy accents.
- **What it establishes:** It reported that a processing-fluency penalty can make accented content seem less credible to listeners. Replication is mixed; see Corrections and cautions.
- **Relevance to Gadfly:** Gadfly's scorers read transcripts, not audio. The direct accent-credibility pathway therefore mostly applies to any human grader who listens to recordings. Blind grading from transcript text is a mitigation. The indirect pathway matters more: accent raises ASR error (Koenecke2020), which makes the transcript less fluent, which may in turn lower rubric scores.
- **Confidence:** Low. The paper body was not read, and the effect has failed several replications.

### Koenecke2020
- **APA:** Koenecke, A., Nam, A., Lake, E., Nudell, J., Quartey, M., Mengesha, Z., Toups, C., Rickford, J. R., Jurafsky, D., & Goel, S. (2020). - **Link:** https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=32205437&rettype=abstract&retmode=text (PMID 32205437)
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (PubMed abstract).
- **Verified quote or claim:**
  - "all five ASR systems exhibited substantial racial disparities"
  - "the race gap was equally large on a subset of identical phrases spoken by black and white individuals"
  - Paraphrase: five commercial ASR systems tested on 42 white and 73 black speakers gave an average word error rate of 0.35 for black speakers versus 0.19 for white speakers. The authors trace the gap to the acoustic models.
- **What it establishes:** Commercial speech-to-text in 2019-2020 nearly doubled word errors for African American speakers relative to white speakers. - **Relevance to Gadfly:** This is a direct fairness risk in the voice pipeline. Transcript errors feed the LLM panel and the clarifying-question logic, and they can also shift the speech-rate and pause features used by the integrity signals. Gadfly should audit word error rate by accent and dialect group on its own ASR provider. The typed small-correction step is a partial mitigation only.
- **Confidence:** High. A peer-reviewed PNAS study with a clear abstract, but the vendors and models are now dated.

### Mujtaba2024
- **APA:** Mujtaba, D., Mahapatra, N., Arney, M., Yaruss, J. S., Gerlach-Houck, H., Herring, C., & Bin, J. (2024). - **Link:** https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.269
- **Retrieved:** 2026-11-21
- **Verification:** Abstract/publisher page read (ACL Anthology page).
- **Verified quote or claim:** "consistent and statistically significant accuracy bias across all ASRs against disfluent speech". Paraphrase: six leading ASR systems were tested on real speech from people who stutter and on synthetic stuttered LibriSpeech. - **Relevance to Gadfly:** Several features are at risk for students who stutter:
  - The 4-second silence that ends an answer (`Gadfly:Voice:EndOfAnswerSeconds`) can cut them off.
  - Their transcripts are degraded before the panel sees them.
  - The pause, hesitation and speech-rate features can misfire. These feed `strain.*`, `reading-cadence` and `fluent-after-silence`.

  This supports an accommodation profile with a longer or disabled silence cutoff, a text answer option, and suppressed or reweighted speech-timing integrity signals.
- **Confidence:** Medium. A peer-reviewed conference paper, abstract only.

## Corrections and cautions

- **"Structure makes orals reliable" is overstated.** Joughin2010 (p. 12) says a high degree of structure "can also increase the reliability". Wass2003 shows a structured oral reaching 0.85 pass/fail. VanderVleuten2010 argues explicitly that "reliability is a matter of sampling, not of standardisation or structuring of assessment". The two positions can be reconciled: structure helps chiefly by ensuring broad, comparable content sampling and examiner alignment. For Gadfly, the number of objectives and topics sampled and the number of independent judges matter more than scripting the follow-ups.
- **The "oral exams are unreliable" folklore is also overstated.** Wass2003 and VanderVleuten2010 both show orals can be made reliable, but only with enough sampled content and judges. A single short oral is unreliable. Gadfly cannot claim reliability from the format alone, or from the panel alone.
- **Lev-Ari and Keysar (2010) is widely cited as settled; it is not.** Lorenzoni, Faccio and Navarrete (2024), *Journal of Cognition, 7*(1), 26 (https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.353, read in full on PMC at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10885845/), summarise the follow-up work:
  - Souza and Markman (2013) found no accent effect in a direct replication.
  - Stocker (2017) found none in a Swiss sample.
  - Boduch-Grabka and Lev-Ari (2021) replicated the effect, and found it shrinks with prior exposure to the accent.
  - Lorenzoni et al.'s own illusory-truth study found that accent did not modulate truth ratings, although foreign speakers were rated lower on social traits.

  Cite the effect as mixed. The better-replicated fairness risk is ASR error (Koenecke2020, Mujtaba2024), not listener credibility.
- **Huxham et al. (2012) is sometimes cited as showing "anxiety does not matter in orals."** The abstract says students were more nervous about orals and that no group was evidently disadvantaged. It does not show that anxiety has no effect on scores, and it does not test students with anxiety disorders, disabilities or second-language speakers specifically.
- **Hembree's "Test anxiety causes poor performance" is a causal claim drawn from a meta-analysis that is largely correlational.** The strongest support is the treatment evidence, where scores improve when anxiety is reduced. Restate it as "anxiety depresses measured performance, and reducing it raises scores", not as a fixed causal size.
- **The Sotiriadou et al. citation:** the DOI resolves to "The role of authentic assessment to preserve academic integrity and promote skill development and employability" (Studies in Higher Education 45(11), first online 2019). Its findings are self-reported perceptions, not measured misconduct or reliability.
- **Memon et al.** is sometimes dated 2008 (its online-first date) and sometimes 2010 (its print issue). Use 2010, volume 15, issue 2.

## Synthesis for Gadfly

- **Orals can be reliable, but only through sampling.** The evidence (Wass2003, VanderVleuten2010, Joughin2010) supports oral exams as a valid and potentially reliable format. Reliability comes from sampling many content areas and several independent judges. Gadfly's LLM panel plus a blind human grader is the right shape. A short exam on a few objectives is not reliable for summative use however good the scorers are. Set a minimum number of objectives per exam and report per-objective scores from short exams as formative.
- **Content dominates error.** In Wass2003, 32% of score variance came from oral content and 27% from examiners plus general error. Spending effort on more objectives and topics will usually improve reliability more than adding a fourth or fifth LLM to the panel.
- **Adaptive follow-ups trade comparability for depth.** Joughin2010 names inter-item variation in follow-up questions as a reliability threat. Gadfly should anchor every objective with a fixed opening question ("what is it?") and score against the instructor rubric, not against how deep the thread happened to go. It should also report how many probes each student received, so that thread length does not silently become a score.
- **Score understanding, not articulateness.** The warning that examiners mistake articulateness for knowledge (Joughin2010), and the early-impression bias, argue for per-objective rubric scoring on the evidence in each answer, with fluency and presentation explicitly excluded from the criteria. Check the LLM panel for a fluency bias using paraphrased answers of equal content and unequal fluency.
- **Anxiety is a real but modest confound, and reducing it improves validity** (Hembree1988, vonderEmbse2018; Huxham2012 shows students are more nervous about orals). Keep the spoken practice question. Consider a fuller ungraded practice session, and keep the held clock. Re-examine two current choices: the clock keeps running while the exam is paused, and a fixed 4-second silence ends the answer. Both add time pressure that anxious, second-language and disfluent speakers feel most.
- **The largest documented fairness risk is the ASR layer, not human prejudice.** Koenecke2020 and Mujtaba2024 show systematic transcription error by dialect and fluency. Gadfly should:
  - measure its provider's word error rate by accent, dialect and disfluency group;
  - make transcript correction easy and visible to reviewers;
  - flag low-confidence ASR segments to the panel and the human grader, so they are not scored as wrong content.
- **Speaking-based integrity signals will misfire on exactly these students.** Accent, stuttering, second-language anxiety and general nerves change pause ratio, speech rate and pitch. A baseline taken from the first two answers can itself be anxious. If a student's nerves are worst at the start, later answers will look "less strained" rather than more. That pattern is a design inference; none of the sources above measured it. This supports keeping the signals as low-confidence reviewer evidence only, never verdicts. Consider suppressing them under a declared accommodation.
- **A text fallback is an accommodation the evidence supports.** Joughin2010 recommends alternative arrangements for undue anxiety and adjustments for speech or hearing impairment. Gadfly's current rule that every exam is voice-only, with typed small corrections only, has no route for these students. A typed-answer mode (or extended-time mode) per student, which instructors can grant, would close the gap without changing the default.
- **What the evidence does not support:**
  - claims that oral scores are free of anxiety or accent effects (Huxham2012 is too small to show this);
  - claims that listener accent bias is established (LevAri2010 replication is mixed);
  - any statement about neurodivergent students, for whom no source was verified here.
