# How the examiner asks questions

The Gadfly examiner doesn't work from a fixed list. It listens to your answer and asks the question that best shows
what you understand next. This page explains how, so nothing about the conversation catches you off guard.

## One topic at a time

Your instructor chooses the topics (the *learning objectives*) and the order. For each one the examiner:

1. **Opens** with a broad question: explain the idea in your own words.
2. **Follows up** on what you said: up to a limit your instructor sets for each topic.
3. **Moves on** as soon as it is clear, either that you understand the topic well, or that you don't know it yet.

**Topics finished X of Y** at the top of the screen shows how far through you are. A topic can close early,
which is not a bad sign: it often means you showed what was needed.

## The kinds of question

Each follow-up is one of a few classic moves. You don't need to name them; it just helps to recognise them.

| Move | What it sounds like | How to answer well |
|---|---|---|
| **Elicit** | "In your own words, what is…?" | Explain it plainly, as to a friend. A definition from memory is only a start. |
| **Clarify** | "What do you mean by…?" | Say what *you* meant by the word. It's not a trick; the examiner wants to understand you. |
| **Probe** | "Why is that?" "What would happen if…?" | Give the reason or the mechanism, not just the fact again. |
| **Counterexample** | "What about this case, which seems not to fit?" | Say whether it really breaks your explanation, and adjust the explanation if it does. Changing your mind for a good reason is a strength. |
| **Contradiction** | "Earlier you said X; now you seem to say Y." | Explain how both fit, or say which one you now think is right and why. |
| **Apply** | "Here's a new situation. What happens?" | Use the idea, step by step. |
| **Synthesize** | "How does this connect to…?" | Link the two ideas; say what one tells you about the other. |

## "I don't know" is a real answer

If you genuinely don't know, say so (or press **I don't know**). Socrates called that moment *aporia*: honest
puzzlement, the start of real learning. The examiner notes it and tries another angle or moves on. It is a much
better answer than a confident guess, which leads to more follow-up questions on something you don't know.

## No read-back

Your spoken answer is taken as heard, and the examiner goes straight on to the next question. It doesn't read your
answer back or ask you to confirm, correct, repeat or change it: being able to rephrase after hearing it back would
turn a real-time conversation into a second, considered try. Words the speech recognizer was unsure of are marked for
the person who reviews your exam instead.

## What the examiner never does

- It never tells you your score, or whether an answer was right, during the exam.
- It doesn't teach or give hints in an examination. (Some instructors set up a separate *learning* assessment,
  where explanations and hints are given; the instructions on **My exams** say which kind it is.)
- It never decides your grade. People do; see [How grading and results work](grading-and-results.md).

## Tips that make a real difference

- **Speak in full thoughts.** "It's because…" is better than a list of keywords.
- **Pausing is fine.** If you stop to think, a short countdown appears; start talking again and your answer
  continues. Press **Done answering** when you've finished, if you don't want to wait.
- **Use your own example.** It shows understanding more than repeating the textbook's.
- **Be honest about uncertainty.** "I think…, but I'm not sure about…" gives the examiner something to work with.
- **Don't recite.** An answer that sounds read out from notes tends to draw more probing, not less.
