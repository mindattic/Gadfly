# Eye contact and gentle reminders

> 한국어로 읽기: [시선 맞추기와 부드러운 알림](eye-contact-ko.md)
> Leer en español: [Contacto visual y recordatorios amables](eye-contact-es.md)

In a spoken exam with a person, you'd look at them while you talk. Gadfly asks the same, the way a friendly person would:
**looking away to think is completely normal**, and only a long look away from the screen while you are speaking gets a
reminder, a gentle one. This page explains exactly when that happens, what is kept, and what it does **not** mean.

The rules follow the research on attention and gaze: people look away to think, it helps them answer, and it gets more
frequent the harder the question is. Gadfly is built so that this never counts against you.

## When the check runs

The eye-contact check runs on exams with integrity notes switched on (most exams; your notice says so). It runs only while
**you are answering**. While the examiner is speaking or thinking, look wherever you like.

On the get-ready page or at the start of the exam you may be asked to follow a dot, or to look at the examiner's face for a
moment, so the camera learns where the examiner is on your screen. If you move your screen or your chair later, press **Set up eye
contact again**.

## What counts, and what doesn't

| Situation | What happens |
|---|---|
| Looking away **before you start speaking**, while you think about the question (up to 20 seconds) | Nothing, ever. This is thinking time. |
| Looking anywhere **on the screen**: the transcript, the clock, the captions, the buttons | Nothing. Anywhere in the exam window counts as looking at the screen. |
| Glancing away from the screen while you speak | Nothing. People look away about a third of the time while they talk. |
| Looking **off the screen** for a long time while you speak (about 8 seconds in a row, and more than half of the last 30 seconds) | The reminders below start. If the camera can't see you well, it waits longer. |
| No face in view, or a second face in view | The examiner says so out loud, but never during your thinking time. |

## The reminders, gentlest first

1. **The examiner reacts.** A small tilt of the head and raised brows, as a person would.
2. **The room dims a little.** If you are still looking off the screen, everything except the examiner softly dims and blurs a
   little. The examiner's face stays sharp and bright, so it is easy to look back. It clears quickly when you do. There is no white-out,
   no warning sound and no warning text.
3. **One short, warm line.** If needed, the examiner says one short, kind line at a pause in your speech, never over you and never
   "look at me", at most once per question and three times in a whole exam.

The reminders never block a button, never stop your answer and never end the exam. What the examiner says is not taken as part of your
answer.

During the exam you don't see yourself: your own camera picture is hidden (seeing yourself while being examined is
distracting and tiring). A small **Camera on** indicator shows that the camera is running, and **Show my camera** opens a
small preview for a few seconds if you want to check how you look.

## What is kept

- **No picture or video, ever.** The camera is analysed on your own device. No image leaves it.
- A **short note** of each episode: what kind it was (a long look off the screen, eye movement that looks like reading, face
  not in view, a second face, how far the reminders went), when it started and how long it lasted, and how well the camera
  could track you. These notes are marked as low-confidence.

The notes are for a person reading your exam afterwards, next to the answer you were giving at the time.

## What it does not mean

- It is **never a verdict.** Looking away has many causes (recalling, planning an answer, nerves, a noise in the room, a
  habit), and the camera can't tell which. Reviewers are told exactly that.
- It is **never part of your grade.** No score is calculated from it, and nothing ends your exam automatically.
- It is **hidden from the examiner who grades you blind**, until their grade is in.
- It doesn't try to read your emotions or decide whether you are honest.

## If eye contact is hard for you

Some people can't comfortably hold eye contact with a screen, for example because of autism, low vision, nystagmus, or a
cultural norm. Ask your instructor for the **eye-contact accommodation** before your exam. With it there is no dot setup and
there are no reminders; the camera still notices if your face is out of view or someone else is there. The indicator then says
that eye contact isn't required for you. See [Accommodations](accommodations.md).

## If the check can't start

On some devices the eye-contact check can't run (for example an older device without the features it needs). The indicator
then says eye contact isn't being checked, and the exam carries on. You don't need to do anything.

## Tips

- Put the light in front of you, not behind you.
- Keep the exam window on the screen you naturally look at, not on a second screen off to the side.
- If you wear glasses that reflect a lot of light, tilt the screen slightly.
- If the room dims while you were thinking, just look back; nothing is lost.
