# Elementary School Math, Grades 3–5

## Place value and regrouping

Our number system is **base ten**: a digit's worth depends on where it sits, which is called **place value**. In 352, the 3 means three hundreds, the 5 five tens and the 2 two ones. Each place is worth ten times the place to its right. The zero is a placeholder that holds a place open so the other digits stay put; without it, 305 and 35 would look alike.

**Regrouping** (what children call carrying and borrowing) is trading between places without changing the total. In 38 + 47 the ones add to 15, which is really one ten and five ones. You write the 5 and move a ten to the tens column. The "carried" 1 is not a one; it is a ten.

Subtraction runs the trade backward. In 52 − 27 you cannot take 7 ones from 2 ones, so you break one of the five tens into ten ones. Now 52 is 4 tens and 12 ones, and 12 − 7 = 5 and 4 − 2 = 2, giving 25. The number never changed; it was only rewritten.
## Multiplication and division

**Multiplication** counts **equal groups**: 4 × 6 is four groups of six, or 24. An **array** shows it as four rows of six dots. Turn the array on its side and you see six rows of four, which is why 4 × 6 = 6 × 4. This is the **commutative property**. It holds for adding and multiplying but not for subtracting or dividing; 8 − 3 is not 3 − 8.

**Division** undoes multiplication, so 24 ÷ 6 asks "what times 6 makes 24?" It has two meanings. In **sharing** division you know the number of groups and want the size of each: 24 cookies among 6 children is 4 each. In **grouping** division you know the group size and want the number of groups: 24 cookies bagged six to a bag fills 4 bags. The same numbers tell different stories. This link is why 4 × 6 = 24, 6 × 4 = 24, 24 ÷ 6 = 4 and 24 ÷ 4 = 6 form a **fact family**, and why knowing multiplication facts makes division easier.

When groups do not come out even, what is left is the **remainder**. 25 ÷ 6 is 4 groups with 1 left over. The remainder must be smaller than the divisor, or another full group could be made. What to do with it depends on the story: 25 students in vans that hold 6 need 5 vans, so the leftover pushes the answer up.

The **distributive property** lets you split a hard multiplication into easy pieces. For 7 × 8, think of 8 as 4 + 4: 7 × 4 is 28, doubled is 56. Or split 8 into 5 + 3: 35 + 21 = 56. These agree because 7 groups of 8 are the same objects however you sort them.
## Fractions

A **fraction** names equal parts of a whole. The **denominator** (bottom) says how many equal parts the whole is cut into, and so how big each part is. The **numerator** (top) says how many parts you have. In 3/4 the whole is cut into four equal parts and you hold three. The parts must be equal; three unequal slices are not "thirds".

A bigger denominator means smaller pieces. Cut a pizza for 8 people and each slice is smaller than when you cut it for 4, so 1/8 is less than 1/4 even though 8 is greater than 4. The tempting mistake is to compare the bottom numbers as if they were whole numbers.

**Equivalent fractions** name the same amount. Cut every half into two pieces and you have twice as many pieces, each half the size, so 1/2 = 2/4. Multiplying top and bottom by the same number is really multiplying by 2/2 or 3/3, which is one, and multiplying by one changes nothing.
To **compare** fractions, use a common denominator or a benchmark such as 1/2: 5/8 is more than half because 4/8 is half. To **add**, the pieces must be the same size, because you can only count things of one kind. 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5, since halves and thirds differ in size. Convert to sixths: 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6. The denominator names the piece size and stays the same; only the number of pieces is added.

## Decimals, fractions and money

**Decimals** continue place value to the right of the **decimal point**: tenths, then hundredths, then thousandths. So 0.5 is five tenths, which is 5/10 or 1/2, and 0.25 is 25/100 or 1/4. A decimal is a fraction whose denominator is ten, a hundred, and so on.

Money is the familiar example. A dime is a tenth of a dollar ($0.10), a penny a hundredth ($0.01), and a quarter is $0.25 because it is a fourth of a dollar.
A common misconception is that 0.15 is bigger than 0.5 because 15 is bigger than 5. But 0.5 is five tenths, the same as 0.50 or fifty hundredths, while 0.15 is only fifteen hundredths. Adding a zero on the right of a decimal does not change its value, and comparing place by place from the left settles it.

## Order of operations

Without an agreed order, one expression could mean two things. Take 2 + 3 × 4. Left to right gives 5 × 4 = 20, but multiplying first gives 2 + 12 = 14. Since everyone who reads an expression must get the same answer, mathematicians share one convention: do **parentheses** first, then **multiplication and division** left to right, then **addition and subtraction** left to right.

The order is not arbitrary. Multiplication is repeated addition, so it binds more tightly: 3 × 4 is one chunk added to 2. Parentheses override the order: (2 + 3) × 4 = 20. Ties go left to right, so 12 − 4 − 2 is 6, not 10.

## Geometry and measurement

**Perimeter** is the distance around a shape, found by adding the sides. **Area** is the flat space inside, counted in unit squares. For a rectangle, area is length times width, because the squares form an array. They measure different things, so they vary independently. A 3 by 5 rectangle has perimeter 16 and area 15; a 4 by 4 square has the same perimeter but area 16; a thin 1 by 7 rectangle again has perimeter 16 but area only 7. Fencing a garden depends on perimeter; covering it with sod depends on area.

**Units** say what is being counted. Perimeter is in plain units like feet, but area is in **square units**, such as square feet, because we count squares. Mixed units must be converted first: 2 feet 3 inches is 27 inches.

An **angle** measures the turn between two lines meeting at a point, in degrees. A full turn is 360°. A **right angle**, like a page corner, is 90°, a quarter turn, and a straight line is 180°. An **acute** angle is smaller than a right angle and an **obtuse** angle is larger than a right angle but smaller than a straight line. An angle's size depends on how far the lines open, not how long they are drawn.

## Word problems and checking

The hard part of a word problem is understanding the story, not the arithmetic. Ask what is happening: things joined or taken away (add or subtract), equal groups (multiply), or something shared or split into groups (divide). Keywords can mislead, so picture the situation. If 48 cookies go equally onto 6 plates, the equal sharing says divide, giving 8 per plate.

Then **estimate** to check. Round to friendly numbers: for 398 + 203, think 400 + 200 = 600, so an answer of 5,101 signals a misplaced digit. For 7 × 48, think 7 × 50 = 350, so the true answer should be a bit less; it is 336. Finally ask whether the answer fits the story, with sensible units and no 3.5 buses.
