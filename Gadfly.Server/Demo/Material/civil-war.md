# The American Civil War, 1861–1865

## Why the Union broke apart

The Civil War grew out of a long conflict over slavery and its expansion. By 1860 nearly four million people were
enslaved in the United States, almost all of them in the South, where cotton grown by enslaved labor was the
region's most valuable export. The North had abolished slavery state by state after the Revolution and was building
an economy of free labor, small farms, railroads and factories.

The sharpest disputes were not about slavery where it already existed but about whether it could spread into the
western territories. Each new state changed the balance of power in the Senate, so every acquisition of land reopened
the question.

- **Missouri Compromise (1820):** Missouri entered as a slave state and Maine as a free state, and slavery was barred
  from the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of latitude 36°30′.
- **Compromise of 1850:** California entered as a free state, and in exchange a much stronger Fugitive Slave Act
  required people in free states to help return those who had escaped slavery. Many Northerners who had been
  indifferent to slavery came to resent being made part of it.
- **Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854):** Senator Stephen Douglas's act let settlers decide the question by "popular
  sovereignty", undoing the Missouri line. Pro- and anti-slavery settlers rushed into Kansas and fought each other
  ("Bleeding Kansas"). The anger it caused gave rise to the Republican Party, organized to stop slavery's expansion.
- **Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857):** the Supreme Court ruled that Black Americans could not be citizens and that
  Congress had no power to ban slavery in the territories. To many Northerners this meant slavery could no longer be
  contained by law.
- **John Brown's raid (1859):** an attempt to start an uprising of enslaved people at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. It
  failed, but Southern leaders took Northern sympathy for Brown as proof that the North meant to destroy slavery.

In the **election of 1860**, Abraham Lincoln won the presidency as a Republican without carrying a single Southern
state. Lincoln promised not to interfere with slavery where it existed, but he opposed its expansion. Southern leaders
concluded that slavery's long-term future was no longer safe inside the Union.

South Carolina seceded in December 1860, followed by six more states of the Deep South by February 1861. They formed
the **Confederate States of America**, with Jefferson Davis as president. Their own declarations of secession named
the protection of slavery as their central reason. The vice president of the Confederacy, Alexander Stephens, said in
his "Cornerstone Speech" (1861) that the new government rested on the idea that slavery was the natural condition of
Black people.

The war began when Confederate forces fired on **Fort Sumter** in Charleston harbor on April 12, 1861. When Lincoln
called for volunteers to put down the rebellion, four more states (Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee and North Carolina)
seceded. Four slave states, the **border states** of Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri, stayed in the Union,
and keeping them loyal shaped Lincoln's early caution about slavery.

## Strengths and strategies

The Union had far more people (about 22 million against 9 million, of whom about 3.5 million were enslaved), most of
the nation's factories and railroads, a navy, and an established government. The Confederacy had the advantage of
fighting a defensive war on its own ground, experienced officers such as Robert E. Lee, and the hope that Britain and
France would recognize it because they needed Southern cotton.

The Union's general plan, nicknamed the **Anaconda Plan**, was to blockade Southern ports, take control of the
Mississippi River to split the Confederacy in two, and wear the South down. The Confederacy did not need to conquer
the North; it needed to survive long enough for the North to give up.

## The course of the war

- **1861–1862:** early battles such as First Bull Run (Manassas) showed the war would be long and bloody. In the West,
  Union forces under Ulysses S. Grant took Forts Henry and Donelson and won at Shiloh (April 1862), and the navy took
  New Orleans. In the East, Lee turned back Union attempts to take Richmond, the Confederate capital.
- **Antietam (September 17, 1862):** Lee's first invasion of the North ended at Antietam in Maryland, the bloodiest
  single day in American history. It was not a clear Union victory, but Lee retreated, and that gave Lincoln the
  moment he had been waiting for to announce emancipation.
- **1863, the turning point:** Lee invaded the North again and was defeated at **Gettysburg** in Pennsylvania (July
  1–3, 1863). On July 4, 1863, Grant captured **Vicksburg**, which gave the Union control of the Mississippi and split
  the Confederacy. After these two victories the Confederacy could no longer hope to win by invading the North, and
  foreign recognition became very unlikely.
- **1864–1865:** Grant, now commanding all Union armies, fought Lee in a long campaign in Virginia that ended in a
  siege of Petersburg. William T. Sherman took Atlanta (September 1864), which helped Lincoln win re-election, and then
  marched to the sea at Savannah, destroying railroads, crops and property to break the South's ability and will to
  fight. This is often called **total war**: targeting the resources that sustain an army, not only the army itself.
- **The end:** Lee surrendered to Grant at **Appomattox Court House**, Virginia, on April 9, 1865. Five days later, on
  April 14, Lincoln was shot by John Wilkes Booth, and he died the next morning.

## Emancipation

At the start, Lincoln's stated war aim was to preserve the Union, not to end slavery, partly to keep the border states
loyal. That changed for several reasons: enslaved people escaped to Union lines in large numbers, making slavery
impossible to ignore; the Confederacy's war effort depended on enslaved labor; and making the war a war against
slavery made it politically impossible for Britain, which had abolished slavery, to support the South.

The **Emancipation Proclamation**, issued after Antietam and taking effect on **January 1, 1863**, declared free all
enslaved people in areas still in rebellion. It did not apply to the border states or to Confederate areas already
under Union control, so on the day it was issued it freed few people directly. Its importance was that it made
freedom a Union war aim: every advance of the Union army now meant freedom for the people in its path. It also opened
the Union army to Black soldiers. About **180,000 Black men** served in the Union army, roughly a tenth of its
soldiers, many of them formerly enslaved.

Because the Proclamation was a war measure, it might not have survived the war in court. The **Thirteenth Amendment**,
passed by Congress in January 1865 and ratified in December 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States.

## Costs and consequences

About **620,000** soldiers died in the war by the traditional count; more recent research puts it near **750,000**.
More died of disease than in battle. Much of the South was physically ruined, and its economy, built on enslaved
labor, had to be rebuilt.

The war settled two questions: the Union could not be dissolved by a state choosing to leave, and slavery was over.
Other questions were left for **Reconstruction** (1865–1877): what freedom would mean for four million formerly
enslaved people, and on what terms the Southern states would return. The **Fourteenth Amendment** (1868) made everyone
born in the United States a citizen and promised equal protection of the laws, and the **Fifteenth Amendment** (1870)
barred denying the vote on account of race. When Reconstruction ended, white Southern governments used violence,
segregation laws and voting restrictions to take many of those rights back, which is why historians often call the
war's promise of equality unfinished.
