# World History: From the First Farmers to the Modern World

## From hunting to farming

For most of human history, people lived as **hunter-gatherers**. Small groups moved with the seasons, following animals and wild plants. They owned little, and groups stayed small because wild food in one area could feed only so many.

Around 10,000 years ago, in places such as the Fertile Crescent of the Middle East, people began to plant seeds and tame animals. This is the **agricultural (Neolithic) revolution**. It was slow, but it changed everything. Farmers had to stay near their fields, so they built permanent **villages**. A good harvest produced a **surplus**, more food than the farmers themselves needed. Surplus meant that not everyone had to farm. Some people could become potters, toolmakers, builders, priests or soldiers, which is called **division of labor**. Surplus could also be stored and traded, which made some people richer and more powerful and created rulers. As villages grew into larger places with food stores, trade, specialized workers and governments, they became the first **cities**.

## The first river civilizations

The earliest civilizations all grew up along great rivers. Rivers gave farmers water and rich soil left behind by floods, and they were highways for moving goods. But floods were unpredictable, so people had to work together on canals, dams and grain stores. Organizing that took leaders, records and laws, which pushed societies toward government.

- **Mesopotamia** ("the land between the rivers," the Tigris and Euphrates) had city-states such as Ur and Babylon. The **Sumerians** invented **writing** (cuneiform, pressed into clay) to keep track of grain, trade and debts. King **Hammurabi** of Babylon had a set of laws, the **Code of Hammurabi**, carved in stone for all to see. Its importance is that laws were written and public, so judges were supposed to follow a known standard.
- **Egypt** depended on the **Nile**, whose yearly flood left fertile soil in the middle of a desert. The river was also an easy way to travel and unite the country. Egyptians were ruled by **pharaohs**, who were seen as both kings and gods and had pyramids built as tombs. Scribes used hieroglyphic writing to run the government.
- The **Indus Valley** civilization (in what is now Pakistan and India), with cities such as Mohenjo-daro, had carefully planned streets and drainage systems. Its writing has not been understood, so much is unknown.
- In **China**, farming grew along the Yellow River. Chinese history is often told as **dynasties**, families that rule for generations. The Zhou dynasty taught the **Mandate of Heaven**: heaven gave a ruler the right to rule only as long as he ruled well. Floods, famine and rebellion were signs a dynasty had lost it.

## Classical civilizations

**Greece** was a land of mountains and islands, divided into independent **city-states** such as Athens and Sparta. In **Athens**, citizens developed an early form of **democracy**, in which they voted directly on laws. It was limited: only free adult men were citizens, and women, enslaved people and foreigners had no vote. Still, the idea that ordinary people could govern themselves became influential.

**Rome** began as a city that expelled its kings and became a **republic**, governed by elected officials and a Senate. As Rome conquered the Mediterranean, generals gained huge armies and power, civil wars followed, and after Julius Caesar the rule of an emperor, Augustus, turned the republic into an **empire**. Rome held its territory together with **roads**, a disciplined army and **law** that later European legal systems drew on. In the West, the empire fell in 476 CE. The causes built up over time: economic troubles, political instability and civil war, a huge frontier that was costly to defend, and pressure from invading peoples. The eastern half, the Byzantine Empire, lasted nearly a thousand years longer.

In **Han China** (206 BCE to 220 CE), the government used a trained bureaucracy and the ideas of Confucius to run a large empire. The Han helped open the **Silk Road**, a network of trade routes across Asia on which silk, spices and other goods traveled, along with ideas and diseases. In India, the **Maurya Empire** united much of the subcontinent; its emperor **Ashoka** turned toward Buddhism and non-violence after a bloody war. The later **Gupta Empire** is known for mathematics, including the idea of zero.

## How religions and ideas spread

Religions and philosophies travel along with people, trade and armies.

- **Judaism**, among the earliest religions to teach belief in one God, grew among the Israelites and shaped later faiths.
- **Christianity** began from the teachings of Jesus in the Roman world, spread along Roman roads, and was eventually adopted by the empire.
- **Islam** began in Arabia with the Prophet Muhammad in the 600s CE and spread quickly through conquest, trade and conversion.
- **Buddhism** began in India with the Buddha and spread along trade routes to China, Korea, Japan and Southeast Asia.
- **Confucianism** is a Chinese philosophy about proper relationships, respect for family and honest, duty-minded government.

Trade routes carried belief as well as goods.

## The Middle Ages and growing connections

After Rome fell, Western Europe was divided. In **feudalism**, a king gave land to nobles in return for loyalty and soldiers, and nobles gave land to knights. Peasants called **serfs** worked the land and were tied to it. It rested on protection and loyalty because there was no strong central government.

Meanwhile the **Islamic Golden Age** (roughly the 700s to 1200s) saw scholars in cities such as Baghdad preserve Greek knowledge, translate it, and advance mathematics (including algebra), medicine, astronomy and navigation.

In the 1200s the **Mongols**, under Genghis Khan and his successors, built the largest connected land empire in history. They were fierce conquerors, but they also kept trade routes safe, so goods and ideas moved across Eurasia more easily.

That same connection helped spread the **Black Death**, a plague that reached Europe in the 1340s, probably along trade routes. It killed a large share of Europe's people, perhaps a third or more. With so many workers dead, survivors could demand higher wages and more freedom, which weakened the feudal system.

## Exploration and its consequences

In the 1400s and 1500s, European states, seeking wealth and trade routes to Asia, sent out ships. In 1492 **Christopher Columbus** crossed the Atlantic and reached islands in the Americas, which were new to Europeans but already home to millions of people.

The **Columbian Exchange** was the movement of plants, animals, people and diseases between the Americas and the rest of the world. Potatoes, maize and tomatoes went to Europe and Africa and helped feed growing populations, while horses, cattle, wheat and sugar went to the Americas. The worst effect was **disease**. Smallpox and other illnesses from Europe were new to Native Americans, who had no immunity, and they killed a very large share of the population, greatly helping the Spanish conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires.

European colonies needed labor, especially on sugar and other plantations. The **Atlantic slave trade** forcibly carried millions of Africans across the ocean in terrible conditions to be enslaved in the Americas. It enriched traders and planters, devastated African families and communities, and left a lasting mark on the Americas.

## A bridge to the modern world

The **Renaissance**, beginning in Italy in the 1300s, was a rebirth of interest in art, learning and the ideas of ancient Greece and Rome. Around 1450 **Johannes Gutenberg** introduced the **printing press** with movable type in Europe. Books became cheaper and far more common, so ideas, including religious and scientific ones, spread faster and more people learned to read.

The **Industrial Revolution** began in Britain in the late 1700s. Machines, steam power and factories replaced much hand work. Goods became cheaper, and railroads and steamships linked places. Daily life changed: many families left farms for growing cities, work moved to factories on fixed schedules, and conditions for workers, including children, were often harsh until reforms came.
