# Stephen King's The Dark Tower

## The world, the Tower and the quest

The Dark Tower is a cycle of eight novels by Stephen King, written over more than twenty years: **The Gunslinger** (1982), **The Drawing of the Three** (1987), **The Waste Lands** (1991), **Wizard and Glass** (1997), **Wolves of the Calla** (2003), **Song of Susannah** (2004), **The Dark Tower** (2004), and later **The Wind Through the Keyhole** (2012). The last is a side story set between the fourth and fifth books. It is a tale within a tale, told by Roland during a storm.

The hero is **Roland Deschain** of Gilead, the last **gunslinger**. Gunslingers were knights of a civilization whose ideals were order, honor and duty, and they lived by a code that Roland keeps even when it makes him hard. His world is an Old West version of a place that has "moved on": time, space and meaning are fraying and the land is decaying. At the center of everything stands the **Dark Tower**, the nexus that holds all worlds together. Six **Beams** of force cross at the Tower and hold it up. Each Beam runs between two "guardians", such as Bear and Turtle. If the Beams fail, the Tower falls and all of reality goes with it.

The villain is the **Crimson King**, who wants the Tower destroyed so that he can rule the nothing that follows. He sends **Breakers**, people with special mental gifts, to a prison called Devar-Toi so that their minds can be used to cut the Beams. The series is about whether Roland can reach the Tower before that happens.

## Roland and his obsession

Roland's youth in Gilead ended in catastrophe. His father **Steven** was the lord of Gilead. Roland's mother, **Gabrielle**, was seduced by the sorcerer **Marten Broadcloak**, an agent of the rebel **John Farson**, and Roland later killed her by mistake while trying to avenge that betrayal. Farson's armies destroyed Gilead, and Roland became the last of his line. From then on the Tower became the only thing that gave his life meaning.

That is the key to the series. Roland's quest is noble in aim but corrosive in practice, because he is willing to pay any price for it. In the first book he lets the boy **Jake Chambers** fall to his death to keep following Walter, the man in black, a decision that haunts him and drives much of the later story. In **Wizard and Glass** we learn why: he once lost the one person he loved because he chose his duty and his friends over his heart.

## The ka-tet and what each member represents

King's central idea is **ka**: fate, or the way things are meant to go. A group bound together by ka is a **ka-tet**, "one made of many". Roland cannot succeed alone, and the series is largely about the family he gathers.

- **Eddie Dean** joins in *The Drawing of the Three*. A heroin addict from 1980s Brooklyn, he is drawn through the first door. His arc is **addiction and redemption**: he becomes a gunslinger by choice, finds a purpose and gives his life for the group.
- **Susannah Dean** is a woman with several selves. **Odetta Holmes** is a wealthy, educated civil-rights activist who lost her legs when pushed in front of a train. **Detta Walker** is the rage and violence that grew up in her after an earlier trauma. Roland helps them merge into **Susannah**, a whole person. Later a demon named **Mia** takes control of her body to carry the child she is pregnant with. Susannah stands for **identity**: she must integrate her divided self, and she is the most obviously changed by the quest.
- **Jake Chambers** is a boy from 1977 New York. He dies twice: once when Roland lets him fall in *The Gunslinger*, and once in New York, killed by a man named **Jack Mort**, who had also pushed Odetta. Because Roland has to save him in a paradoxical way, Jake is dragged between worlds and comes back, which reflects the guilt Roland carries. Jake is the child Roland sacrificed and the **conscience** of the ka-tet.
- **Oy** is a **billy-bumbler**, a small creature that can imitate speech. He is Jake's loyal friend and a symbol of simple loyalty.
- **Father Callahan**, a lapsed priest from King's earlier novel *'Salem's Lot*, joins in *Wolves of the Calla*. He is a man who has lost his faith and is looking for redemption, and he fights the monsters that once beat him.

## The books and their turning points

- **The Gunslinger.** Roland crosses a desert chasing Walter, who knows how to reach the Tower. He meets Jake and lets him die, then talks with the man in black.
- **The Drawing of the Three.** On a beach Roland finds three doors that open onto New York. Through them he draws Eddie, then Odetta/Detta, then Jack Mort, the killer connected to Jake's and Odetta's pasts.
- **The Waste Lands.** Roland must save Jake to keep his own mind from splitting, and Jake returns. The group meets **Blaine the Mono**, a mad, riddle-loving train who plans to kill them all in a suicidal ride. Eddie beats him with bad jokes, because Blaine cannot cope with questions that make no logical sense.
- **Wizard and Glass.** Roland tells the story of his youth in **Mejis**, where he and his friends Cuthbert and Alain are sent for safety. There Roland falls in love with **Susan Delgado**, and loses her to the townsfolk's cruelty. The tragedy explains Roland's emotional hardness.
- **Wolves of the Calla.** The ka-tet helps a farming village, **Calla Bryn Sturgis**, resist the **Wolves** that come every generation to steal half their children. Callahan joins them and the fight reminds Roland that gunslingers exist to protect people, not just chase the Tower.
- **Song of Susannah.** Mia takes control of Susannah and carries a child, **Mordred**, who is both Roland's son and the Crimson King's. The story also brings King into his own tale: he appears as a character in the group's world.
- **The Dark Tower.** The ka-tet battles to save the **rose** in a vacant New York lot, a living symbol of the Tower. They also free the Beams from the Breakers. Both Eddie and Jake die before the end and Roland deals with Mordred.

## Themes and connections

The series is about **fate against choice**. Ka looks like destiny, but the characters keep choosing, and Roland's most important choices are the ones he gets wrong. It is about **obsession**: a goal can hold a person together and also hollow them out. It also links much of King's work into one universe, with Callahan from *'Salem's Lot*, the villain Randall Flagg from *The Stand*, and other recurring figures and places. In the later books King writes **himself** into the story as the author who may be channeling Roland's world, which blurs the line between a writer and what he writes.

## The ending

Roland finally reaches the Tower, but it brings him no triumph. On the way up he sees his past, and the Tower's own voices call to him. At the top he does not find peace. Instead he is sent back to the start of the desert, with the same quest ahead of him.

This is a **loop**: Roland has lived the story many times. But this time he has the **Horn of Eld**, which he failed to bring in an earlier loop and which stands for the friends and lessons he carries with him. That change hints that a new cycle might break the pattern, which gives the ending hope.

King warned readers before the coda that they might want to stop, because the ending is not what most expect. His reason is that he wanted readers to make a choice. Readers who want a tidy ending can stop there; those who go on must accept that Roland's obsession will not let him rest.
