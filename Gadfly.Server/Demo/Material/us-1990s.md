# The United States in the 1990s

## The end of the Cold War and a new world role

The decade opened with the end of the Cold War. The Berlin Wall fell in November 1989, Germany reunified in October 1990,
and the **Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991**. The United States was left as the world's only superpower, and
President **George H. W. Bush** spoke of a "new world order" in which nations would cooperate against aggression.

The first test came when Iraq, under Saddam Hussein, invaded **Kuwait** in August 1990. Bush assembled a broad coalition of
more than thirty countries with United Nations approval. In the **Gulf War** (Operation Desert Storm, January–February
1991) an air campaign followed by a ground offensive lasting about 100 hours drove Iraq out of Kuwait. Coalition losses
were low, and Bush's approval rating briefly reached about 90 percent. The coalition chose not to march on Baghdad and
remove Saddam Hussein, a decision debated for years afterwards.

Later in the decade the US struggled to define when it should intervene abroad. In **Somalia**, a humanitarian mission
turned into fighting, and after 18 American soldiers were killed in the Battle of Mogadishu (October 1993) the US withdrew.
That experience made the US reluctant to act during the **Rwandan genocide** of 1994, in which about 800,000 people were
killed. In the former Yugoslavia, NATO air strikes in **Bosnia** led to the **Dayton Accords** (1995), and a NATO air war
over **Kosovo** in 1999 forced Serbian forces to withdraw.

## Politics: from Bush to Clinton

Bush's popularity faded with a **recession in 1990–1991** and after he broke his 1988 pledge, "Read my lips: no new
taxes", by agreeing to a 1990 budget deal that raised taxes. In the **1992 election**, Democrat **Bill Clinton**, the
governor of Arkansas, campaigned on the economy and won with 43 percent of the vote; the independent candidate **Ross
Perot** won nearly 19 percent, one of the strongest third-party showings in American history.

Clinton called himself a "New Democrat", seeking a middle path between the parties. His early plan for **universal health
care**, led by Hillary Clinton, failed in Congress in 1994. In the **1994 midterm elections** Republicans, led by Newt
Gingrich and their "**Contract with America**", won control of both the House and the Senate for the first time in
forty years. Clinton then worked with and against the Republican Congress: two **government shutdowns** in 1995–1996 over
the budget ended largely in his favor, and in 1996 he signed a **welfare reform** law that replaced the old cash welfare
program with time-limited, work-focused aid. He was re-elected in 1996.

In 1998 Clinton's affair with a White House intern, **Monica Lewinsky**, and his false testimony about it led the House of
Representatives to **impeach** him for perjury and obstruction of justice. In February 1999 the Senate **acquitted** him,
since neither charge came close to the two-thirds vote needed to remove him. He left office with high approval ratings.

## The economy and the technology boom

After the early recession, the US enjoyed the **longest economic expansion** in its history up to then, from 1991 to 2001.
Unemployment fell to about 4 percent, inflation stayed low, and in **1998 the federal budget showed its first surplus
since 1969**, helped by spending limits, the 1990 and 1993 tax increases and booming tax revenue.

Trade expanded: the **North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA)**, which took effect on January 1, 1994, removed most
trade barriers between the United States, Canada and Mexico. Supporters said it lowered prices and grew trade; opponents,
including many unions, blamed it for lost manufacturing jobs. The US also helped create the **World Trade Organization**
in 1995.

The decade's defining change was the rise of the **internet and personal computers**. The World Wide Web, invented by Tim
Berners-Lee, became public in the early 1990s; the **Mosaic** and **Netscape** browsers (1993–1994) made it easy to use, and
companies such as Amazon (1994) and Google (1998) were founded. Investors poured money into internet companies, creating
the **dot-com bubble**, which burst in 2000.

## Society, conflict and violence at home

The 1990s also saw deep divisions. In 1992, after a mostly white jury acquitted Los Angeles police officers who had been
videotaped beating a Black motorist, **Rodney King**, the city erupted in the **Los Angeles riots**, in which more than 50
people died. The 1995 murder trial of former football star **O. J. Simpson**, who was acquitted, showed how differently
many Black and white Americans viewed the justice system.

Domestic terrorism became a new fear. The **World Trade Center bombing** (1993) was carried out by Islamist extremists.
At **Ruby Ridge** (1992) and the siege of the Branch Davidian compound in **Waco**, Texas (1993), confrontations between
federal agents and armed groups ended in deaths and fed anti-government anger. On April 19, 1995, the second anniversary
of Waco, **Timothy McVeigh** bombed the federal building in **Oklahoma City**, killing 168 people, the deadliest act of
domestic terrorism in US history. In 1999 two students killed thirteen people at **Columbine High School** in Colorado,
starting a lasting national debate about guns and school safety.

Crime, which had peaked in the early 1990s, then fell sharply across the country. The **1994 crime bill** funded more
police and prisons, lengthened sentences and banned some assault weapons; it is still debated because it contributed to
mass incarceration.
