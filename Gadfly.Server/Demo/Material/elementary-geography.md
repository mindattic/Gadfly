# Elementary Geography: How the Earth Works and Where People Live

## Maps and finding places

To say where something is on a round planet, geographers use an invisible grid. **Latitude** lines run east to west and measure how far a place is north or south of the **equator**, the 0° line halfway between the poles. The poles are at 90° north and south. **Longitude** lines run from pole to pole and measure how far east or west a place is from the **prime meridian**, which passes through Greenwich, England. Unlike latitude, longitude has no natural starting line, so countries simply agreed on Greenwich in 1884. One latitude and one longitude together pick out exactly one spot.

The equator splits the planet into the **Northern and Southern Hemispheres**; the prime meridian and the 180° line on the far side split it into the **Eastern and Western Hemispheres**. Seasons are opposite in the northern and southern halves.

Only a globe shows the Earth faithfully. You cannot flatten a sphere onto paper without stretching or tearing it, just as an orange peel will not lie flat unless you cut it. Every flat map uses a **map projection**, a rule for turning the sphere into a plane, and every projection distorts something: shape, area, distance or direction. The Mercator map keeps directions right, which helped sailors, but it stretches regions far from the equator, so Greenland looks as big as Africa although Africa is about fourteen times larger. No map is wrong; each is a trade-off for a purpose.

**Scale** relates distance on the map to distance on the ground. A large-scale map shows a small area in great detail, like a city. A small-scale map shows a big area with little detail, like a continent.

## Continents and oceans

A **continent** is one of the very large land masses of the Earth. No scientific rule defines it, which is why some countries count five, six or seven. The usual seven are Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia (Oceania), Europe, North America and South America. Europe and Asia share one land mass and are separate mainly because of history and culture. About 71 percent of the surface is water, all of it connected: the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic Oceans. The Pacific is the largest and deepest.

Physical features show how the planet was shaped. The **Himalayas** are the highest mountains, the **Andes** run along South America's western edge, and the **Rockies** cross western North America. The **Nile** in Africa is traditionally called the longest river, while the **Amazon** carries the most water. The **Sahara** is the largest hot desert, but Antarctica is the largest desert of all, because a desert is defined by very little precipitation, not by heat.

## Why climate varies

**Weather** is what the air does today; **climate** is the usual pattern over many years. The main cause of climate differences is the **angle of the sun's rays**. Near the equator the sun is high overhead, so light arrives almost straight down and is concentrated on a small patch of ground, heating it strongly. Near the poles the same light arrives at a slant, spreads over a bigger area and passes through more air, so it warms much less.

**Seasons** are not caused by the Earth moving closer to or farther from the sun; the Earth is actually nearest the sun in early January, during Northern Hemisphere winter. Seasons come from the **tilt of the Earth's axis**, about 23.5°. As the Earth orbits the sun, the tilt keeps pointing the same direction in space, so for part of the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the sun, with more direct light and longer days (summer), and later leans away (winter). The Southern Hemisphere is always opposite. Near the equator the tilt changes the light little, so there are hardly any seasons.

Other factors adjust the pattern. **Altitude**: thinner air holds less heat, so temperature drops roughly six degrees Celsius per thousand meters, and mountaintops near the equator can be snowy. **Oceans**: water heats and cools slowly, so coasts are milder than continental interiors, and currents carry warmth, as the Gulf Stream does for western Europe. **Rain shadows**: when wind from the sea is forced up a mountain range, the air cools and drops its moisture as rain on the windward side. The air that descends on the far side is dry, so that side is often a desert.

## Landforms and how they form

The Earth's outer shell is broken into huge slabs called **tectonic plates**, which creep a few centimeters a year, about as fast as fingernails grow, carried by slowly flowing rock beneath. Where plates collide, rock is pushed up into mountains; the Himalayas rose as India pushed into Asia. Where one plate slides under another, rock melts and erupts, so **volcanoes** cluster along plate edges such as the Pacific "Ring of Fire". **Earthquakes** happen when plates stuck against each other suddenly slip and release stored energy, so they also concentrate at plate boundaries.

Once land rises, other forces wear it down. **Erosion** is the wearing away and carrying off of rock and soil. Moving water is the strongest agent: over millions of years the Colorado River carved the Grand Canyon. **Glaciers**, slow rivers of ice, scrape valleys into U-shapes and leave lakes behind. **Wind** shapes dunes, mostly where few plants hold the soil. Erosion also builds. A river slowing at the sea drops the sand and mud it carried, and this **sediment** builds new land called a **delta**, like the Nile or Mississippi Delta. Deltas are very fertile because the mud is rich in nutrients.

## Biomes

A **biome** is a large region with a characteristic climate, plants and animals. Temperature and rainfall decide which plants can live in a place, and plants decide which animals can.

- **Tropical rainforest:** hot, wet and near the equator, so plants grow all year and support the most variety of life on land.
- **Desert:** very little precipitation, often in a rain shadow or under the dry, sinking air near 30° latitude. Life must save water.
- **Grassland:** enough rain for grass but not for many trees, often in continental interiors, like prairies and the African savanna.
- **Temperate forest:** four seasons and moderate rain, with trees that drop their leaves for winter.
- **Taiga:** cold northern forest of evergreens that cope with snow and short summers.
- **Tundra:** so cold that the deeper ground stays frozen (**permafrost**), so roots cannot go deep and only moss and low shrubs grow.

## People and places

People settle where life is easier. Early cities grew beside rivers because rivers give drinking water, fertile soil, fish, and a way to carry goods. Coasts offer harbors for trade. Cities also grow near resources such as farmland, coal, timber or metals, and where routes meet, because trade passes through.

**Population density** is people per unit of area, such as per square kilometer. It is high where land and work are plentiful, like river valleys and coasts, and low where the environment is harsh, like deserts, polar regions and high mountains. It differs from total population: a huge country can have many people but low density.

People adapt to their surroundings, for instance with stilt houses in flood zones or terraces cut into hillsides. They also change them. **Irrigation** brings water to dry land, letting farms grow in deserts, but drawing too much can dry up rivers and leave salt in the soil. **Deforestation** clears land for farms and timber but exposes soil to erosion, destroys habitats and can change local rainfall.

## Resources, trade and interdependence

**Natural resources** are useful things from the Earth. **Renewable** ones, like sunlight, wind, water and trees, can be replaced if not overused. **Nonrenewable** ones, like coal, oil and metals, took millions of years to form. Resources are spread unevenly: oil is concentrated in some regions, fertile land in others, and crops like coffee and cocoa grow only where the climate allows.

Because no place has everything, people **trade**, selling what they have plenty of and buying what they lack. This makes places **interdependent**, so a drought, war or closed shipping route in one region can raise prices elsewhere. Geography shapes trade routes too: ships move heavy goods cheaply, which is why ports and straits matter so much.
