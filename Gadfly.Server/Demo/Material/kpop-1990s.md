# K-Pop in the 1990s, 1990–2000

## The music scene before 1992

At the start of the 1990s, South Korean popular music was dominated by two styles: **trot**, an older sentimental genre with roots in the colonial period, and smooth **ballads**. Trot was aimed at older audiences, while ballad singers such as **Shin Seung-hun** won huge followings with quiet, emotional songs. Younger listeners who wanted rock, rap or dance music mostly turned to imported records.

Success depended heavily on the **TV music shows**. Only a few broadcasters mattered, and a singer who could not get onto their programs had almost no way to reach a national audience. The shows rewarded performers who looked and sounded safe, so television acted as a gatekeeper that shaped what music could become popular.

The state was another gatekeeper. Under the military governments, songs were checked before release by a **review board**, and lyrics could be banned for being immoral, politically sensitive or harmful to public order. After **democratization in 1987**, the grip loosened and artists became bolder, but the board and its power to demand changes survived. The early 1990s were therefore a period of new freedom with the old machinery still in place, and that tension explains much of what followed.

## Seo Taiji and Boys

In **1992**, **Seo Taiji and Boys** (Seo Taiji, **Yang Hyun-suk** and **Lee Juno**) appeared on a TV talent show with "Nan Arayo". The judges gave it a low score, yet it became a sensation. Their music mixed **rap, rock and dance** with Korean lyrics, drawing on American hip hop and dance styles and on heavier rock. The group was revolutionary for several reasons:

- It showed that Korean-language songs built on imported styles could be a mainstream success, not a niche.
- Its performances and fashion made dance and visual style central to a pop act, not an extra.
- Its lyrics spoke to young people about school pressure, conformity and social problems, so it became a voice for a generation.
- Seo Taiji wrote and produced much of the material himself, which made the group an example of a creative act, not a manufactured one.

The 1995 song "Come Back Home" took a harder, rap-metal sound and carried a message aimed at teenage runaways. It was a huge hit and showed the group could be both commercial and socially engaged.

The group's fight with the censors came with the song "**Shidae Yugam**" (roughly, "regret of the times"), on a 1995 album. Its lyrics criticized the education system and society, and the review board, the **Public Performance Ethics Committee**, rejected them and demanded changes. The group refused to rewrite the words and released the song as an instrumental, with no singing. Public attention and criticism of the board grew, and in **1996** the **Constitutional Court** ruled that prior review of music was unconstitutional. This removed the requirement that lyrics be approved before release. The group's refusal was not the only cause, but it gave the issue a famous face and helped turn public opinion. The result was that Korean artists could write about almost anything without first asking permission.

Seo Taiji and Boys announced their **disbandment in January 1996**, at the height of their popularity. The shock showed how large and devoted a young fan base had become. Yang Hyun-suk later founded **YG Entertainment**.

## Other early-1990s acts and the rise of dance and hip hop

Seo Taiji opened the door for others. **Deux**, a duo that debuted around 1993, brought an upbeat dance and hip hop style. **Kim Gun-mo**, who debuted in the early 1990s, became a major solo star with a dance-influenced pop sound and a large audience in the mid-decade. Shin Seung-hun kept the ballad tradition strong, which shows that the new styles grew alongside the old ones and did not simply replace them.

The important change was in what young audiences expected: a performer who could dance, a sound that borrowed from global genres, and an image designed for a young market. Record sales grew rapidly during the early and mid 1990s, and the industry became a larger business with real money to invest.

## The idol system

The next step was to ask whether this kind of act could be built deliberately. **Lee Soo-man**, a singer turned producer, founded **SM Entertainment** and created **H.O.T.** in **1996**, followed by the girl group **S.E.S.** in **1997**. The idea was a **system** and not a single lucky act:

- **Trainees** were recruited young, often through auditions, and spent years training in singing, dancing, languages and media skills before debuting.
- The company controlled the whole process: choosing members, developing songs and choreography, styling and promotion.
- Groups were presented as a package of voices, personalities and looks, so that different fans could favor different members.

Other companies copied the model with their own variations. **DSP Media** created **Sechs Kies** (1997) and the girl group **Fin.K.L** (1998). **YG**, built by Yang Hyun-suk with a stronger focus on hip hop, produced acts such as **Jinusean** and **1TYM** at the end of the decade. **Park Jin-young** (JYP), a singer and writer, founded his own company later in the decade, and it produced the group **g.o.d**. Each company built a distinct identity, which later became part of the industry's structure.

**Fandom culture** became organized. Official **fan clubs** gave fans a community, a way to buy merchandise and a collective voice. Each group had a **color**, and fans waved plastic **balloons** in that color at concerts, with **white for H.O.T.** and **yellow for Sechs Kies**. The **H.O.T. versus Sechs Kies rivalry** was the first great fan competition: fans argued over awards, rankings and loyalty. The rivalry was good for business because it tied fans' identity to supporting their group, and it set a pattern for later rivalries.

## The 1997 financial crisis and the turn outward

In late **1997**, South Korea was hit by the **Asian financial crisis** and needed a rescue package from the International Monetary Fund. Companies failed, unemployment rose and people spent much less. In music, **album sales fell sharply**, partly because consumers had less money and partly because piracy and easy copying made things worse. The domestic market suddenly looked too small and too fragile.

The crisis pushed companies, and the government, to look at culture as an **export**. If the home market could not support the cost of training and promotion, then overseas fans were needed. The government also began to treat cultural industries as a growth area. Hardship at home thus helped push Korean entertainment toward the rest of Asia.

## The beginning of Hallyu

Korean popular culture began to be noticed in other countries in the late 1990s. Korean dramas, such as **What Is Love**, were broadcast in **China**, and Korean songs and dramas gained audiences in **Taiwan** and other places. Around **1999**, Chinese media began using the word **Hallyu** ("Korean wave") to describe this growing popularity. H.O.T. became very popular in China and held a concert in **Beijing in 2000**, a landmark for a Korean pop group performing there.

A further factor was Japan. From **1998**, South Korea began to lift its long-standing **ban on Japanese culture** in stages, which opened the way for cultural exchange in both directions. It also showed that the country was becoming confident enough to compete in an open market.

## Legacy

The 1990s created the template that later K-pop still follows. Seo Taiji and Boys proved that a Korean act could blend global genres and be a creative force, and its fight removed the legal barrier to free lyrics. The idol system turned that energy into an industry with trainees, company-built groups and dedicated fandoms. The 1997 crisis made export a necessity, and early Hallyu showed where fans could be found. These first-generation idols led to a **second generation** in the 2000s, with groups that took the same model to a wider Asian audience and then to the world, and to the later groups that followed.
