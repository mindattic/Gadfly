# Korea in the 1990s

## South Korea consolidates its democracy

South Korea entered the 1990s as a new democracy. Mass protests in June 1987 forced the military-backed government to
accept direct presidential elections, and a new constitution took effect in 1988. Roh Tae-woo, a former general and an
ally of the previous ruler Chun Doo-hwan, won the 1987 election because the opposition vote was split between Kim
Young-sam and Kim Dae-jung.

In 1990 Roh's ruling party merged with two opposition parties, including Kim Young-sam's, to form the Democratic Liberal
Party. Critics called it a betrayal of the democracy movement; supporters said it ended political deadlock. The merger
made Kim Young-sam the governing party's candidate, and he won the 1992 election.

**Kim Young-sam (1993–1998)** was the first civilian president in more than thirty years. His government:

- broke up **Hanahoe**, a secret faction of army officers that had backed the 1979 and 1980 military takeovers, so the
  army could no longer act as a political power;
- introduced the **real-name financial transaction system** (August 1993), which banned bank accounts held under false
  names, a common way of hiding bribes and political slush funds;
- held the first full **local elections** in 1995, so governors and mayors were elected rather than appointed;
- pursued "righting history": in 1995 and 1996 the former presidents **Chun Doo-hwan and Roh Tae-woo were arrested and
  tried** for the 1979 coup, for the bloody suppression of the 1980 **Gwangju Uprising**, and for collecting huge bribes.
  Chun was sentenced to death (reduced to life imprisonment) and Roh to a long prison term. Both were pardoned in
  December 1997 in the name of national reconciliation.

The decade also exposed the costs of rapid, careless growth. The **Seongsu Bridge** in Seoul collapsed in 1994, and the
**Sampoong Department Store** collapsed in 1995, killing 502 people; both disasters were traced to poor construction,
ignored warnings and corruption.

## Nordpolitik and the two Koreas

Roh Tae-woo's **Nordpolitik** ("northern policy") reached out to the communist powers that had backed North Korea. South
Korea opened diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union in 1990 and with China in 1992. In September 1991 **both Koreas
joined the United Nations** as separate members. At the end of 1991 they signed the **Inter-Korean Basic Agreement**
on reconciliation and non-aggression and a joint declaration that neither would build nuclear weapons.

## North Korea: nuclear crisis, succession and famine

The **first North Korean nuclear crisis** began in 1993 when North Korea, refusing international inspections, announced
it would withdraw from the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. The United States considered military strikes on the
Yongbyon nuclear complex. The crisis eased after former US president Jimmy Carter visited Pyongyang in June 1994, and in
October 1994 the US and North Korea signed the **Agreed Framework**: North Korea would freeze its plutonium program, and
in return it would receive two light-water power reactors (built by an international consortium largely funded by South
Korea and Japan) and shipments of heavy fuel oil.

**Kim Il-sung**, North Korea's founder, died on July 8, 1994, weeks before a planned first summit with Kim Young-sam. His
son **Kim Jong-il** took over, observing a long formal mourning period before taking the top party post in 1997.

In the mid-1990s North Korea suffered a devastating **famine**, which its government called the "Arduous March". The
collapse of the Soviet Union had ended cheap fuel, fertilizer and trade; the state-run farms were inefficient; and severe
floods in 1995 and 1996 destroyed harvests. The public food distribution system broke down. Estimates of the deaths range
widely, from several hundred thousand to more than a million. Ordinary people survived through informal markets, which
became a lasting part of North Korean life. In 1998 North Korea launched a Taepodong rocket over Japan, alarming its
neighbors.

## The 1997 financial crisis ("the IMF crisis")

South Korea joined the **OECD** in 1996, a symbol of its arrival among rich economies. But its large family-run business
groups, the **chaebol**, had expanded with heavy borrowing, much of it short-term foreign debt. In 1997 several, including
**Hanbo Steel** and **Kia**, went bankrupt. When the Asian financial crisis spread from Thailand, foreign lenders pulled
their money out, the won collapsed, and South Korea nearly ran out of foreign currency.

In December 1997 South Korea accepted a rescue package led by the **International Monetary Fund** of about 57 billion
dollars, then the largest ever. In return it had to raise interest rates, open its markets and restructure its banks and
chaebol. Companies closed, unemployment rose sharply in 1998, and many families suffered. Koreans still call it "the IMF
crisis". In a famous **gold-collecting campaign** in early 1998, millions of people donated their gold jewelry to help the
country repay its debts. The economy recovered quickly from 1999, and the restructuring broke up some chaebol: the
**Daewoo** group collapsed in 1999.

## Kim Dae-jung and the Sunshine Policy

In the December 1997 election, in the middle of the crisis, the longtime opposition leader **Kim Dae-jung** won. It was
the **first peaceful transfer of power to the opposition** in South Korea's history. Kim had been jailed, kidnapped and
sentenced to death under the military governments.

Kim's **Sunshine Policy** sought to change North Korea through engagement rather than pressure: aid, trade, tourism and
family reunions, while keeping a strong defense. In 1998 the Hyundai founder **Chung Ju-yung** drove hundreds of cattle
across the border as a gift to the North, and Hyundai began tourist trips to **Mount Kumgang** in North Korea. The policy
led to the first inter-Korean summit in June 2000. Critics argued it gave aid without getting real change in return.

## Culture opens up

The 1990s also saw the beginnings of the **Korean Wave (Hallyu)**. The group Seo Taiji and Boys (1992) mixed rap, rock
and dance and changed Korean pop music; idol groups such as H.O.T. followed from 1996. Korean television dramas became
popular in China in the late 1990s, where the term "Hallyu" came into use. In 1998 Kim Dae-jung's government began
lifting the long-standing ban on Japanese films and music, and in 1996 South Korea and Japan were chosen to co-host the
2002 World Cup.
