# Video games from 1990 to 2020

## The 16-bit era and the console war (early 1990s)

At the start of the 1990s Nintendo dominated home video games after reviving the industry in the 1980s. Sega challenged
it with the **Sega Genesis** (Mega Drive) and an aggressive, "cooler" marketing image. Nintendo answered with the **Super
Nintendo (SNES)**, released in Japan in 1990 and North America in 1991. Sega's mascot **Sonic the Hedgehog** (1991) was
built around speed to contrast with Nintendo's Mario. This rivalry, the first great **console war**, showed that a
platform's success depended on its exclusive games and on how it was marketed, not only on its hardware.

In arcades, Capcom's **Street Fighter II** (1991) started a boom in competitive fighting games. Midway's **Mortal Kombat**
(1992) drew attention for its realistic graphic violence. Together with other violent games it led to **US Senate hearings
in 1993**, and the industry, wanting to avoid government regulation, created its own rating system, the **Entertainment
Software Rating Board (ESRB)**, in 1994.

## PC gaming and the first-person shooter

On personal computers, id Software's **Wolfenstein 3D** (1992) and **Doom** (1993) created the **first-person shooter**.
Doom spread through **shareware**: the first part was given away free and players paid for the rest. It also popularized
networked multiplayer ("deathmatch") and **modding**, because players could build their own levels. **Myst** (1993) showed
what the new **CD-ROM** format could hold. Later in the decade **StarCraft** (1998) became a national phenomenon in South
Korea, where professional leagues and televised matches laid the foundations of **esports**, and **Half-Life** (1998)
brought movie-like storytelling to shooters; a fan modification of it became **Counter-Strike**.

## The move to 3D (mid-to-late 1990s)

Sony entered the market with the **PlayStation** (1994 in Japan, 1995 in the West). It used **CDs**, which were far
cheaper to make and held much more than Nintendo's cartridges, and Sony courted outside developers and older players.
When Square moved **Final Fantasy VII** (1997) from Nintendo to the PlayStation, it signaled Sony's lead. Nintendo's
**Nintendo 64** (1996) kept cartridges but launched with **Super Mario 64**, which, with the console's analog stick, set
the rules for moving a character and a camera in 3D space; **The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time** (1998) followed.
Sega's consoles struggled; its **Dreamcast** (1998–1999) had a built-in modem for online play, but Sega stopped making
consoles in 2001 and became a game publisher.

On handhelds, Nintendo's Game Boy found new life with **Pokémon** (1996 in Japan, 1998 in North America), whose
collecting and trading between players made it one of the largest media franchises in the world. On PCs, **Ultima
Online** (1997) and **EverQuest** (1999) created the **massively multiplayer online** role-playing game.

## The 2000s: online consoles, open worlds and new audiences

Sony's **PlayStation 2** (2000), which also played DVDs, became the **best-selling console of all time**, with more than
150 million sold. Microsoft entered with the **Xbox** (2001), led by the shooter **Halo**, and **Xbox Live** (2002) made
online multiplayer a normal part of console gaming. **Grand Theft Auto III** (2001) popularized the 3D **open world**, a
city players could explore freely, along with new controversy over violence.

Blizzard's **World of Warcraft** (2004) became the most popular online role-playing game, with about 12 million paying
subscribers at its peak. Valve's **Steam** (2003), which **Half-Life 2** required in 2004, grew into the main store for
**digital distribution** of PC games, letting small developers sell directly to players.

Nintendo chose not to compete on raw power. The **Nintendo DS** (2004) used a touch screen, and the **Wii** (2006) used
**motion controls**; games like Wii Sports drew in families and older people who had never played, an example of
expanding the market rather than fighting for existing players. Microsoft's **Xbox 360** (2005) and Sony's **PlayStation 3**
(2006) competed for the traditional audience with high-definition graphics.

## Phones, indies, streaming and free-to-play (2008–2015)

Apple's **App Store** (2008) turned smartphones into game machines. Games such as **Angry Birds** (2009) and **Candy
Crush Saga** (2012) reached hundreds of millions of people. Many mobile games were **free-to-play**, earning money from
**microtransactions** (small in-game purchases) and advertising rather than a purchase price. This business model spread
to PC and console games.

Digital stores also made room for **independent ("indie") games** made by small teams, such as Braid (2008). **Minecraft**
(first released in 2009, finished in 2011), made by a single developer at first, became the **best-selling video game of
all time**. Watching games became as important as playing them: **Twitch** (2011) and YouTube made streamers into
celebrities, and esports grew into a global industry led by games like **League of Legends** (2009). The **PlayStation 4**
and **Xbox One** arrived in 2013.

## Live services and battle royale (2015–2020)

Nintendo's **Switch** (2017) could be played both on a television and as a handheld. In 2017 **PUBG** and then **Fortnite
Battle Royale** made the **battle royale** genre a worldwide craze. Fortnite was free, ran on almost every device with
cross-platform play, and was run as a **live service**, constantly updated with seasons, events and a paid **battle
pass**. Games became ongoing services rather than finished products.

The decade brought new debates. **Loot boxes**, paid random rewards, were criticized as resembling gambling; the outcry
over **Star Wars Battlefront II** (2017) led some countries, such as Belgium in 2018, to restrict them. In 2011 the US
Supreme Court ruled in **Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association** that video games are protected speech, striking
down a California law restricting sales of violent games to minors. In 2019 the World Health Organization added "gaming
disorder" to its classification of diseases, a decision some researchers disputed.

In **2020** the COVID-19 pandemic kept people at home and gaming surged; **Animal Crossing: New Horizons** (March 2020)
became a way for people to meet friends when they could not see them. The year ended with the launch of the
**PlayStation 5** and **Xbox Series X/S**. By then video games earned more money worldwide than the film and music
industries combined.
